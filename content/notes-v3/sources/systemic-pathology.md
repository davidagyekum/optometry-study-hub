## How to use these notes

Systemic pathology becomes manageable when every disease is organised with the
same questions:

```text
What normal structure is involved?
→ What mechanism injures it?
→ What morphologic change follows?
→ How does that change produce symptoms and signs?
→ What complication or spread pattern matters?
```

The five primary lessons follow the current curated assessment scope:

1. breast pathology;
2. cardiovascular pathology;
3. endocrine pathology;
4. gastrointestinal pathology;
5. renal pathology.

Two historical repository sections—lymphoreticular and respiratory
pathology—remain important, but they are outside the current five-block
curated assessment. Their existing Notes V2 text and completion records should
remain visible as **legacy supplemental notes** during implementation rather
than being deleted or silently presented as newly rewritten content.

The focus labels mean:

- **Must know:** defining mechanism, morphology and presentation.
- **Should know:** useful supporting classification and comparison.
- **Useful extension:** clinical depth that should not displace the main
  pattern.

# 1. Breast Pathology

## Big picture

Breast pathology is easiest to understand from the normal **terminal
duct–lobular unit (TDLU)** and its supporting stroma.

Most lesions arise from or distort one of four components:

```text
duct or lobule epithelium
myoepithelial layer
intralobular or interlobular stroma
nipple–areolar and skin structures
```

The main diagnostic progression is:

```text
inflammatory or reactive lesion
→ benign non-proliferative change
→ proliferative lesion without atypia
→ atypical proliferation
→ carcinoma in situ
→ invasive carcinoma
```

Not every patient follows this sequence, and many benign lesions never become
cancer. The sequence is a framework for comparing risk and boundary
invasion.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- breast location, axillary tail and nipple–areolar complex;
- ducts, lobules, luminal cells, myoepithelial cells and stroma;
- TDLU as the functional unit;
- mastalgia, nipple discharge and palpable mass;
- acute bacterial mastitis;
- periductal mastitis, duct ectasia and fat necrosis;
- fibrocystic change;
- epithelial hyperplasia and atypical hyperplasia;
- intraductal papilloma;
- fibroadenoma versus phyllodes tumour;
- gynaecomastia;
- major breast-cancer risk themes;
- molecular classes based on ER, PR and HER2;
- carcinoma in situ versus invasive carcinoma;
- DCIS, LCIS and Paget disease;
- invasive carcinoma of no special type versus invasive lobular carcinoma;
- inflammatory carcinoma and peau d’orange;
- lymph-node status, tumour size and distant metastasis as prognostic factors.

### Should know

- breast boundaries and lobar organisation;
- intralobular versus interlobular stroma;
- menstrual and pregnancy-related changes;
- galactocele and lactational adenoma;
- large-duct versus small-duct papillomas;
- special invasive types such as mucinous, tubular, papillary, apocrine,
  medullary-pattern and secretory carcinoma;
- common patterns of metastatic spread.

### Useful extension

- why myoepithelial cells and basement membrane help distinguish in situ from
  invasive disease;
- why fat necrosis and duct ectasia can mimic malignancy;
- how molecular classification guides prediction and treatment;
- why a clinical “triple assessment” uses more than palpation alone.

## 1.1 Gross anatomy

The breast is a modified skin appendage on the upper anterior chest wall.

The supplied deck places it broadly:

- between the second and sixth ribs;
- from the sternum medially toward the anterior axillary line laterally;
- with an **axillary tail** extending into the axilla.

It contains:

- skin;
- subcutaneous tissue;
- ducts;
- lobules;
- fibrous and adipose stroma.

### Nipple–areolar complex

The centrally placed nipple–areolar complex contains:

- abundant sensory innervation;
- smooth muscle;
- dense fibrous tissue;
- sebaceous and apocrine glands.

Smooth-muscle contraction contributes to nipple erection.

## 1.2 Histological organisation

### Ducts and lobules

The breast is divided into lobes. Lobules drain through collecting ducts toward
lactiferous ducts opening at the nipple.

The deck gives about 20 lobes and approximately 6–10 final nipple openings.

> **Course qualification:** These numbers are anatomical approximations; the
> branching pattern varies. The durable concept is a converging ductal tree
> linked to lobular secretory units.

### Two epithelial layers

Normal ducts and acini have:

- an inner **luminal epithelial layer**;
- an outer **myoepithelial layer** adjacent to basement membrane.

Myoepithelial cells have contractile and structural roles and are an important
diagnostic boundary marker.

### Two stromal compartments

**Intralobular stroma**:

- surrounds acini and terminal ducts;
- is loose, specialised and hormonally responsive;
- contains scattered lymphocytes.

**Interlobular stroma**:

- separates lobules;
- contains more dense connective tissue and adipose tissue.

Many stromal lesions reflect one of these compartments.

## 1.3 Terminal duct–lobular unit

The **TDLU** consists of terminal ducts and grape-like acini enclosed by
intralobular stroma.

It is the functional unit of the non-lactating breast and an important origin
for:

- fibrocystic change;
- epithelial hyperplasia;
- atypical hyperplasia;
- many in situ and invasive carcinomas.

### Memory model

```text
terminal duct
+ lobular acini
+ specialised stroma
= TDLU
```

## 1.4 Hormonal influence

The breast changes through reproductive life.

### Menstrual cycle

The deck describes:

- relative quiescence during the first half of the cycle;
- post-ovulatory epithelial proliferation and increased acini;
- stromal oedema or fullness;
- regression after menses.

This helps explain cyclical lumpiness and mastalgia.

### Pregnancy and lactation

Pregnancy produces extensive lobular and acinar development. The mature breast
produces colostrum and then milk.

Lactation-related enlargement is physiological; a discrete persistent mass
still requires assessment.

## 1.5 Developmental disorders

### Supernumerary nipple or breast tissue

Remnants can persist along the embryologic milk line from axilla toward the
groin/perineal region.

Accessory axillary tissue can enlarge with pregnancy or hormonal stimulation.

### Congenital nipple inversion

Congenital inversion may be unilateral and can evert with pregnancy or
traction.

A newly acquired retracted nipple is a different clinical problem because
fibrosis or tumour can pull the nipple inward.

## 1.6 Symptoms and signs

### Pain

**Mastalgia/mastodynia** may be:

- cyclical, often linked to premenstrual change;
- non-cyclical, including trauma, inflammation or cyst-related pain.

Pain alone is not a reliable separator of benign from malignant disease.

### Nipple discharge

Discharge may be:

- milky;
- serous;
- bloody.

Interpretation depends on:

- unilateral versus bilateral;
- spontaneous versus expressed;
- single-duct versus multiple-duct;
- associated mass or skin change.

Bloody or serous single-duct discharge raises concern for a papilloma or other
ductal lesion but is not automatically malignant.

### Palpable mass

The deck notes that many masses become palpable when several centimetres in
size and that many are benign.

A mass should be described by:

- site;
- size;
- mobility;
- consistency;
- border;
- tenderness;
- relationship to skin, nipple and chest wall.

### Distribution

The deck states that many cancers occur in the upper outer quadrant and gives
course percentages for quadrants.

> **Course qualification:** These are teaching distributions, not a diagnostic
> rule. A cancer can arise in any breast region.

## 1.7 Acute bacterial mastitis

Acute bacterial mastitis is especially associated with early breastfeeding.

The source mechanism is:

```text
nipple crack or fissure
→ bacterial entry
→ acute inflammation
→ painful erythematous breast with fever
→ possible cellulitis or abscess
```

Common organisms in the deck include:

- Staphylococcus aureus;
- streptococci.

A localized fluctuant collection suggests abscess formation.

## 1.8 Granulomatous mastitis

Granulomatous inflammation can arise from:

- tuberculosis or another infection;
- systemic granulomatous disease such as sarcoidosis;
- silicone leakage or implant rupture;
- idiopathic granulomatous mastitis.

Because it can produce a mass, it may clinically mimic cancer. The diagnosis
requires correlation with histology, microbiology and clinical setting.

## 1.9 Periductal mastitis and subareolar abscess

This lesion is also called **squamous metaplasia of lactiferous ducts**.

The source sequence is:

```text
squamous metaplasia deep in major duct
→ keratin accumulates
→ duct ruptures
→ intense inflammatory and granulomatous response
→ subareolar abscess
→ fistula may track to areolar edge
```

The deck associates it strongly with smoking and tobacco-related epithelial
change.

### Common trap

**Wrong idea:** The keratin in the duct is a normal secretory product.

**Correction:** Squamous metaplasia creates keratin where the duct normally has
glandular epithelium.

## 1.10 Duct ectasia

**Duct ectasia** commonly affects older adults.

It features:

- dilated subareolar ducts;
- thick inspissated secretions;
- lipid-laden macrophages;
- periductal chronic inflammation;
- later fibrosis.

Fibrosis may produce:

- an irregular periareolar mass;
- skin or nipple retraction.

This is one reason a benign inflammatory lesion can resemble carcinoma.

## 1.11 Fat necrosis

Fat necrosis often follows trauma or surgery.

It can present as:

- painless mass;
- skin thickening or retraction;
- mammographic density;
- calcification.

### Evolution

```text
fat injury and haemorrhage
→ necrotic adipocytes
→ neutrophils and macrophages
→ lipid-laden macrophages/giant-cell reaction
→ fibrosis and calcification
```

Clinical history helps, but tissue diagnosis may be required because the lesion
can mimic malignancy.

## 1.12 Galactocele

A **galactocele** is a milk-filled cystic dilation of an obstructed duct during
lactation.

```text
duct obstruction
→ retained milk
→ thin-walled cystic mass
```

It belongs to the lactational setting and should be distinguished from acute
infection.

## 1.13 Risk grouping of benign epithelial lesions

The source groups lesions according to subsequent cancer risk.

| Group | Course examples | Relative risk concept |
|---|---|---|
| Non-proliferative | fibrocystic change, lactational adenoma | no important increase in the deck model |
| Proliferative without atypia | usual epithelial hyperplasia, intraductal papilloma | modest increase |
| Proliferative with atypia | atypical ductal or lobular hyperplasia | greater increase |

The deck gives approximate relative-risk multipliers.

> **Course qualification:** Use the qualitative hierarchy for learning. Exact
> individual risk depends on pathology, age, family history and other factors.

## 1.14 Fibrocystic change

Fibrocystic change commonly presents as a lumpy or nodular breast.

The deck identifies three principal morphologic components:

1. cyst formation, often with apocrine metaplasia;
2. fibrosis;
3. adenosis.

It is a non-proliferative pattern in the course classification and is not by
itself treated as a major premalignant lesion.

## 1.15 Lactational adenoma

A lactational adenoma occurs during pregnancy or lactation.

It is composed of otherwise normal breast tissue showing lactational
development and often regresses after breastfeeding stops.

Its timing and morphology separate it from a fibroadenoma or carcinoma, though
a persistent mass still requires proper evaluation.

## 1.16 Usual epithelial hyperplasia

Usual epithelial hyperplasia has:

- three or more epithelial layers;
- mixed luminal and myoepithelial cell populations;
- irregular slit-like spaces;
- distention of ducts or lobules.

The mixed, heterogeneous pattern helps distinguish it from a monotonous clonal
atypical proliferation.

## 1.17 Intraductal papilloma

An intraductal papilloma is a benign papillary tumour.

### Large-duct papilloma

- near the nipple/lactiferous sinus;
- often solitary;
- may cause bloody or serous discharge.

### Small-duct papillomas

- deeper in the ductal system;
- may be multiple.

Histologically, true papillae have:

- fibrovascular cores;
- benign epithelial covering;
- supporting myoepithelial cells.

## 1.18 Atypical hyperplasia

**Atypical hyperplasia** is a clonal proliferation with some but not all
features of carcinoma in situ.

### Atypical ductal hyperplasia

- relatively monomorphic regularly spaced cells;
- partial involvement/filling of ducts;
- architectural resemblance to low-grade DCIS but insufficient extent for the
  full diagnosis.

### Atypical lobular hyperplasia

- small, monomorphic, loosely cohesive cells;
- partial filling of lobular acini;
- source threshold of less than half the acini involved.

The precise boundary with in situ carcinoma is a pathologic classification
decision, not a palpable distinction.

## 1.19 Fibroadenoma

A **fibroadenoma** is the most common benign breast tumour in young women in the
source.

Typical features:

- well circumscribed;
- rubbery;
- mobile;
- painless;
- hormonally responsive;
- can enlarge during pregnancy.

Histologically it contains:

- proliferating fibrous stroma;
- compressed or distorted benign ducts.

### Clinical pattern

```text
young patient
+ discrete mobile mass
→ fibroadenoma is a strong possibility
```

This pattern guides but does not replace imaging or tissue assessment.

## 1.20 Phyllodes tumour

A **phyllodes tumour** is a fibroepithelial tumour often occurring at an older
age than fibroadenoma.

Features include:

- potentially very large size;
- less complete encapsulation;
- hypercellular stroma;
- benign epithelial ducts;
- leaf-like stromal projections into cystic spaces.

The stroma is the neoplastic driving component. Phyllodes tumours range from
benign to malignant based on pathologic features not fully detailed in the
source block.

## 1.21 Gynaecomastia

**Gynaecomastia** is enlargement of the male breast due to relative oestrogenic
effect compared with androgen effect.

It may be:

- unilateral or bilateral;
- pubertal;
- age related;
- idiopathic;
- secondary to endocrine disease, liver disease, tumour or drugs.

The source morphology is:

- increased ducts;
- epithelial hyperplasia;
- dense collagenous stroma;
- absence of true lobule formation.

A button-like subareolar enlargement is typical.

## 1.22 Breast-cancer risk themes

The deck identifies:

- increasing age;
- female sex;
- family history and first-degree relatives;
- germline BRCA1/BRCA2 and other susceptibility genes;
- personal history;
- prolonged reproductive hormone exposure;
- early menarche;
- late menopause;
- nulliparity or late first childbirth;
- high breast density;
- ionising radiation during breast development;
- alcohol, smoking and physical inactivity as modifiable associations.

Protective themes listed include:

- early full-term pregnancy;
- prolonged breastfeeding;
- risk-reducing surgery in selected very-high-risk patients;
- endocrine chemoprevention in selected settings.

> **Course qualification:** Epidemiologic frequency statements in the deck
> reflect its teaching date and population framing. The notes retain risk
> relationships without presenting old global case counts as current real-time
> statistics.

## 1.23 Molecular classification

The deck uses receptor and proliferative patterns.

| Class | Broad receptor pattern in the source | General implication |
|---|---|---|
| Luminal A | ER positive, PR positive, HER2 negative, lower proliferation | endocrine-responsive, generally more favourable |
| Luminal B | ER positive, PR lower/absent, HER2 variable in broader practice, higher proliferation | more proliferative luminal disease |
| HER2-enriched | HER2-driven, often high proliferation | may respond to HER2-targeted therapy |
| Basal-like/triple negative | ER negative, PR negative, HER2 negative | lacks those three targets and is often high grade |

> **Course qualification:** The deck gives a simplified receptor profile. Gene-
> expression classes and immunohistochemical surrogate groups are related but
> not perfectly identical.

## 1.24 Carcinoma in situ

**Carcinoma in situ** means neoplastic epithelial cells remain confined by the
basement membrane and myoepithelial boundary.

```text
malignant clonal cells
but
no stromal invasion
→ no direct access to stromal lymphatics or blood vessels
```

In situ disease can still extend through ducts or lobules and can be a precursor
or marker of invasive risk.

## 1.25 Ductal carcinoma in situ

**DCIS** is a clonal epithelial proliferation confined to ducts/lobules by the
basement membrane.

Presentations include:

- mammographic calcification;
- density;
- nipple discharge;
- incidental biopsy finding;
- occasionally a palpable lesion.

Patterns in the source:

- solid;
- comedo;
- cribriform;
- papillary.

### Comedo pattern

High-grade cells surround central necrotic material, which can calcify.

## 1.26 Paget disease of the nipple

Paget disease is a manifestation of underlying ductal carcinoma, commonly DCIS,
extending through lactiferous ducts into nipple epidermis.

Presentation:

- unilateral erythematous scaly or crusted nipple lesion;
- possible pruritus;
- can resemble eczema.

Histology shows large malignant **Paget cells** within the epidermis.

### Clinical trap

A persistent unilateral “eczema” of the nipple requires assessment for
underlying breast carcinoma.

## 1.27 Lobular carcinoma in situ

**LCIS** consists of small, uniform, discohesive cells filling lobular units.

Features:

- often incidental;
- usually no gross mass;
- often no calcification or desmoplastic response;
- loss of E-cadherin is characteristic in the deck.

LCIS is an important marker of bilateral breast-cancer risk and a non-obligate
precursor within lobular neoplasia.

## 1.28 Invasive carcinoma of no special type

This is the common invasive ductal-pattern carcinoma in the source.

Typical gross/clinical features:

- hard;
- irregular;
- radiodense or spiculated;
- may fix to chest wall;
- may retract skin or nipple.

Histology may show:

- nests;
- cords;
- glands;
- anaplastic epithelial cells;
- desmoplastic fibrous stroma;
- associated in situ foci.

The defining feature is invasion beyond the basement-membrane/myoepithelial
boundary.

## 1.29 Invasive lobular carcinoma

Invasive lobular carcinoma characteristically has:

- discohesive cells;
- single-file or “Indian-file” infiltration;
- loss of E-cadherin;
- possible signet-ring cells with intracellular mucin.

It can be difficult to detect by palpation or imaging because it may infiltrate
diffusely rather than form a firm mass.

The deck notes distinctive metastatic tendencies including:

- peritoneum and retroperitoneum;
- gastrointestinal tract;
- ovaries and uterus;
- leptomeninges.

## 1.30 Special invasive types

The source introduces several special patterns.

### Medullary pattern

- circumscribed soft/fleshy appearance;
- prominent lymphocytic infiltrate;
- source associates it with a better prognosis than ordinary high-grade NST.

### Mucinous/colloid carcinoma

- soft gelatinous tumour;
- clusters of cells in large mucin pools;
- generally favourable in the classic pure form.

### Tubular carcinoma

- well-formed tubules;
- apical snouts;
- generally favourable when pure.

### Papillary carcinoma

- true fibrovascular papillae lined by tumour.

### Apocrine carcinoma

- abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm;
- prominent nucleoli;
- resembles apocrine cells.

### Secretory carcinoma

- occurs especially in children or young patients in the deck;
- secretory material in spaces;
- usually well circumscribed with a favourable pattern.

### Metaplastic carcinoma

May include:

- squamous;
- spindle;
- osseous;
- cartilaginous;
- matrix-producing components.

## 1.31 Inflammatory carcinoma

**Inflammatory carcinoma** is a clinical presentation caused by extensive dermal
lymphatic obstruction by tumour.

Features:

- diffuse erythema;
- oedema;
- skin thickening;
- **peau d’orange**;
- often no discrete mass;
- aggressive course.

```text
tumour emboli in dermal lymphatics
→ lymph drainage blocked
→ skin oedema around tethered follicles
→ orange-peel appearance
```

It can be mistaken for mastitis, delaying diagnosis.

## 1.32 Prognostic and predictive factors

The deck emphasises:

### Axillary lymph nodes

Number and level of involved nodes strongly influence stage and prognosis.

### Distant metastasis

Distant spread indicates systemic disease; cure becomes unlikely even though
treatment can provide remission or palliation.

### Tumour size and local invasion

Larger tumours and invasion of skin or chest wall indicate more advanced local
disease.

### Lymphovascular invasion

Tumour cells in lymphatic or vascular spaces increase concern for spread.

### Histological and molecular type

Special types and ER/PR/HER2 status influence prognosis and treatment
selection.

### Response to neoadjuvant therapy

Pathologic response can provide prognostic information, especially in
HER2-positive and triple-negative disease.

## 1.33 Treatment categories in the source

The deck lists:

- surgery, including breast-conserving surgery or mastectomy;
- radiotherapy;
- chemotherapy;
- oestrogen-receptor-directed therapy;
- HER2-targeted therapy.

Treatment is individualised by stage, biology and patient factors. These notes
do not provide a clinical regimen.

## Worked example: separating three masses

### Mass A

- breastfeeding patient;
- painful red breast;
- fever.

Best initial pathologic pattern:

```text
acute bacterial mastitis
```

### Mass B

- young adult;
- mobile, rubbery, painless, circumscribed mass.

Strong benign possibility:

```text
fibroadenoma
```

### Mass C

- older adult;
- hard irregular fixed mass;
- nipple retraction.

Concerning pattern:

```text
invasive carcinoma until assessed
```

The comparison is probabilistic. Each requires appropriate clinical work-up.

## Worked example: in situ versus invasive

A duct is filled by malignant epithelial cells, but:

- basement membrane is intact;
- myoepithelial cells remain around the involved structure;
- no tumour is present in surrounding stroma.

Interpretation:

```text
carcinoma in situ
```

If neoplastic cells breach that boundary and infiltrate desmoplastic stroma:

```text
invasive carcinoma
```

## Common traps

### “Every painful breast lesion is benign.”

Inflammation is often painful, but pain does not exclude malignancy.

### “Bloody discharge proves cancer.”

Papilloma and carcinoma are both possibilities; duct pattern and evaluation
matter.

### “Fibrocystic change is a direct cancer precursor.”

It is placed in the non-proliferative, no-important-risk-increase group in the
course model.

### “Atypical hyperplasia is already invasive cancer.”

It is a clonal higher-risk proliferation confined within existing structures.

### “Myoepithelial cells are the malignant luminal cells.”

They form an outer supporting layer and help identify preserved duct/lobule
boundaries.

### “LCIS usually produces a hard palpable mass.”

It is commonly incidental and non-mass-forming.

### “Inflammatory carcinoma is an infection.”

It is carcinoma with dermal lymphatic obstruction, though its redness can mimic
mastitis.

### “All molecular classifications are defined only by ER, PR and HER2.”

Those markers are practical surrogates; gene-expression classes are related but
not perfectly identical.

## Key terms

- **Axillary tail** — extension of breast tissue into the axilla.
- **TDLU** — terminal duct–lobular functional unit.
- **Luminal cell** — inner epithelial cell lining ducts and acini.
- **Myoepithelial cell** — contractile outer epithelial cell at the
  duct/lobule boundary.
- **Intralobular stroma** — specialised loose stroma surrounding lobular units.
- **Interlobular stroma** — fibroadipose tissue between lobules.
- **Mastalgia** — breast pain.
- **Mastitis** — breast inflammation.
- **Periductal mastitis** — keratin-related inflammation after squamous
  metaplasia of major ducts.
- **Duct ectasia** — dilation of major ducts with secretions, inflammation and
  fibrosis.
- **Fat necrosis** — inflammatory and fibrotic response to damaged breast fat.
- **Galactocele** — milk-retention cyst.
- **Fibrocystic change** — non-proliferative cystic, fibrotic and adenosis
  pattern.
- **Epithelial hyperplasia** — increased epithelial layers within ducts/lobules.
- **Intraductal papilloma** — benign papillary duct tumour with fibrovascular
  cores.
- **Atypical hyperplasia** — clonal proliferation with partial in situ
  carcinoma features.
- **Fibroadenoma** — common benign fibroepithelial tumour.
- **Phyllodes tumour** — fibroepithelial tumour with leaf-like hypercellular
  stroma.
- **Gynaecomastia** — male breast enlargement from relative oestrogenic effect.
- **DCIS** — ductal carcinoma in situ.
- **LCIS** — lobular carcinoma in situ.
- **Paget disease** — nipple epidermal involvement by underlying ductal
  carcinoma cells.
- **Desmoplasia** — fibrous stromal response to invasive carcinoma.
- **Peau d’orange** — orange-peel skin from dermal lymphatic obstruction.
- **ER/PR** — oestrogen and progesterone receptors.
- **HER2** — growth-factor receptor that can be amplified/overexpressed in a
  breast-cancer subset.
- **Triple negative** — ER-, PR- and HER2-negative surrogate category.
- **Lymphovascular invasion** — tumour within lymphatic or vascular channels.

## Memory hook

Breast-lesion ladder: **I-N-P-A-I**

```text
Inflammatory
Non-proliferative
Proliferative
Atypical
In situ / Invasive
```

Boundary rule:

```text
inside basement membrane = in situ
through basement membrane = invasive
```

## One-minute summary

- The TDLU, luminal cells, myoepithelial cells and specialised stroma organise
  breast pathology.
- Symptoms include pain, discharge and mass; none alone determines benignity.
- Acute mastitis, periductal mastitis, duct ectasia and fat necrosis can produce
  inflammatory or mass-like presentations.
- Fibrocystic change is non-proliferative; usual hyperplasia and papilloma are
  proliferative without atypia; atypical hyperplasia carries greater risk.
- Fibroadenoma is a common mobile benign tumour in young patients; phyllodes is
  a stromal-driven leaf-like tumour often occurring later.
- Gynaecomastia shows ductal proliferation and fibrous stroma without lobules.
- DCIS and LCIS remain within existing basement-membrane boundaries.
- Paget disease is nipple epidermal spread from underlying ductal carcinoma.
- Invasive NST often forms a hard desmoplastic mass; invasive lobular carcinoma
  is discohesive and can infiltrate in single files.
- Inflammatory carcinoma blocks dermal lymphatics and causes peau d’orange.
- Nodal status, distant spread, size, local invasion, molecular class and
  treatment response are major prognostic or predictive factors.

14. NST often forms glands/nests in desmoplastic stroma; lobular carcinoma has
    discohesive single-file cells and E-cadherin loss.
15. Tumour emboli obstruct dermal lymphatics, causing oedema around tethered
    skin structures.
16. Nodal burden, distant metastasis, tumour size, skin/chest-wall invasion,
    lymphovascular invasion, histologic/molecular type and treatment response.

</details>

# 2. Cardiovascular Pathology

## Big picture

The cardiovascular system fails through a limited number of recurring
mechanisms:

```text
pump failure
abnormal pressure or volume load
obstructed or regurgitant flow
ischaemia
infection or immune injury
primary myocardial disease
vessel-wall injury
thrombosis, embolism or rupture
```

The useful approach is to identify the affected structure first:

```text
myocardium?
valve?
pericardium?
conduction system?
coronary artery?
systemic vessel?
```

Then connect structure to haemodynamic consequence.

### Must know

- heart chambers, wall thickness, valves and coronary territories;
- conduction system;

- left- versus right-sided failure;
- congenital-heart-disease classification;
- VSD, ASD, PDA and Tetralogy of Fallot;
- ischaemic heart disease, angina and myocardial infarction;
- transmural versus subendocardial infarction;
- MI complications;
- hypertensive heart disease and cor pulmonale;
- rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart disease;
- infective endocarditis;

- dilated, hypertrophic and restrictive cardiomyopathy;
- pericardial effusion and tamponade;
- vessel-wall layers;
- arteriosclerosis and atherosclerosis;
- atheroma pathogenesis and complications;
- selected vasculitides;
- aneurysm, deep venous thrombosis and lymphoedema.

### Should know

- dextrocardia and situs inversus;
- common right-to-left shunts and outflow obstructions;
- non-bacterial thrombotic and Libman–Sacks endocarditis;
- myocarditis;
- pericarditis patterns;
- cardiac myxoma;

### Useful extension

- how systemic vascular disease creates ocular manifestations.

## 2.1 Normal heart as a pump

The heart has four chambers:

- right atrium;
- right ventricle;
- left atrium;
- left ventricle.

The left ventricle has a much thicker wall because it pumps against systemic
vascular resistance.

The supplied deck gives approximate adult dimensions and weights.

> **Course qualification:** Heart weight and wall thickness vary with body size,
> sex, fixation and measurement technique. Learn the relative pattern:
> left-ventricular wall is much thicker than right-ventricular wall.

### Valves

- tricuspid: right atrium to right ventricle;
- pulmonary: right ventricle to pulmonary artery;
- mitral: left atrium to left ventricle;
- aortic: left ventricle to aorta.

Valves maintain forward flow by opening and closing according to pressure
gradients.

## 2.2 Conduction system

The course identifies:

- sinoatrial node;
- atrioventricular node;
- atrioventricular bundle/Bundle of His;
- bundle branches and downstream conduction pathways.

```text
SA node initiates
→ atria depolarise
→ AV node delays
→ His–Purkinje system distributes
→ ventricles contract in coordination
```

Disease can affect impulse generation, conduction or myocardial response.

## 2.3 Coronary arterial territories

### Left anterior descending artery

The source associates the LAD with:

- anterior left ventricle;
- apex;
- anterior two-thirds of interventricular septum.

### Left circumflex artery

The LCx supplies:

- left atrium;
- lateral left-ventricular regions.

### Right coronary artery

The deck associates the RCA with:

- right atrium;
- right ventricle;
- posterior septal and posterior left-ventricular regions.

The exact distribution depends on coronary dominance.

### Why territory matters

```text
artery occluded
→ downstream myocardium becomes ischaemic
→ electrical and mechanical function fail
→ complications reflect the involved region
```

## 2.4 Heart failure

The deck defines heart failure as impaired cardiac function that cannot maintain
circulation adequate for tissue metabolic needs.

Causes can be grouped.

### Intrinsic pump failure

- ischaemic heart disease;
- myocarditis;
- cardiomyopathy;
- arrhythmia.

### Increased workload

- systemic hypertension;
- pulmonary hypertension;
- valvular stenosis or regurgitation;
- severe anaemia;
- thyrotoxicosis.

### Impaired filling

- cardiac tamponade;
- constrictive pericardial disease;
- restrictive myocardial disease.

## 2.5 Compensation

The source highlights:

- cardiac hypertrophy;
- cardiac dilatation.

Additional physiological compensation includes neurohormonal activation and
fluid retention, but the deck’s morphologic emphasis is:

```text
increased load
→ myocyte hypertrophy and/or chamber dilation
→ temporary maintenance of output
→ rising oxygen demand and wall stress
→ decompensation
```

Compensation can therefore become part of the disease.

## 2.6 Left-sided heart failure

Common course causes:

- systemic hypertension;
- ischaemic heart disease;
- mitral disease.

Main consequence:

```text
left ventricle fails
→ left atrial and pulmonary venous pressure rise
→ pulmonary congestion and oedema
→ dyspnoea and orthopnoea
```

Other manifestations include:

- fatigue;
- reduced exercise tolerance;
- reduced systemic perfusion.

### Pulmonary congestion

Chronic congestion can produce:

- oedematous heavy lungs;
- capillary pressure elevation;
- red-cell leakage;
- haemosiderin-laden macrophages over time.

The deck illustrates pulmonary oedema and congestion as the defining organ
effect.

## 2.7 Right-sided heart failure

Causes in the source:

- left-sided failure;
- intrinsic lung disease;
- pulmonary hypertension.

Consequences:

```text
right ventricle fails
→ systemic venous pressure rises
→ dependent oedema
→ hepatic and splenic congestion
→ ascites
```

The congested liver may have a mottled **nutmeg** appearance.

### Biventricular failure

Combined left- and right-sided findings constitute congestive or biventricular
failure.

## 2.8 Congenital heart disease

Congenital heart disease is a structural abnormality present from birth.

The deck groups it into:

1. malposition;
2. left-to-right shunt;
3. right-to-left shunt;
4. obstruction.

Risk themes include genetic abnormalities, chromosomal syndromes and harmful
maternal exposures, although a cause is not identified in many cases.

## 2.9 Malposition

**Dextrocardia** means the cardiac apex points to the right.

It may occur with **situs inversus**, in which visceral organ arrangement is
mirrored.

The clinical importance depends on associated defects rather than the position
alone.

## 2.10 Left-to-right shunts

Blood initially moves from high-pressure systemic circulation toward lower-
pressure pulmonary circulation.

These are often acyanotic early.

### Ventricular septal defect

The source calls VSD the commonest congenital heart disease and lists:

- membranous/perimembranous;
- muscular;
- juxta-arterial/infundibular types.

```text
LV pressure > RV pressure
→ left-to-right ventricular flow
→ pulmonary overcirculation
→ possible pulmonary hypertension
→ late reversal can produce cyanosis
```

### Atrial septal defect

Types:

- ostium secundum/fossa ovalis;
- ostium primum;
- sinus venosus.

### Patent ductus arteriosus

PDA is persistent connection between aorta and pulmonary artery.

The ductus normally closes after birth. Persistence causes:

```text
aortic pressure
→ pulmonary arterial flow
→ pulmonary overcirculation
```

Prostaglandin signalling is important in maintaining fetal ductal patency.

## 2.11 Right-to-left shunts

These deliver deoxygenated blood into systemic circulation and cause cyanosis.

Examples in the deck:

- persistent truncus arteriosus;
- transposition of great arteries;
- tricuspid atresia;
- Tetralogy of Fallot;
- total anomalous pulmonary venous connection.

### Tetralogy of Fallot

The four features are:

1. pulmonary stenosis/outflow obstruction;
2. ventricular septal defect;
3. overriding aorta;
4. right-ventricular hypertrophy.

Memory hook: **PROVe**

```text
Pulmonary stenosis
Right ventricular hypertrophy
Overriding aorta
Ventricular septal defect
```

## 2.12 Obstructive congenital lesions

Examples:

- coarctation of aorta;
- pulmonary stenosis;
- aortic stenosis.

**Coarctation** is localised narrowing of the aorta, classified in the source as
preductal/infantile or postductal/adult patterns.

Obstruction creates pressure overload proximal to the narrowing and reduced
flow distally.

## 2.13 Ischaemic heart disease

IHD is cardiac dysfunction from an imbalance between myocardial oxygen supply
and demand.

The commonest cause is coronary atherosclerosis.

The deck lists the common severe coronary distribution:

```text
LAD
> RCA
> LCx
```

and uses more than roughly 75% luminal narrowing as a significant fixed
stenosis concept.

> **Course qualification:** Symptoms and acute events do not depend on one
> universal percentage. Plaque disruption and thrombosis can cause acute
> disease even when prior stenosis was less severe.

## 2.14 Angina pectoris

Angina is transient myocardial ischaemia without sustained necrosis.

### Stable angina

- predictable with exertion or excitement;
- relieved by rest in the course model;
- usually reflects fixed flow limitation.

### Prinzmetal/variant angina

- occurs at rest;
- related to coronary vasospasm.

### Unstable angina

- increasing frequency, duration or severity;
- can occur at rest;
- part of acute coronary syndrome;
- signals high infarction risk.

## 2.15 Myocardial infarction

Myocardial infarction is ischaemic necrosis of heart muscle.

### Transmural infarction

Involves much or all of the wall thickness in an arterial territory, classically
after acute coronary occlusion.

### Subendocardial infarction

Involves the inner myocardial region most vulnerable to reduced perfusion and
increased wall stress.

The deck contrasts full-thickness and laminar patterns.

## 2.16 Complications of myocardial infarction

The source lists:

- arrhythmia;
- congestive failure;
- cardiogenic shock;
- thromboembolism;
- rupture;
- aneurysm.

Mechanistic map:

```text
necrotic myocardium
→ electrical instability
→ arrhythmia

loss of contractile tissue
→ low output
→ heart failure or shock

weakened wall
→ rupture or later aneurysm

akinetic endocardial surface
→ mural thrombus
→ embolism
```

Timing and risk vary by complication, but the deck’s core concern is that death
can occur early.

## 2.17 Hypertensive heart disease

The deck defines this as concentric left-ventricular hypertrophy associated with
systemic hypertension in the absence of another sufficient explanation.

Pressure overload produces:

```text
high systemic resistance
→ increased LV wall stress
→ parallel sarcomere addition
→ concentric hypertrophy
→ impaired relaxation and higher oxygen demand
→ eventual dilation and failure
```

Grossly, the LV wall and heart weight increase.

## 2.18 Cor pulmonale

**Cor pulmonale** is right-heart disease caused by pulmonary disease or
pulmonary vascular hypertension.

```text
chronic lung/pulmonary vascular disease
→ pulmonary resistance rises
→ pulmonary hypertension
→ RV pressure overload
→ RV hypertrophy/dilation
→ right-heart failure
```

Left-sided heart disease is not the defining primary cause of cor pulmonale.

## 2.19 Rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart disease

The source describes rheumatic fever as an immune-mediated post-streptococcal
disease with molecular mimicry between streptococcal antigens and host tissues.

Cardiac manifestations include:

- pancarditis;
- Aschoff bodies;
- Anitschkow cells;
- small verrucae along valve closure lines.

The mitral valve is most commonly affected.

Chronic rheumatic heart disease causes:

- leaflet thickening;
- commissural fusion;
- chordal shortening;
- stenosis and/or regurgitation.

> **Source note:** The deck groups preceding throat, upper-respiratory and skin
> infection together. The enduring course mechanism is immune cross-reactivity
> after group A streptococcal infection; the exact antecedent-infection
> relationship should follow the lecturer’s assessed wording and current local
> clinical guidance.

Extracardiac features listed:

- migratory polyarthritis;
- subcutaneous nodules;
- erythema marginatum;
- Sydenham chorea.

## 2.20 Infective endocarditis

Infective endocarditis is microbial invasion of valves or endocardium producing
friable infected vegetations.

### Acute pattern

- virulent organism;
- may attack a previously normal valve;
- source examples include Staphylococcus aureus.

### Subacute pattern

- less virulent organism;
- often affects a previously abnormal valve;
- source examples include viridans streptococci.

Risk factors include:

- congenital or rheumatic heart disease;
- prosthetic valves;
- dental or other bacteraemia sources;
- intravenous drug use;
- immunosuppression.

### Complications

- valve destruction;
- stenosis or regurgitation;
- perforation;
- ring or myocardial abscess;
- septic emboli.

## 2.21 Non-infective vegetations

### Non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis

- sterile vegetations;
- associated with hypercoagulability and advanced malignancy;
- also called marantic/cachectic endocarditis in the deck;
- along closure lines.

### Libman–Sacks endocarditis

- associated with systemic lupus erythematosus;
- vegetations may occur on either side of a valve leaflet.

The distinction is source, sterility and distribution.

## 2.22 Valvular heart disease

### Stenosis

Failure of a valve to open completely, obstructing forward flow.

### Regurgitation/incompetence

Failure of a valve to close completely, permitting backflow.

```text
stenosis
→ pressure overload upstream

regurgitation
→ volume overload upstream and downstream
```

The mitral and aortic valves are commonly involved in acquired disease.

## 2.23 Myocarditis

Myocarditis is inflammation of myocardium.

The deck lists:

- viral;
- bacterial;
- fungal;
- parasitic;
- immune/connective-tissue;
- chemical;
- idiopathic causes.

The heart may be enlarged and flabby, with inflammatory infiltrates reflecting
the cause.

Consequences include:

- arrhythmia;
- reduced contractility;
- heart failure;
- progression to dilated cardiomyopathy in some cases.

## 2.24 Cardiomyopathies

### Dilated cardiomyopathy

- dilation of all four chambers;
- progressive systolic pump failure;
- enlarged heavy flabby heart;
- mural thrombi may occur;
- valves are not primarily diseased.

Source causes include:

- genetic cytoskeletal/sarcomeric abnormalities;
- viral myocarditis;
- alcohol and nutritional injury;
- toxins;
- peripartum disease.

### Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

- marked hypertrophy without ordinary chamber dilation;
- often asymmetric septal hypertrophy;
- impaired diastolic filling;
- possible dynamic outflow obstruction;
- sarcomeric gene mutations;
- risk of sudden death in young athletes.

### Restrictive cardiomyopathy

- stiff ventricles;
- impaired diastolic filling;
- reduced effective chamber volume;
- source examples include amyloidosis and endomyocardial fibrosis.

### Comparison

| Pattern | Dominant geometry | Main functional problem |
|---|---|---|
| Dilated | enlarged chambers | systolic contraction |
| Hypertrophic | thick myocardium, often septal | filling and possible outflow |
| Restrictive | rigid walls, relatively small chamber volume | diastolic filling |

## 2.25 Pericardial effusion and tamponade

Normal pericardial fluid is a small serous volume.

Abnormal fluid may be:

- serous;
- serosanguineous;
- blood;
- pus;
- chyle.

**Cardiac tamponade** occurs when pressure from pericardial fluid compromises
cardiac filling.

The deck contrasts:

```text
rapid smaller accumulation
→ dangerous pressure rise

slow larger accumulation
→ pericardium stretches and may accommodate more
```

The rate matters as much as the volume.

## 2.26 Pericarditis

Acute patterns in the source:

- serous;
- fibrinous/serofibrinous;
- purulent;
- haemorrhagic.

Causes include viral disease, uraemia, MI, rheumatic disease, connective-tissue
disease, bacteria, tuberculosis and malignancy.

Chronic patterns include adhesive and constrictive pericarditis.

Constrictive disease limits filling and can mimic right-sided failure.

## 2.27 Cardiac tumours

Primary cardiac tumours are rare.

### Myxoma

The commonest primary cardiac tumour in the source:

- usually left atrium;
- often near fossa ovalis;
- polypoid/pedunculated;
- may resemble an organising thrombus;
- can obstruct the mitral orifice or embolise.

Secondary tumours are more common and can spread from lung, breast, melanoma,
lymphoma or leukaemia.

## 2.28 Vessel-wall anatomy

Arteries have:

### Tunica intima

- endothelium;
- subendothelial connective tissue;
- internal elastic lamina.

### Tunica media

- smooth muscle;
- elastic fibres;
- between internal and external elastic laminae.

### Tunica adventitia

- outer connective tissue;
- nerves and supporting vessels in larger arteries.

Veins have thinner, less distinctly layered walls and many contain valves.

Capillaries consist mainly of endothelial tubes with no muscular media.

## 2.29 Arteriosclerosis

**Arteriosclerosis** means thickening and hardening of arterial walls.

Types in the source:

- senile arteriosclerosis;
- Mönckeberg medial sclerosis;
- arteriolosclerosis;
- atherosclerosis.

### Mönckeberg medial sclerosis

- calcification of media in muscular arteries;
- older adults;
- lumen usually not significantly narrowed;
- intima relatively spared.

## 2.30 Arteriolosclerosis

### Hyaline arteriolosclerosis

Associated in the deck with:

- chronic hypertension;
- diabetes;
- ageing.

Features:

- eosinophilic hyaline wall thickening;
- luminal narrowing.

### Hyperplastic arteriolosclerosis

Associated with severe/malignant hypertension:

- concentric smooth-muscle proliferation;
- “onion-skin” pattern;
- severe luminal narrowing.

### Necrotising arteriolitis

- fibrinoid necrosis;
- inflammatory injury in severe hypertension.

## 2.31 Atherosclerosis

Atherosclerosis affects the intima of large and medium arteries and forms
fibrofatty plaques.

Common sites:

- aorta;
- coronary arteries;
- cerebral arteries.

### Risk factors

Modifiable:

- dyslipidaemia;
- hypertension;
- diabetes;
- smoking.

Non-modifiable:

- age;
- sex-related risk pattern;
- inherited and familial factors.

Other source factors include obesity, inactivity, stress, homocysteine and
prothrombotic states.

## 2.32 Pathogenesis of atheroma

The deck provides a response-to-injury sequence:

```text
endothelial injury/dysfunction
→ LDL enters and is modified in intima
→ monocytes adhere and migrate
→ macrophages ingest lipid and become foam cells
→ platelets and inflammatory cells release mediators
→ smooth muscle migrates/proliferates
→ extracellular matrix forms fibrous cap
→ lipid and necrotic debris form central core
→ calcification may occur
```

This produces a plaque with:

- fibrous cap;
- cellular shoulder;
- lipid-rich necrotic core.

## 2.33 Fatty streak and atheroma

### Fatty streak

- flat or slightly raised yellow lesion;
- composed largely of foam cells;
- precursor-type lesion but not every streak becomes a dangerous plaque.

### Atheromatous plaque

- raised white-yellow lesion;
- firm fibrous cap;
- soft yellow necrotic lipid core.

## 2.34 Complications of atherosclerosis

The source lists:

- calcification;
- ulceration/rupture;
- thrombosis;
- haemorrhage into plaque;
- aneurysmal dilation.

Clinical consequences by territory:

- coronary: IHD/MI;
- cerebral: stroke;
- aorta: aneurysm;
- mesenteric: bowel ischaemia;
- lower limb: claudication and gangrene.

## 2.35 Vasculitis

Vasculitis is inflammation of blood-vessel walls.

The deck groups causes as:

- infection;
- immune disease;
- chemical/mechanical/radiation injury.

Selected patterns:

### Giant-cell/temporal arteritis

- medium/large cranial arteries;
- headache;
- can threaten vision.

### Takayasu arteritis

- aorta and major branches;
- reduced upper-limb pulses.

### Kawasaki disease

- infants and young children;
- coronary aneurysm risk.

### Polyarteritis nodosa

- small/medium muscular arteries in multiple organs.

### Buerger disease

- small/medium extremity arteries and veins;
- strong association with heavy tobacco use;
- claudication and distal ischaemia.

## 2.36 Aneurysm

An aneurysm is permanent abnormal dilation from vessel-wall weakness.

### True aneurysm

Wall includes attenuated components of the original vessel layers.

### False aneurysm

A wall defect causes an extravascular haematoma that communicates with the
lumen.

Shapes in the source include:

- saccular;
- fusiform;
- cylindrical;
- serpentine;
- racemose.

Cause-based examples:

- atherosclerotic abdominal aortic aneurysm;
- syphilitic thoracic aneurysm;
- aortic dissection;
- infected/mycotic aneurysm;
- berry aneurysm at the circle of Willis.

Complications:

- rupture and haemorrhage;
- thrombosis/embolism;
- compression of nearby structures.

## 2.37 Venous and lymphatic disease

### Varicose veins

Permanently dilated tortuous superficial veins, especially in lower limbs.

Source associations:

- pregnancy;
- prolonged standing;
- increased venous pressure;
- valve dysfunction.

### Deep venous thrombosis

The deck uses phlebothrombosis and thrombophlebitis for venous thrombosis, most
often in deep leg veins.

Complications:

- distal oedema;
- pulmonary thromboembolism;
- post-thrombotic venous disease.

### Lymphoedema

Local soft-tissue swelling from impaired lymph drainage.

Causes:

- congenital/primary;
- tumour invasion;
- surgical removal;
- filarial obstruction.

## Worked example: left versus right failure

### Patient A

- orthopnoea;
- pulmonary crepitations;
- oedematous lungs.

Localisation:

```text
left-sided failure
```

### Patient B

- ankle oedema;
- ascites;
- congested enlarged liver.

Localisation:

```text
right-sided failure
```

A patient can show both patterns when failure becomes biventricular.

## Worked example: three wall-thickening diseases

### Concentric LV hypertrophy

Mechanism: systemic pressure overload.

### Onion-skin arteriole

Mechanism: severe hypertension with hyperplastic arteriolosclerosis.

### Fibrous-cap lipid-core plaque

Mechanism: atherosclerosis of medium/large artery.

The shared word “thickening” should not obscure the different structures.

## Common traps

### “Heart failure means the heart has stopped.”

It means output or filling is inadequate for metabolic needs; the heart may
still beat.

### “Left failure causes only systemic oedema.”

Its characteristic backward effect is pulmonary congestion.

### “All congenital shunts are cyanotic from birth.”

Left-to-right shunts are often initially acyanotic and may become cyanotic only
after pulmonary vascular reversal.

### “Angina and infarction are synonyms.”

Angina is transient ischaemia; infarction includes necrosis.

### “Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is ordinary hypertensive hypertrophy.”

It is a primary sarcomeric disease with characteristic asymmetric patterns.

### “Every valve vegetation is infective.”

NBTE and Libman–Sacks vegetations are sterile.

### “Mönckeberg sclerosis narrows the lumen like atherosclerosis.”

It is medial calcification and usually does not significantly obstruct the
lumen.

### “Atherosclerosis is simply lipid stuck to a normal artery.”

It is a chronic inflammatory, cellular and matrix-remodelling intimal lesion.

### “A false aneurysm contains all vessel-wall layers.”

It is a contained wall rupture or defect.

- **Cor pulmonale** — right-heart disease secondary to pulmonary disease.
- **Aschoff body** — characteristic rheumatic inflammatory lesion.
- **Vegetation** — mass on valve/endocardium containing thrombotic,
  inflammatory or infectious material.
- **Stenosis** — failure of a valve to open fully.
- **Regurgitation** — failure of a valve to close fully.
- **Dilated cardiomyopathy** — enlarged chambers with systolic failure.
- **Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy** — primary myocardial hypertrophy with
  impaired filling and possible outflow obstruction.
- **Restrictive cardiomyopathy** — stiff ventricles with impaired filling.
- **Tamponade** — pressure from pericardial fluid compromising filling.
- **Arteriosclerosis** — arterial wall thickening/hardening.
- **Atherosclerosis** — intimal fibrofatty plaque disease.
- **Foam cell** — lipid-laden macrophage or smooth-muscle cell.

- **Vasculitis** — inflammatory vessel-wall disease.

- **Aortic dissection** — blood tracking within separated aortic wall layers.
- **Deep venous thrombosis** — thrombus in deep veins.
- **Lymphoedema** — swelling caused by impaired lymph drainage.

- Cardiovascular disease can affect pump, valve, pericardium, conduction,
  coronary flow or vessel wall.
- Left failure causes pulmonary congestion; right failure causes systemic
  venous congestion.
- Congenital disease is grouped into malposition, left-to-right shunts,
  right-to-left shunts and obstruction.
- IHD reflects oxygen supply–demand imbalance, usually from coronary
  atherosclerosis.
- Angina is transient ischaemia; infarction is necrosis.
- MI can cause arrhythmia, failure, shock, rupture, thromboembolism and
  aneurysm.
- Hypertension produces concentric LV hypertrophy and small-vessel injury.
- Rheumatic disease is immune mediated; infective endocarditis contains

- Rapid pericardial fluid can cause tamponade at a smaller volume than slow
  accumulation.
- Atherosclerosis is an intimal inflammatory fibrofatty plaque process.
- Plaque rupture, thrombosis, haemorrhage and aneurysm produce major clinical
  events.
- Vasculitis, aneurysm, DVT and lymphatic obstruction are additional vascular
  patterns.

7. Transmural involves most/full wall thickness in a vascular territory;
   subendocardial affects the vulnerable inner myocardium.
8. Arrhythmia, failure, cardiogenic shock, thromboembolism, rupture and

# 3. Endocrine Pathology

## Big picture

Endocrine disorders arise through four broad mechanisms:

```text
too much hormone
too little hormone
target-tissue resistance
mass effect from an endocrine lesion
```

The most useful tool is the **feedback axis**.

```text
hypothalamus
→ pituitary trophic hormone
→ target endocrine gland
→ target-gland hormone
→ negative feedback to hypothalamus and pituitary
```

A hormone value becomes meaningful only when interpreted with its controlling
hormone and the clinical pattern.

> **Source limitation:** A direct Endocrine Pathology slide deck is not present
> in the source files available for this authoring batch. This section is
> restricted to the topics and slide locators documented in the reviewed
> Systemic Pathology source package: pituitary disorders, thyroid and
> parathyroid disease, insulin and diabetes, adrenal cortical and medullary
> syndromes, and MEN. The notes do not invent an unsupported full endocrine
> tumour syllabus or reproduce current treatment thresholds as if they came
> from the missing deck.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- endocrine negative feedback;

- anterior versus posterior pituitary;
- prolactinoma;
- growth-hormone excess: gigantism versus acromegaly;
- ADH and oxytocin synthesis in hypothalamus with posterior-pituitary storage
  and release;
- diabetes insipidus versus SIADH;
- thyroid follicular and C-cell products;
- hyperthyroidism versus hypothyroidism;
- Graves disease mechanism;
- Hashimoto destructive autoimmunity;
- parathyroid hormone and calcium relationships;
- β-cell glucose-stimulated insulin release;
- type 1 versus type 2 diabetes;
- acute versus chronic diabetic complications;
- adrenal cortical zones and products;
- Cushing disease versus Cushing syndrome;
- primary hyperaldosteronism;
- primary adrenal insufficiency;
- pheochromocytoma;
- MEN1 “three P’s” and broad MEN2 distinction.

### Should know

- how endocrine disease produces ocular signs such as Graves orbitopathy,
  diabetic retinopathy or aniridia-associated syndromes.

## 3.1 Endocrine signalling

Hormones travel to target tissues and bind receptors.

Endocrine output depends on:

- gland structure;
- hormone synthesis and release;
- trophic stimulation;
- receptor response;
- clearance;
- feedback.

A disorder can therefore occur even when the gland itself is normal, for
example when a receptor is resistant or a pituitary signal is absent.

## 3.2 Primary, secondary and tertiary disease

### Primary endocrine disease

The abnormality begins in the target gland.

Example pattern:

```text
target-gland hormone low
→ feedback inhibition falls
→ pituitary trophic hormone rises
```

### Secondary endocrine disease

The abnormality begins in the pituitary.

```text
pituitary trophic hormone low
→ target gland is under-stimulated
→ target-gland hormone low
```

### Tertiary disease

The abnormality begins at the hypothalamic level.

The supplied source package emphasises primary-versus-secondary reasoning. The
tertiary label is useful extension rather than a major assessed block.

## 3.3 Anterior pituitary

The anterior pituitary contains endocrine cells producing trophic and effector
hormones.

The current source package focuses on:

- prolactin;
- growth hormone;
- pituitary hyperfunction and deficiency.

A pituitary adenoma may be:

- functioning, producing a hormone syndrome;
- non-functioning, presenting through mass effect;
- mixed in its clinical consequences.

## 3.4 Pituitary mass effects

An enlarging sellar lesion can cause:

- headache;
- compression of normal pituitary tissue;
- hormone deficiency;
- visual-pathway compression.

Because the optic chiasm lies above the pituitary, upward expansion can impair
crossing nasal retinal fibres and produce a bitemporal field pattern.

This structure–function link is especially important in optometry.

## 3.5 Prolactinoma

A prolactin-secreting pituitary adenoma can cause:

- galactorrhoea;
- amenorrhoea or menstrual disturbance;
- infertility;
- reduced libido;
- hypogonadal symptoms.

Mechanism:

```text
prolactin excess
→ hypothalamic–gonadal signalling suppressed
→ gonadotropin function falls
→ reproductive dysfunction
```

A similar laboratory pattern can also arise from loss of hypothalamic dopamine
inhibition or certain drugs, so elevated prolactin is not automatically an
adenoma diagnosis.

## 3.6 Growth-hormone excess

Growth hormone stimulates tissue growth partly through insulin-like growth
factors.

### Before epiphyseal closure

```text
GH excess
→ longitudinal bone growth continues
→ gigantism
```

### After epiphyseal closure

```text
GH excess
→ bones cannot lengthen normally
→ acral and soft-tissue overgrowth
→ acromegaly
```

Clinical features can include enlarged hands and feet, coarse facial change,
jaw prominence and metabolic effects.

The age-dependent difference is one of the source package’s central concepts.

## 3.7 Hypopituitarism

Loss of pituitary function can follow:

- destructive mass;
- surgery or irradiation;
- infarction;
- inflammation;
- hypothalamic or stalk disease.

The clinical pattern depends on which hormones are deficient.

A pituitary mass can therefore produce hormone excess from tumour cells and
hormone deficiency from compressed normal tissue at the same time.

## 3.8 Posterior pituitary and hypothalamus

The posterior pituitary does not synthesize ADH and oxytocin in local endocrine
cells.

Correct sequence:

```text
hypothalamic neurons synthesize ADH and oxytocin
→ axonal transport to posterior pituitary
→ storage in nerve terminals
→ release into blood
```

> **Course correction:** The reviewed source package explicitly corrects any
> wording that attributes synthesis to the posterior pituitary itself.

## 3.9 Diabetes insipidus

**Diabetes insipidus (DI)** causes inability to conserve water adequately.

### Central DI

- inadequate ADH synthesis or release.

### Nephrogenic DI

- kidney does not respond properly to ADH.

Typical pattern:

- large volumes of dilute urine;
- polydipsia;
- risk of dehydration or hypernatraemia if water access is limited.

DI is not diabetes mellitus; the shared name refers to excessive urination.

## 3.10 SIADH

**Syndrome of inappropriate ADH secretion (SIADH)** causes excessive water
retention relative to solute.

Broad pattern:

```text
ADH effect excessive
→ water reabsorbed
→ plasma becomes diluted
→ hyponatraemia develops
→ urine remains inappropriately concentrated
```

Neurologic symptoms can occur if hyponatraemia is severe or develops rapidly.

### DI versus SIADH

| Feature | Diabetes insipidus | SIADH |
|---|---|---|
| ADH effect | too low | excessive |
| Urine volume | high | lower |
| Urine concentration | dilute | inappropriately concentrated |
| Plasma sodium tendency | high | low |
| Main water problem | loss | retention |

## 3.11 Thyroid structure and hormones

Thyroid follicles contain:

- follicular cells producing thyroid hormones;
- colloid storing thyroglobulin-related material.

Parafollicular **C cells** produce calcitonin.

The source package uses the cell–hormone relationship:

```text
thyroid follicular cell → T3/T4
thyroid C cell → calcitonin
```

## 3.12 Thyroid feedback axis

```text
hypothalamic TRH
→ pituitary TSH
→ thyroid T3/T4
→ negative feedback
```

### Primary hypothyroidism

```text
thyroid hormone low
→ TSH usually rises
```

### Secondary hypothyroidism

```text
pituitary TSH inadequate
→ thyroid hormone low
→ TSH low or inappropriately normal
```

### Primary hyperthyroidism

```text
thyroid hormone high
→ TSH suppressed
```

The direction of both values helps localise the abnormal level.

## 3.13 Hyperthyroidism

The source package identifies a pattern of increased metabolic and adrenergic
activity:

- heat intolerance;
- weight loss despite appetite;
- palpitations or tachycardia;
- fine tremor;
- increased bowel frequency;
- anxiety or restlessness.

Not every patient has every feature.

## 3.14 Graves disease

Graves disease is autoimmune hyperthyroidism caused by immunoglobulins that
stimulate the TSH receptor.

```text
TSH-receptor-stimulating antibody
→ diffuse thyroid stimulation
→ excess thyroid hormone
→ pituitary TSH suppressed by feedback
```

Course features include:

- diffuse goitre;
- hyperthyroid symptoms;
- ophthalmopathy;
- pretibial dermopathy.

### Ocular connection

Graves ophthalmopathy can involve:

- proptosis;
- eyelid retraction;
- extraocular-muscle enlargement;
- exposure;
- diplopia;
- compressive optic neuropathy in severe disease.

The orbital process is not explained simply by high circulating thyroid hormone;
it reflects autoimmune orbital inflammation and tissue expansion.

## 3.15 Hashimoto thyroiditis

Hashimoto thyroiditis is dominated by autoimmune destruction of thyroid tissue.

Broad contrast:

```text
Graves
→ stimulatory autoimmunity
→ hyperfunction

Hashimoto
→ destructive autoimmunity
→ progressive hypofunction
```

Hashimoto disease can have an early transient hyperthyroid phase as stored
hormone is released, but the long-term pattern is commonly hypothyroid.

## 3.16 Hypothyroidism

Typical low-metabolic features include:

- cold intolerance;
- fatigue;
- weight gain tendency;
- constipation;
- slowed cognition or movement;
- dry skin;
- bradycardia.

Severe manifestations vary by age and duration.

The source package uses the hyper-versus-hypothyroid contrast rather than a
complete diagnostic algorithm.

## 3.17 Parathyroid hormone

Parathyroid hormone raises extracellular calcium through coordinated effects on:

- bone turnover;
- kidney calcium and phosphate handling;
- activation of vitamin D pathways and intestinal absorption.

A useful course relationship is:

```text
PTH rises
→ serum calcium tends to rise
→ serum phosphate tends to fall through renal loss
```

The actual pattern depends on renal function and cause.

## 3.18 Hyperparathyroidism

### Primary hyperparathyroidism

Autonomous parathyroid overactivity produces:

- high PTH;
- high calcium;
- low or lower phosphate tendency;
- bone, renal and neurogastrointestinal effects.

### Secondary hyperparathyroidism

Appropriate compensatory PTH increase occurs in response to chronic low-calcium
drive, commonly in chronic kidney disease.

```text
phosphate retention + reduced active vitamin D
→ calcium drive falls
→ PTH rises
```

The key distinction is autonomous versus compensatory secretion.

## 3.19 Hypoparathyroidism

Low PTH can produce hypocalcaemia and increased neuromuscular excitability.

Possible manifestations:

- tingling;
- cramps;
- tetany;
- seizures in severe disease.

Interpret calcium together with PTH rather than treating one value in isolation.

## 3.20 Pancreatic endocrine cells

The source package uses these core relationships:

- β cells → insulin;
- α cells → glucagon;
- other islet cells produce additional regulatory hormones.

Insulin is the central hormone in the diabetes block.

## 3.21 Glucose-stimulated insulin secretion

The documented source sequence is:

```text
glucose enters and is metabolised by β cell
→ intracellular ATP rises
→ ATP-sensitive K+ channels close
→ membrane depolarises
→ voltage-gated Ca2+ channels open
→ Ca2+ triggers insulin-granule exocytosis
```

This links nutrient sensing to secretion.

### Common trap

**Wrong idea:** Glucose opens the ATP-sensitive potassium channel.

**Correction:** Rising ATP closes it, reducing potassium efflux and causing
depolarisation.

## 3.22 Insulin action

Insulin promotes storage and use of nutrients.

Broad effects include:

- increased glucose uptake in insulin-responsive tissues;
- glycogen synthesis;
- lipid storage;
- protein synthesis;
- suppression of hepatic glucose output and excessive fat breakdown.

The source package focuses on pathophysiology rather than a complete receptor-
signalling cascade.

## 3.23 Type 1 diabetes mellitus

Type 1 diabetes results from loss of insulin-producing β cells, usually through
autoimmune destruction.

Consequences:

```text
absolute insulin deficiency
→ glucose uptake and storage impaired
→ hepatic glucose output rises
→ lipolysis and ketone production increase
→ hyperglycaemia and ketoacidosis risk
```

It often presents earlier in life but can occur at other ages.

## 3.24 Type 2 diabetes mellitus

Type 2 diabetes combines:

- insulin resistance;
- progressive β-cell dysfunction;
- relative insulin deficiency.

It is strongly associated with metabolic risk factors but should not be reduced
to body weight alone.

```text
target tissues respond poorly
→ β cells compensate with more insulin
→ compensation eventually fails
→ sustained hyperglycaemia
```

## 3.25 Acute diabetic complications

### Diabetic ketoacidosis

- marked insulin deficiency;
- ketone production;
- metabolic acidosis;
- dehydration;
- often more strongly associated with type 1 disease.

### Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state

- severe hyperglycaemia and dehydration;
- high osmolality;
- little or less marked ketoacidosis;
- commonly associated with type 2 disease.

### Hypoglycaemia

Can occur from treatment, missed food intake or altered activity and can impair
brain function.

> **Safety focus:** Altered consciousness, dehydration or rapid/deep breathing
> in a person with diabetes can indicate acute metabolic decompensation and
> requires urgent care.

## 3.26 Chronic diabetic complications

The source package separates:

### Microvascular

- retinopathy;
- nephropathy;
- peripheral and autonomic neuropathy.

### Macrovascular

- accelerated atherosclerotic disease;
- myocardial infarction;
- stroke;
- peripheral arterial disease.

Mechanistic summary:

```text
chronic hyperglycaemia
→ biochemical and endothelial injury
→ basement-membrane and microvascular dysfunction
→ ischaemia and leakage

plus
accelerated atherosclerosis
→ macrovascular events
```

Diabetic retinopathy and nephropathy directly connect this module to optometry
and renal pathology.

## 3.27 Adrenal cortex

The adrenal cortex has three zones.

Memory aid: **G-F-R = Salt, Sugar, Sex**

```text
zona Glomerulosa → mineralocorticoids, especially aldosterone
zona Fasciculata → glucocorticoids, especially cortisol
zona Reticularis → adrenal androgens
```

The source package also distinguishes the adrenal medulla, which produces
catecholamines.

## 3.28 Cortisol feedback

```text
hypothalamic CRH
→ pituitary ACTH
→ adrenal cortisol
→ negative feedback
```

This allows localisation.

### ACTH-dependent cortisol excess

ACTH high or inappropriately normal stimulates both adrenals.

### ACTH-independent cortisol excess

Adrenal autonomous secretion or exogenous glucocorticoid effect suppresses
endogenous ACTH.

## 3.29 Cushing syndrome and Cushing disease

**Cushing syndrome** is the clinical state of glucocorticoid excess from any
cause.

**Cushing disease** is specifically a pituitary ACTH-secreting adenoma.

Possible features:

- central weight gain;
- facial rounding;
- proximal muscle weakness;
- skin thinning and striae;
- hypertension;
- glucose intolerance;
- infection and bone risks.

### Feedback comparison

| State | Cortisol/glucocorticoid effect | ACTH tendency |
|---|---|---|
| Pituitary Cushing disease | high cortisol | high/inappropriately normal |
| Adrenal cortisol-producing lesion | high cortisol | low |
| Exogenous glucocorticoid use | high glucocorticoid effect, endogenous cortisol suppressed | low |

The exact laboratory interpretation depends on timing and testing, but the
feedback direction is the high-yield concept.

## 3.30 Primary hyperaldosteronism

Autonomous aldosterone excess produces:

- hypertension;
- sodium retention;
- potassium loss;
- suppressed renin tendency.

Hypokalaemia may occur but is not required in every patient.

```text
aldosterone excess
→ renal Na+ reabsorption increases
→ volume and blood pressure rise
→ K+ and H+ loss increase
```

## 3.31 Primary adrenal insufficiency

Primary destruction or failure of adrenal cortex causes:

- low cortisol;
- low mineralocorticoid effect in many cases;
- high ACTH from lost feedback;
- fatigue;
- weight loss;
- hypotension;
- electrolyte disturbance;
- hyperpigmentation.

Hyperpigmentation supports elevated ACTH-related peptide production.

### Secondary adrenal insufficiency

Pituitary ACTH deficiency produces low cortisol but usually no ACTH-driven
hyperpigmentation and less direct mineralocorticoid loss.

## 3.32 Pheochromocytoma

A pheochromocytoma is a catecholamine-producing tumour of adrenal-medullary
chromaffin cells.

Classic episodic pattern:

- headache;
- sweating;
- palpitations;
- hypertension.

Symptoms can be sustained rather than purely episodic.

The important localisation is medulla/catecholamine rather than adrenal cortex/
steroid hormone.

## 3.33 Multiple endocrine neoplasia

MEN syndromes are inherited patterns of multiple endocrine tumours.

### MEN1

The source package uses the **three P’s**:

```text
Parathyroid
Pancreatic endocrine
Pituitary
```

### MEN2

The source package documents major MEN patterns but the directly accessible
deck is absent. The safe broad distinction is that MEN2 centres on medullary
thyroid carcinoma and pheochromocytoma, with parathyroid involvement in one
major subtype.

Do not invent a complete genotype or surveillance schedule from this limited
source boundary.

## Worked example: localising cortisol abnormalities

### Patient A

- central obesity and purple striae;
- cortisol high;
- ACTH high.

Likely level:

```text
ACTH-dependent, with pituitary Cushing disease among key causes
```

### Patient B

- same glucocorticoid phenotype;
- taking long-term exogenous steroid;
- ACTH low;
- endogenous cortisol production suppressed.

Mechanism:

```text
exogenous feedback suppression
```

### Patient C

- weight loss, hypotension and hyperpigmentation;
- cortisol low;
- ACTH high.

Likely pattern:

```text
primary adrenal insufficiency
```

## Worked example: polyuria

### Patient A

- high urine volume;
- very dilute urine;
- rising plasma sodium.

Consider:

```text
diabetes insipidus
```

### Patient B

- hyperglycaemia;
- glucose in urine;
- osmotic diuresis.

Consider:

```text
diabetes mellitus
```

The same symptom can arise through different mechanisms.

## Common traps

### “A high target-gland hormone should always produce a high pituitary trophic
hormone.”

Negative feedback usually suppresses the pituitary signal in primary target-
gland excess.

### “The posterior pituitary synthesises ADH.”

ADH is synthesised in hypothalamic neurons and stored/released posteriorly.

### “Diabetes insipidus and diabetes mellitus are variants of one disease.”

One is a water-balance/ADH disorder; the other is a glucose/insulin disorder.

### “Graves disease is caused by destruction of thyroid follicles.”

It is caused by stimulatory TSH-receptor antibodies.

### “All hypothyroidism has high TSH.”

Central/pituitary hypothyroidism may have low or inappropriately normal TSH.

### “PTH always means high calcium regardless of context.”

PTH can be compensatorily elevated in chronic kidney disease while calcium is
low or normal.

### “Type 2 diabetes means insulin is completely absent from onset.”

It begins with insulin resistance and relative deficiency, with progressive
β-cell failure.

### “Diabetic ketoacidosis is a chronic microvascular complication.”

It is an acute metabolic complication.

### “Cushing disease and Cushing syndrome are synonyms.”

Disease is pituitary ACTH excess; syndrome includes all causes.

### “Hyperpigmentation is expected in secondary adrenal insufficiency.”

It is more characteristic of primary failure with elevated ACTH.

### “MEN1 and MEN2 contain the same three tumours.”

MEN1 uses parathyroid, pancreatic endocrine and pituitary; MEN2 has a different
pattern centred on medullary thyroid carcinoma and pheochromocytoma.

## Key terms

- **Endocrine feedback axis** — regulatory relationship among hypothalamus,
  pituitary and target gland.
- **Trophic hormone** — hormone stimulating another endocrine gland.
- **Primary endocrine disorder** — disorder originating in the target gland.
- **Secondary endocrine disorder** — disorder originating in pituitary control.
- **Pituitary adenoma** — benign neoplasm of pituitary endocrine cells.
- **Prolactinoma** — prolactin-secreting pituitary adenoma.
- **Gigantism** — GH excess before epiphyseal closure.
- **Acromegaly** — GH excess after epiphyseal closure.
- **ADH** — antidiuretic hormone regulating renal water retention.
- **Diabetes insipidus** — inadequate ADH effect causing dilute polyuria.
- **SIADH** — excessive ADH effect causing water retention and hyponatraemia.
- **Graves disease** — TSH-receptor-stimulating autoimmune hyperthyroidism.
- **Hashimoto thyroiditis** — destructive autoimmune thyroiditis.
- **Parathyroid hormone** — calcium-regulating hormone from parathyroid glands.
- **Insulin** — β-cell hormone promoting nutrient uptake and storage.
- **Insulin resistance** — reduced target-tissue response to insulin.
- **Diabetic ketoacidosis** — acute insulin-deficient ketotic metabolic
  acidosis.
- **Diabetic microangiopathy** — small-vessel injury associated with chronic
  diabetes.
- **Zona glomerulosa** — aldosterone-producing adrenal cortical zone.
- **Zona fasciculata** — cortisol-producing adrenal cortical zone.
- **Zona reticularis** — adrenal-androgen-producing cortical zone.
- **Cushing syndrome** — glucocorticoid-excess state.
- **Cushing disease** — pituitary ACTH-secreting cause of Cushing syndrome.
- **Primary hyperaldosteronism** — autonomous aldosterone excess.
- **Primary adrenal insufficiency** — adrenal cortical failure with elevated
  ACTH.
- **Pheochromocytoma** — catecholamine-producing chromaffin-cell tumour.
- **MEN1** — multiple endocrine neoplasia involving parathyroid, pancreatic
  endocrine and pituitary tumours.

# 4. Gastrointestinal Pathology

## Big picture

Gastrointestinal disease can be organised by the dominant failure:

```text
motility
mucosal injury
infection or inflammation
malabsorption
obstruction
bleeding
neoplasia
```

Symptoms become useful when linked to location.

```text
dysphagia or odynophagia
→ oesophagus

epigastric pain, early satiety or haematemesis
→ upper GI tract

diarrhoea, steatorrhoea or nutrient deficiency
→ intestinal absorption

colicky pain, distension and vomiting
→ obstruction

haematochezia or iron-deficiency anaemia
→ lower-GI bleeding pattern
```

The same symptom can have more than one cause, so use mechanism and morphology
together.

## 4.1 Oral aphthous ulcers

**Aphthous ulcers** are painful, shallow, round-to-oval oral ulcers with a
yellowish exudative base.

They can be recurrent and may be associated in the source with:

- coeliac disease;
- inflammatory bowel disease;
- systemic lupus erythematosus;
- Behçet syndrome;
- HIV infection.

An aphthous ulcer is different from a persistent indurated malignant ulcer.

## 4.2 Oral squamous-cell carcinoma

Oral SCC is the commonest oral malignancy in the deck and often affects the
tongue.

Risk associations:

- tobacco;
- alcohol;
- selected HPV-related disease.

Presentation:

- non-healing ulcer;
- irregular margin;
- raised or indurated border;
- possible leukoplakia or erythroplakia as precursor change.

A lesion that persists should not be repeatedly treated as trauma without
assessment.

## 4.3 Salivary obstruction and tumours

### Sialolithiasis

Stone formation in a major salivary duct causes:

- recurrent pain;
- swelling around meals;
- obstruction-related inflammation.

The source associates it with salivary stasis, dehydration and trauma.

### Salivary tumours

Most salivary tumours arise in parotid and many are benign, but tumours in
smaller glands have a higher malignant proportion.

Clinical clues:

- painless mass is common;
- facial paralysis or pain raises concern for malignancy.

Source examples:

- pleomorphic adenoma;
- Warthin tumour;
- mucoepidermoid carcinoma.

## 4.4 Dysphagia as a localisation clue

### Solids first, then liquids

Suggests a progressively narrowing mechanical lesion such as carcinoma.

### Solids and liquids from early in the course

Suggests a motility disorder such as achalasia.

This is a pattern, not an absolute rule.

## 4.5 Achalasia

Achalasia results from degeneration of inhibitory neurons containing nitric
oxide and VIP in the myenteric plexus.

Consequences:

```text
inhibitory neurons lost
→ lower oesophageal sphincter fails to relax
→ peristalsis is impaired
→ food accumulates
→ oesophagus dilates
```

Presentation:

- progressive dysphagia to solids and liquids;
- regurgitation;
- weight loss or aspiration;
- “bird’s-beak” taper on barium study.

Primary achalasia is idiopathic. Secondary causes include Chagas disease and
malignancy-related pseudoachalasia.

The source notes increased oesophageal-cancer risk.

## 4.6 Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease

GERD results from inappropriate reduction in lower-oesophageal-sphincter
competence.

Symptoms:

- heartburn;
- acid regurgitation;
- dysphagia;
- chronic cough or hoarseness in some cases.

Complications:

- erosive oesophagitis;
- stricture;
- Barrett oesophagus.

Asthma and reflux can aggravate one another clinically, but one does not explain
every case of the other.

## 4.7 Oesophagitis

### Reflux oesophagitis

The most common form in the source, caused by acid reflux.

### Medication-induced oesophagitis

Source examples:

- bisphosphonates;
- tetracyclines;
- NSAIDs;
- ferrous sulphate;
- potassium chloride.

Local injury is favoured when a tablet remains in contact with mucosa.

### Eosinophilic oesophagitis

- immune mediated;
- eosinophil predominant;
- associated with atopy;
- rings and longitudinal furrows may be seen;
- dysphagia and food impaction are common.

### Infectious oesophagitis

Especially in immunosuppression:

| Organism | Course endoscopic clue |
|---|---|
| Candida | white pseudomembranes |
| HSV | small punched-out ulcers |
| CMV | larger linear ulcers |

### Corrosive oesophagitis

Follows caustic ingestion and can cause acute necrosis and later stricture.

## 4.8 Plummer–Vinson syndrome

The classic triad is:

```text
dysphagia
+ iron-deficiency anaemia
+ oesophageal webs
```

The source associates it with increased oesophageal squamous-cell-carcinoma
risk and possible glossitis.

## 4.9 Mallory–Weiss tear versus Boerhaave rupture

### Mallory–Weiss syndrome

- longitudinal mucosal/submucosal laceration at gastro-oesophageal junction;
- follows severe vomiting or retching;
- causes haematemesis;
- does not traverse the full wall.

### Boerhaave syndrome

- transmural oesophageal rupture;
- often distal;
- follows violent retching;
- can produce mediastinal air and severe systemic illness.

```text
Mallory–Weiss = mucosal tear
Boerhaave = full-thickness rupture
```

## 4.10 Oesophageal varices

Varices are dilated submucosal veins, usually in the distal oesophagus, caused
by portal hypertension.

They can rupture and cause life-threatening haematemesis.

The lesion is venous collateral dilation, not a primary oesophageal epithelial
disease.

## 4.11 Distal oesophageal spasm

This disorder causes spontaneous uncoordinated contractions with normal or
near-normal LES relaxation in the course description.

Features:

- dysphagia;
- angina-like chest pain;
- “corkscrew” appearance on barium study.

It differs from achalasia, where LES relaxation fails and the oesophagus dilates
above a distal taper.

## 4.12 Scleroderma oesophagus

Smooth-muscle atrophy and fibrosis reduce distal oesophageal motility and LES
tone.

Consequences:

```text
weak peristalsis + low LES pressure
→ reflux
→ oesophagitis
→ stricture or Barrett change
→ aspiration risk
```

It can occur in CREST/systemic sclerosis.

## 4.13 Oesophageal perforation

The source says the commonest cause is iatrogenic instrumentation.

Other causes:

- spontaneous rupture;
- foreign body;
- trauma;
- malignancy.

Air can dissect into mediastinum and subcutaneous tissue, producing crepitus.

This is an emergency pattern, not routine reflux.

## 4.14 Barrett oesophagus

Barrett oesophagus is intestinal metaplasia of distal oesophageal mucosa:

```text
chronic reflux injury
→ normal non-keratinised squamous lining replaced
→ columnar intestinal-type epithelium with goblet cells
→ increased adenocarcinoma risk
```

Metaplasia is an adaptation, but it creates a substrate for dysplasia and
carcinoma.

## 4.15 Oesophageal carcinoma

Typical presentation:

- progressive dysphagia, solids then liquids;
- weight loss;
- advanced disease at diagnosis.

The source notes that the oesophagus lacks a serosal covering, facilitating
extension into nearby structures.

The deck does not provide a complete squamous-versus-adenocarcinoma comparison,
so the note does not invent one.

## 4.16 Acute gastritis

Acute erosive injury can follow:

- NSAIDs;
- alcohol;
- severe burns;
- brain injury or physiologic stress.

### NSAIDs

```text
cyclo-oxygenase inhibition
→ protective prostaglandins fall
→ mucus, bicarbonate and blood-flow support fall
→ erosion risk rises
```

### Curling ulcer

Severe burns/hypovolaemia can cause mucosal ischaemia.

### Cushing ulcer

CNS injury can increase vagal stimulation and acid production in the source
model.

## 4.17 Chronic gastritis

### H. pylori gastritis

- commonest in the source;
- often begins in antrum;
- can extend to body;
- associated with peptic ulcer disease;
- increases risk of gastric adenocarcinoma and MALT lymphoma.

### Autoimmune gastritis

- autoimmunity against parietal-cell H+/K+ ATPase and intrinsic factor;
- affects body/fundus;
- causes parietal-cell loss;
- hypochlorhydria;
- hypergastrinaemia;
- vitamin B12 deficiency/pernicious anaemia risk;
- increased neoplasia risk.

### Comparison

| Feature | H. pylori | Autoimmune |
|---|---|---|
| Initial site | antrum commonly | body/fundus |
| Main target | chronic infection-related mucosa | parietal cells/intrinsic factor |
| Acid pattern | variable | low acid |
| Important associations | PUD, MALT, carcinoma | pernicious anaemia, carcinoma/neuroendocrine change |

## 4.18 Ménétrier disease

Ménétrier disease produces:

- giant hypertrophied rugae;
- foveolar/mucus-cell hyperplasia;
- protein-losing gastropathy;
- parietal-cell atrophy and reduced acid;
- oedema from protein loss;
- precancerous association in the source.

The deck’s memory pattern is **WEAPON**:

```text
Weight loss
Epigastric pain
Anorexia
Protein loss
Oedema
Nausea/vomiting
```

## 4.19 Gastric carcinoma

The source contrasts two major adenocarcinoma patterns.

### Intestinal type

Associations:

- H. pylori;
- chronic gastritis and intestinal metaplasia;
- dietary nitrosamines/smoked foods;
- smoking;
- achlorhydria.

Often forms a glandular, ulcerated mass with raised margins.

### Diffuse type

- often linked to E-cadherin dysfunction;
- signet-ring cells;
- infiltrative wall thickening;
- **linitis plastica** or leather-bottle stomach.

### Metastatic signs in the source

- Virchow node: left supraclavicular;
- Krukenberg tumour: ovarian metastasis, often bilateral;
- Sister Mary Joseph nodule: umbilical metastasis;
- Blumer shelf: pelvic peritoneal metastatic deposit palpable rectally.

These are classic clues, not required in every patient.

## 4.20 Peptic ulcer disease

Peptic ulcers result from acid–peptic injury overcoming mucosal defence.

Major associations include:

- H. pylori;
- NSAID use.

### Complications

#### Haemorrhage

The commonest complication in the source.

- lesser-curvature gastric ulcer can involve left gastric artery;
- posterior duodenal ulcer can erode gastroduodenal artery.

#### Perforation

- anterior duodenal ulcer classically perforates into peritoneal cavity;
- free subdiaphragmatic air may occur;
- diaphragmatic irritation can refer pain to shoulder.

#### Obstruction

Scarring or oedema near pylorus/duodenum can block gastric emptying.

### Memory hook

```text
posterior duodenal = bleeding
anterior duodenal = perforation
```

## 4.21 Upper versus lower GI bleeding

### Upper GI bleeding

Origin proximal to ligament of Treitz.

Common presentations:

- haematemesis;
- melaena.

Source associations:

- PUD;
- varices.

### Lower GI bleeding

Origin distal to ligament of Treitz.

Common presentation:

- haematochezia.

Source associations include:

- diverticular disease;
- inflammatory disease;
- angiodysplasia;
- haemorrhoids;
- fissure;
- cancer.

Brisk upper bleeding can sometimes present with haematochezia, so the stool
colour rule is not absolute.

## 4.22 Malabsorption

Malabsorption can produce:

- diarrhoea;
- steatorrhoea;
- weight loss;
- weakness;
- vitamin/mineral deficiency.

The source mentions faecal-fat assessment and the D-xylose concept.

## 4.23 Coeliac disease

Coeliac disease is immune-mediated intolerance to gluten-related gliadin
proteins in wheat, barley and rye.

Associations:

- HLA-DQ2/DQ8;
- proximal small bowel;
- dermatitis herpetiformis;
- low bone density;
- increased T-cell lymphoma risk.

Histology:

```text
villous atrophy
+ crypt hyperplasia
+ intraepithelial lymphocytosis
```

Serology in the source:

- IgA tissue transglutaminase;
- anti-endomysial antibodies;
- deamidated gliadin peptide antibodies.

Treatment principle: gluten-free diet.

## 4.24 Lactose intolerance

Lactase deficiency causes:

```text
lactose remains in lumen
→ osmotic water retention
→ bacterial fermentation
→ gas, cramps and acidic diarrhoea
```

Villi may appear normal unless deficiency is secondary to mucosal injury.

The hydrogen breath test can show increased hydrogen after lactose.

## 4.25 Pancreatic insufficiency

Insufficient digestive enzymes can follow:

- chronic pancreatitis;
- cystic fibrosis;
- obstructing carcinoma.

Consequences:

- fat malabsorption;
- deficiencies of vitamins A, D, E and K;
- vitamin B12 malabsorption;
- low faecal elastase;
- low duodenal bicarbonate/pH.

The source distinguishes it from primary mucosal malabsorption by a normal
D-xylose test.

## 4.26 Tropical sprue

Tropical sprue resembles coeliac disease but occurs in residents of or visitors
to tropical regions and responds to antibiotics in the source.

It can involve duodenum and jejunum, later ileum, producing:

- folate deficiency;
- later B12 deficiency;
- megaloblastic anaemia.

The exact cause is not defined in the deck.

## 4.27 Whipple disease

Caused by Tropheryma whipplei.

Histology:

- PAS-positive foamy macrophages in small-intestinal lamina propria.

Systemic features can include:

- arthralgia;
- cardiac involvement;
- neurologic symptoms;
- later diarrhoea/steatorrhoea.

The source notes predominance in older males.

## 4.28 Microscopic colitis

Microscopic colitis causes chronic watery diarrhoea, especially in older women.

Key dissociation:

```text
colon looks normal at endoscopy
but
biopsy shows inflammation
```

Histologic patterns:

- lymphocytic colitis;
- collagenous colitis with thick subepithelial collagen band.

## 4.29 Irritable bowel syndrome

IBS causes recurrent abdominal pain associated with at least two of:

- relation to defaecation;
- altered stool frequency;
- altered stool form.

There is no defining structural lesion.

It can be:

- diarrhoea predominant;
- constipation predominant;
- mixed.

The source associates it with mood disorders and fibromyalgia.

IBS is not diagnosed merely because tests are normal; the symptom pattern and
absence of alarm features matter.

## 4.30 Appendicitis

Common causes of appendiceal luminal obstruction:

- faecalith in adults;
- lymphoid hyperplasia in children.

Mechanism:

```text
lumen obstructed
→ secretion and pressure rise
→ venous outflow compromised
→ bacterial inflammation
→ ischaemia and possible perforation
```

Pain progression:

```text
early visceral T8–T10 input
→ vague periumbilical pain

serosal/parietal peritoneal irritation
→ right-lower-quadrant pain near McBurney point
```

Associated features:

- nausea;
- fever;
- guarding/rebound;
- psoas, obturator or Rovsing signs.

## 4.31 Diverticula

A **diverticulum** is a blind pouch communicating with the gut lumen.

### True diverticulum

Includes all wall layers.

Example: Meckel diverticulum.

### False diverticulum

Only mucosa and submucosa herniate through the muscular wall.

Most acquired colonic diverticula are false and occur where vessels penetrate
the muscle.

## 4.32 Diverticulosis and diverticulitis

### Diverticulosis

- multiple colonic pseudodiverticula;
- commonly sigmoid;
- related to focal weakness and high intraluminal pressure;
- often asymptomatic;
- can cause painless haematochezia.

### Diverticulitis

- inflammation of a diverticulum;
- classically left-lower-quadrant pain;
- fever and leukocytosis;
- complications: abscess, fistula, obstruction, perforation.

A colovesical fistula can produce pneumaturia.

Bleeding is more characteristic of diverticulosis than active diverticulitis.

## 4.33 Zenker diverticulum

A pharyngoesophageal false diverticulum through Killian triangle.

Presentation:

- dysphagia;
- regurgitation;
- aspiration;
- halitosis;
- gurgling or neck mass.

It commonly occurs in older adults.

## 4.34 Meckel diverticulum

Meckel diverticulum is a true diverticulum from persistence of the vitelline
duct.

It may contain ectopic:

- gastric mucosa;
- pancreatic tissue.

Consequences:

- bleeding from acid-related ulceration;
- inflammation;
- intussusception;
- volvulus;
- obstruction.

### Rule of twos

The source uses:

- about 2% of population;
- 2 inches long;
- 2 feet from ileocaecal valve;
- two times more common in males;
- often presents in first two years;
- may contain two ectopic tissue types.

Treat these as a mnemonic approximation.

## 4.35 Hirschsprung disease

Hirschsprung disease results from failure of neural crest cells to populate
distal bowel.

Absent ganglion cells in:

- Auerbach plexus;
- Meissner plexus.

Mechanism:

```text
distal aganglionic segment cannot relax
→ functional obstruction
→ proximal colon dilates
→ transition zone forms
```

Presentation:

- failure to pass meconium within 48 hours;
- abdominal distension;
- bilious vomiting;
- chronic constipation;
- explosive stool after rectal examination.

Associated with RET mutations and Down syndrome.

Diagnosis requires rectal biopsy showing absent ganglion cells.

## 4.36 Malrotation and volvulus

### Malrotation

Abnormal fetal midgut rotation causes:

- abnormal bowel position;
- Ladd bands;
- predisposition to volvulus and obstruction.

### Volvulus

Bowel twists around its mesentery:

```text
twist
→ luminal obstruction
→ venous congestion
→ arterial compromise
→ infarction/perforation
```

Source patterns:

- midgut volvulus in infants/children;
- sigmoid volvulus in older adults with coffee-bean appearance;
- gastric volvulus with severe pain, dry heaving and inability to pass a
  nasogastric tube.

## 4.37 Intussusception

A proximal bowel segment telescopes into a distal segment, often ileocaecal in
children.

Possible lead points:

- lymphoid hyperplasia;
- Meckel diverticulum;
- bowel-wall haematoma;
- tumour in adults.

Consequences:

- intermittent pain;
- vomiting;
- vascular compromise;
- currant-jelly stool;
- sausage-shaped mass;
- target sign on ultrasound/CT.

## 4.38 Necrotising enterocolitis

NEC occurs especially in premature, formula-fed infants with immature
intestinal defence.

It can produce:

- mucosal necrosis;
- terminal ileal/proximal colonic injury;
- pneumatosis intestinalis;
- portal venous gas;
- perforation and pneumoperitoneum.

It is a neonatal emergency pattern.

## 4.39 Small-bowel obstruction

Common causes in the source:

- postoperative adhesions;
- hernia;
- tumour.

Pathophysiology:

```text
lumen blocked
→ gas and fluid accumulate proximally
→ bowel dilates
→ vomiting and dehydration
→ venous/arterial compromise if strangulated
→ necrosis or perforation
```

Features:

- abrupt colicky pain;
- nausea/vomiting;
- distension;
- air–fluid levels.

Management principles named in the source include decompression, fluid
resuscitation and bowel rest, with surgery depending on cause and complication.

## 4.40 Benign and non-neoplastic polyps

### Hamartomatous polyps

Disorganised overgrowth of normal local tissues. A solitary lesion is not
automatically highly malignant, but syndromic polyposis carries important
cancer risk.

### Hyperplastic polyps

Common, often small and rectosigmoid. Some serrated-pathway lesions are
biologically different from ordinary small distal hyperplastic polyps.

### Inflammatory pseudopolyps

Regenerating islands after mucosal ulceration, especially in inflammatory bowel
disease.

### Mucosal/submucosal polyps

Can include clinically insignificant mucosal elevations and submucosal lipoma,
leiomyoma or fibroma.

## 4.41 Adenomatous and serrated polyps

### Adenomatous polyps

Neoplastic dysplastic epithelium.

Architecture:

- tubular;
- tubulovillous;
- villous.

Cancer risk rises with:

- greater villous architecture;
- larger size;
- high-grade dysplasia;
- multiple lesions.

### Serrated pathway lesions

Features in the source:

- saw-toothed crypt pattern;
- CpG-island methylation;
- possible MLH1 silencing;
- microsatellite instability;
- BRAF mutation.

Not every hyperplastic-looking polyp has the same risk.

## 4.42 Polyposis syndromes

### Familial adenomatous polyposis

- autosomal dominant APC mutation;
- hundreds to thousands of adenomas;
- near-certain colorectal carcinoma without preventive management;
- rectum involved.

### Gardner syndrome

FAP plus:

- osteomas;
- soft-tissue tumours;
- impacted/supernumerary teeth;
- congenital hypertrophy of retinal pigment epithelium.

The retinal association is especially relevant to optometry.

### Turcot syndrome

Polyposis or Lynch-related syndrome with a malignant CNS tumour.

### Peutz–Jeghers syndrome

- hamartomatous GI polyps;
- mucocutaneous pigmentation around lips/mouth and elsewhere;
- increased GI and extraintestinal cancer risk.

### Juvenile polyposis

- multiple hamartomatous polyps in childhood;
- increased colorectal-cancer risk.

### MUTYH-associated polyposis

- autosomal recessive DNA-repair disorder;
- multiple adenomatous/serrated polyps;
- increased colorectal and selected other cancer risks.

## 4.43 Lynch syndrome

Lynch syndrome is hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer.

Mechanism:

```text
germline mismatch-repair defect
→ second-hit loss
→ microsatellite instability
→ rapid accumulation of mutations
→ colorectal and extracolonic cancer risk
```

Source associations:

- proximal colorectal cancer;
- endometrial;
- ovarian;
- selected skin tumours.

Unlike FAP, the colon is not carpeted by thousands of adenomas.

## 4.44 Colorectal carcinoma

Risk themes:

- age;
- adenomatous/serrated polyps;
- inherited syndromes;
- inflammatory bowel disease;
- tobacco;
- processed-meat/low-fibre dietary pattern.

### Right-sided cancer

Often:

- exophytic;
- occult bleeding;
- iron-deficiency anaemia;
- weight loss;
- less early obstruction because lumen is wider.

### Left-sided cancer

Often:

- annular/infiltrating;
- narrower lumen;
- altered stool calibre;
- colicky pain;
- obstruction;
- haematochezia.

### Other clues

- Streptococcus gallolyticus bacteraemia/endocarditis;
- apple-core lesion on contrast study;
- CEA for monitoring, not screening.

## 4.45 Molecular pathways of colorectal carcinoma

### Chromosomal-instability pathway

```text
APC loss
→ adenoma
→ additional mutations such as KRAS
→ progressive dysplasia
→ carcinoma
```

This is the classic adenoma–carcinoma sequence.

### Microsatellite-instability pathway

```text
mismatch-repair loss or silencing
→ microsatellite instability
→ serrated or Lynch-associated progression
→ carcinoma
```

The pathways explain why different precursor polyps and syndromes require
different risk interpretation.

### Haematemesis and melaena

Think upper GI:

- peptic ulcer;
- varix;
- erosive disease.

### Painless haematochezia in an older adult

- diverticulosis;

Consider occult gastrointestinal loss, including right-sided colorectal

## Common traps

### “Dysphagia to solids and liquids always means obstruction.”

Early involvement of both is typical of motility disease.

### “Mallory–Weiss and Boerhaave are the same depth of tear.”

One is mucosal/submucosal; the other is transmural.

### “Barrett oesophagus is a cancer.”

It is intestinal metaplasia with increased adenocarcinoma risk.

### “H. pylori and autoimmune gastritis affect the same site and mechanism.”

H. pylori often begins antrally; autoimmune disease targets body/fundus
parietal cells.

### “Ménétrier disease causes high acid because the folds are large.”

It causes mucus-cell hyperplasia, protein loss and reduced parietal-cell
function.

### “Every diverticulum contains all bowel-wall layers.”

Most acquired colonic diverticula are false.

### “Diverticulitis is the commonest cause of painless diverticular bleeding.”

Bleeding is more characteristic of diverticulosis; diverticulitis causes
inflammation and pain.

### “IBS is an inflammatory structural disease.”

It has no defining structural lesion.

### “A normal colonoscopy excludes microscopic colitis.”

Biopsy can reveal disease despite normal gross mucosa.

### “All colorectal polyps have the same malignant potential.”

Risk depends on histology, size, dysplasia and syndrome.

### “CEA is a screening test.”

It is mainly used to follow known disease.

- **Adenoma** — dysplastic neoplastic glandular polyp.
- **Serrated polyp** — polyp with saw-tooth architecture and possible
  methylation/MSI pathway.
- **FAP** — APC-related diffuse adenomatous polyposis.
- **Lynch syndrome** — mismatch-repair-related hereditary cancer syndrome.
- **Microsatellite instability** — length variation from mismatch-repair
  failure.
- **CEA** — tumour marker used mainly in follow-up, not screening.

## Memory hook

Dysphagia:

```text
Solids then liquids = narrowing
Solids and liquids early = motility
```

Ulcer anatomy:

```text
Posterior duodenum bleeds
Anterior duodenum perforates
```

Meckel: **rule of twos**.

CRC sides:

```text
Right = bleed quietly
Left = block visibly
```

## One-minute summary

- GI pathology is organised by motility, mucosal injury, inflammation,
  malabsorption, obstruction, bleeding and neoplasia.
- Achalasia causes failed LES relaxation and dysphagia to solids and liquids.
- GERD can cause erosive oesophagitis, stricture and Barrett metaplasia.
- Mallory–Weiss is a mucosal tear; Boerhaave is transmural rupture.
- Varices are portal-hypertension-related veins that can bleed massively.
- H. pylori and autoimmune gastritis differ in site and mechanism.
- Ménétrier disease causes giant folds, protein loss and reduced acid.
- Gastric intestinal and diffuse carcinomas have different precursor
  relationships and morphology.
- Peptic ulcers can bleed, perforate or obstruct.
- Coeliac disease causes villous atrophy, crypt hyperplasia and intraepithelial
  lymphocytes.
- Appendicitis pain migrates from periumbilical to right lower quadrant as
  inflammation reaches parietal peritoneum.
- Most acquired colonic diverticula are false; Meckel is true.
- Hirschsprung disease is distal aganglionosis with proximal dilation.
- Intussusception telescopes; volvulus twists.
- Adenomatous and selected serrated polyps are premalignant.
- FAP is APC related; Lynch is mismatch-repair related.
- Right colon cancer often causes occult bleeding; left cancer more often
  obstructs.
- Screening specifics must follow current local guidance.

# 5. Renal Pathology

## Big picture

Renal disease is easiest to localise by nephron compartment:

```text
glomerulus
→ filtration and blood/protein leakage patterns

tubules and interstitium
→ concentration, reabsorption, toxin and infection patterns

renal vessels
→ perfusion and hypertension patterns

pelvis and urinary outflow
→ obstruction, infection and stone patterns

renal parenchyma
→ cystic and neoplastic patterns
```

The urine is a sample of renal function. Haematuria, proteinuria, casts,
bacteria, crystals and urine volume provide clues to the structure involved.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- kidney gross structure and blood flow;
- nephron components;
- glomerular capillary wall;
- fenestrated endothelium, GBM and podocyte slit diaphragm;
- juxtaglomerular apparatus;
- renal excretory, regulatory and endocrine functions;
- azotaemia versus uraemia;
- nephritic versus nephrotic syndrome;
- AKI versus CKD;
- congenital anomalies;
- immune-complex and in-situ antibody injury;
- podocyte injury and proteinuria;
- acute tubular injury;
- acute and chronic pyelonephritis;
- hypertensive nephrosclerosis;
- ADPKD versus ARPKD;
- obstruction and hydronephrosis;
- calcium, struvite, uric-acid and cystine stones;
- renal-cell carcinoma;
- Wilms tumour and associated syndromes.

### Should know

- cortex, medulla, pyramids, calyces and renal pelvis;
- renal arterial sequence;
- GBM layers and matrix components;
- size and charge selectivity as the course filtration model;
- nephritic and nephrotic examples;
- routes of kidney infection;
- extrarenal ADPKD manifestations;
- unilateral versus bilateral obstruction;
- benign papillary adenoma and angiomyolipoma.

### Useful extension

- why podocyte loss can produce progressive sclerosis;
- how reduced GFR produces sodium retention and hypertension;
- why a urease-positive infection creates struvite stones;
- why renal tumours can remain silent until large;
- how renal and ocular microvascular disease intersect in hypertension and
  diabetes.

## 5.1 Gross anatomy

The kidneys are paired retroperitoneal organs.

The source gives an average adult weight of about 150 g per kidney.

Each kidney contains:

- outer cortex;
- inner medulla;
- approximately 12 medullary pyramids;
- papillae draining into calyces;
- renal pelvis;
- ureter.

A medullary pyramid with its overlying cortex forms a renal lobe.

> **Course qualification:** Weight and pyramid number vary. Learn the
> cortex–medulla–papilla–calyx–pelvis sequence.

## 5.2 Urine drainage pathway

```text
collecting ducts
→ papilla
→ minor/major calyces
→ renal pelvis
→ ureter
→ bladder
→ urethra
```

Obstruction at any level can increase upstream pressure and damage renal
parenchyma.

## 5.3 Renal blood supply

The kidney receives a large fraction of cardiac output in order to filter and
regulate plasma.

Arterial sequence in the source:

```text
renal artery
→ anterior and posterior divisions
→ segmental arteries
→ interlobar arteries
→ arcuate arteries
→ interlobular arteries
→ afferent arterioles
→ glomerular capillaries
→ efferent arterioles
```

The efferent arteriole then feeds peritubular capillaries or vasa recta,
depending on nephron location.

### Functional significance

The glomerulus lies between two arterioles:

```text
afferent arteriole
→ high-pressure capillary tuft
→ efferent arteriole
```

This allows filtration pressure to be regulated at both ends.

## 5.4 The nephron

The nephron is the functional unit of the kidney.

Components:

- glomerulus/Bowman capsule;
- proximal convoluted tubule;
- loop of Henle;
- distal convoluted tubule;
- collecting system.

The source gives about one million nephrons per kidney as an approximation.

Each segment has different transport and injury patterns.

## 5.5 Glomerular structure

The glomerulus is an anastomosing tuft of capillaries supported by mesangial
cells.

### Endothelial cells

- fenestrated;
- permit water and small solutes;
- retain blood cells.

### Glomerular basement membrane

The deck lists:

- central lamina densa;
- lamina rara interna;
- lamina rara externa.

Components include:

- type IV collagen;
- laminin;
- entactin;
- glycosaminoglycan-rich matrix.

### Podocytes

Visceral epithelial cells with interdigitating foot processes.

Adjacent processes form filtration slits bridged by slit diaphragms.

### Mesangial cells

Provide:

- structural support;
- matrix production;
- contractile and phagocytic functions;
- response to inflammatory signals.

## 5.6 The filtration barrier

The three-part course model is:

```text
fenestrated endothelium
+ GBM
+ podocyte slit diaphragm
→ selective ultrafiltration
```

### Size selectivity

Large plasma proteins and cells are retained.

### Charge selectivity

The deck emphasises negative GBM charge and relative exclusion of negatively
charged albumin.

> **Course qualification:** Modern filtration models distribute selectivity
> across endothelium, glycocalyx, GBM and podocyte slit structures. Do not treat
> one layer or charge alone as the entire barrier. The durable lesson is that
> barrier disruption permits protein and/or blood to enter urine.

### Podocytes

The source calls podocytes a major barrier to protein loss.

Loss or effacement of foot processes and slit-diaphragm dysfunction are central
to many proteinuric diseases.

## 5.7 Bowman space

Bowman/urinary space lies between:

- visceral podocytes;
- parietal epithelial cells of Bowman capsule.

Filtrate enters this space before passing into proximal tubule.

In crescentic glomerulonephritis, proliferating cells and inflammatory material
can fill this space.

## 5.8 Juxtaglomerular apparatus

The JGA lies near the vascular pole and contains:

### Macula densa

Specialised distal-tubular/thick-ascending-limb cells sensing tubular salt
delivery.

### Juxtaglomerular cells

Modified afferent-arteriolar smooth-muscle cells that secrete renin.

### Extraglomerular mesangial cells

Cells between macula densa and hilar arterioles.

Functional relationship:

```text
reduced perfusion or altered distal salt delivery
→ renin release
→ renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system
→ vascular and sodium-retention responses
```

## 5.9 Functions of the kidney

The source lists:

### Excretion

- metabolic waste;
- drugs and toxins.

### Homeostasis

- water;
- sodium and other electrolytes;
- calcium and phosphate;
- acid–base balance.

### Endocrine roles

- renin;
- erythropoietin;
- prostaglandins;
- vitamin D activation/regulation.

The kidney is therefore an endocrine and metabolic organ as well as a filter.

## 5.10 Glomerular filtration rate

The deck gives approximately 120 mL/min or 170 L/day as a normal GFR scale and
1–2 L/day urine output.

> **Course qualification:** GFR varies with age, sex, body size, physiologic
> state and measurement method. The filtration volume is far greater than final
> urine volume because most filtered water and solute are reabsorbed.

GFR depends on:

- glomerular capillary pressure;
- opposing hydrostatic and oncotic forces;
- filtration-barrier properties;
- number and surface area of functioning glomeruli.

## 5.11 Azotaemia and uraemia

### Azotaemia

Biochemical elevation of nitrogenous waste products such as urea.

### Uraemia

Clinical syndrome caused by severe renal failure and retained toxins, with
systemic signs and symptoms.

```text
azotaemia = laboratory abnormality
uraemia = clinical syndrome
```

## 5.12 Nephritic syndrome

Nephritic syndrome reflects inflammatory glomerular injury.

Main features:

- haematuria;
- dysmorphic red cells;
- red-cell casts;
- subnephrotic proteinuria;
- reduced GFR;
- oliguria;
- azotaemia;
- hypertension;
- oedema may occur.

Mechanism:

```text
glomerular inflammation
→ capillary-wall injury
→ red cells enter filtrate
→ filtration falls
→ salt and water retained
→ hypertension and oedema
```

Examples in the source:

- acute post-infectious glomerulonephritis;
- rapidly progressive/crescentic glomerulonephritis.

## 5.13 Nephrotic syndrome

Nephrotic syndrome reflects marked glomerular permeability to protein.

Features:

- heavy proteinuria, classically more than 3.5 g/day in adults;
- hypoalbuminaemia;
- generalised oedema;
- hyperlipidaemia;
- lipiduria.

Mechanism:

```text
filtration barrier loses protein selectivity
→ albumin lost in urine
→ plasma oncotic pressure falls
→ oedema develops
→ liver increases lipoprotein production
→ hyperlipidaemia/lipiduria
```

Examples in the source:

- minimal-change disease;
- membranous nephropathy.

## 5.14 Nephritic versus nephrotic

| Feature | Nephritic | Nephrotic |
|---|---|---|
| Dominant injury | inflammatory capillary injury | permeability/podocyte injury |
| Urine | blood, dysmorphic RBCs, RBC casts | heavy protein, lipid |
| Protein | usually moderate/subnephrotic | heavy |
| GFR | often reduced | variable |
| Blood pressure | often high | can be normal or high |
| Oedema | from retention, variable | often marked from hypoalbuminaemia |

A patient can have mixed features.

## 5.15 Acute kidney injury

AKI is a rapid decline in GFR over hours to days, causing:

- retention of urea and creatinine;
- fluid and electrolyte dysregulation;
- oliguria or anuria in severe cases.

AKI can be:

- prerenal;
- intrinsic renal;
- postrenal.

The supplied deck focuses on intrinsic tubular injury and obstruction rather
than a complete AKI classification.

## 5.16 Chronic kidney disease

The deck defines CKD using persistent reduction in GFR below 60
mL/min/1.73 m² for at least three months and/or persistent albuminuria.

This is a course diagnostic definition; current clinical staging uses GFR and
albuminuria categories.

CKD is generally progressive and less reversible than many AKI causes.

Systemic effects include:

- anaemia from low erythropoietin;
- mineral/bone disorder;
- acid–base and electrolyte problems;
- fluid overload;
- cardiovascular risk;
- uraemic symptoms.

## 5.17 Congenital anomalies

### Renal agenesis

- bilateral agenesis is incompatible with sustained life and is associated with
  severe fetal consequences;
- unilateral agenesis can be compatible with normal life if the other kidney
  undergoes compensatory hypertrophy.

### Hypoplasia

Kidney fails to reach normal size.

- bilateral severe disease can cause early renal failure;
- unilateral disease may be compensated.

### Ectopic kidney

Kidney lies low or in pelvis.

Abnormal ureteral course can predispose to:

- obstruction;
- infection.

### Horseshoe kidney

Fusion occurs most often at lower poles, creating a midline isthmus.

Abnormal ascent and drainage can increase obstruction or infection risk.

## 5.18 Immune glomerular injury

The source gives two broad antibody mechanisms.

### In-situ antibody binding

Antibody reacts with:

- intrinsic glomerular antigen;
- extrinsic antigen planted in the glomerulus.

### Circulating immune-complex deposition

Antigen–antibody complexes form in circulation and deposit in glomeruli.

Local consequences:

```text
immune deposition or antibody binding
→ complement and cell activation
→ inflammatory mediators
→ endothelial, GBM, mesangial or podocyte injury
→ haematuria, proteinuria and reduced GFR
```

Localisation depends on:

- size;
- charge;
- antigen/antibody properties;
- barrier integrity.

## 5.19 Mediators of glomerular injury

Cells:

- neutrophils;
- monocytes/macrophages;
- T cells;
- platelets;
- resident glomerular cells.

Soluble mediators:

- complement;
- cytokines;
- eicosanoids;
- nitric oxide;
- angiotensin;
- endothelin.

Different combinations produce proliferative, necrotising, membranous or
sclerosing patterns.

## 5.20 Podocyte injury

Podocytes can be injured by:

- antibodies;
- toxins;
- cytokines;
- viral infection;
- genetic defects in slit-diaphragm proteins.

Loss of normal slit diaphragms can produce proteinuria even without major
inflammatory-cell infiltration.

The source names nephrin and podocin mutations as examples.

Podocytes have limited regenerative capacity. Progressive loss exposes the
glomerular tuft to sclerosis.

## 5.21 Histological reaction patterns

The deck lists three broad glomerular responses:

- hypercellularity;
- basement-membrane thickening;
- hyalinosis/sclerosis.

These are reaction patterns rather than single diagnoses.

### Hypercellularity

Can arise from:

- resident-cell proliferation;
- leukocyte infiltration;
- crescent formation.

### Thickening

Can result from:

- immune deposits;
- increased matrix;
- duplicated basement membrane.

### Sclerosis

Replacement of functioning capillary tuft by scar-like matrix.

## 5.22 Acute tubular injury

Acute tubular injury causes AKI with tubular epithelial damage.

### Ischaemic ATI

Caused by reduced/interrupted blood flow, for example severe hypovolaemic shock.

### Nephrotoxic ATI

Caused by:

- endogenous pigments such as haemoglobin, myoglobin or bilirubin;
- drugs;
- heavy metals;
- radiocontrast agents.

Mechanism:

```text
tubular epithelial injury
→ cells lose polarity or die
→ debris obstructs lumen
→ filtrate leaks back
→ GFR falls
```

The term acute tubular necrosis is still used, but not every affected tubule
shows widespread necrosis.

## 5.23 Pyelonephritis

Pyelonephritis is inflammation of:

- tubules;
- interstitium;
- renal pelvis.

### Routes

1. ascending from lower urinary tract;
2. haematogenous through blood.

Ascending infection is common.

### Organisms

The source says most UTIs arise from enteric gram-negative bacilli:

- Escherichia coli most common;
- Proteus;
- Klebsiella;
- Enterobacter.

Other bacteria, fungi and viruses can affect selected patients.

## 5.24 Acute pyelonephritis

Clinical pattern:

- sudden fever;
- malaise;
- flank/costovertebral-angle pain;
- pyuria;
- bacteriuria.

Gross pathology:

- multiple small abscesses;
- wedge-shaped suppurative areas.

Microscopy:

- neutrophils in interstitium and tubules.

White-cell casts support renal rather than purely bladder inflammation.

## 5.25 Chronic pyelonephritis

The source describes repeated bacterial infection with predisposing factors
such as obstruction.

Repeated injury produces:

- irregular cortical scarring;
- calyceal deformity;
- tubular atrophy;
- interstitial fibrosis and chronic inflammation.

Reflux and obstruction are major structural contexts, although the deck does
not provide a complete subtype comparison.

## 5.26 Hypertensive nephropathy

**Nephrosclerosis** is sclerosis of renal arterioles and small arteries strongly
associated with hypertension.

Gross pattern:

- normal or moderately reduced kidneys;
- fine, even, grain-leather cortical granularity.

Vascular narrowing produces chronic ischaemia and nephron loss.

Hypertension can be both:

- a cause of kidney injury;
- a consequence of renal sodium retention and renin activation.

## 5.27 Adult polycystic kidney disease

Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease:

- bilateral;
- PKD1 or PKD2 mutations;
- polycystin abnormalities;
- progressive expansion of cysts;
- eventual destruction of parenchyma.

Grossly:

- very large kidneys;
- numerous cysts of variable size;
- clear, turbid or haemorrhagic fluid.

Clinical features:

- pain;
- haematuria;
- abdominal mass;
- hypertension;
- progressive CKD.

Extrarenal source associations:

- hepatic and other organ cysts;
- intracranial berry aneurysms;
- subarachnoid haemorrhage risk.

## 5.28 Childhood polycystic kidney disease

Autosomal recessive polycystic kidney disease:

- PKHD1 mutation;
- fibrocystin;
- childhood presentation spectrum;
- enlarged smooth kidneys;
- elongated radially oriented collecting-duct cysts;
- associated hepatic disease in the broader syndrome.

Comparison:

| Feature | ADPKD | ARPKD |
|---|---|---|
| Inheritance | dominant | recessive |
| Genes | PKD1/PKD2 | PKHD1 |
| Typical presentation | often adult | infancy/childhood |
| Cysts | variable, widespread | elongated collecting-duct pattern |
| Extrarenal clue | liver cysts, berry aneurysm | congenital hepatic fibrosis pattern |

## 5.29 Urinary-tract obstruction

Obstruction increases risk of:

- infection;
- stone formation;
- renal atrophy.

Causes in the source:

### Congenital

- posterior urethral valves;
- strictures;
- meatal stenosis;
- ureteropelvic-junction obstruction.

### Acquired

- stones;
- benign prostatic enlargement;
- prostate/bladder/cervical/uterine tumours;
- inflammation;
- blood clot or sloughed papilla;
- pregnancy;
- neurogenic or dysfunctional bladder.

## 5.30 Hydronephrosis

**Hydronephrosis** is dilation of renal pelvis and calyces with pressure atrophy
from obstruction.

```text
urine outflow blocked
→ pressure transmitted backward
→ pelvis and calyces dilate
→ tubules compress
→ cortex thins
→ irreversible atrophy if prolonged
```

The degree depends on:

- completeness;
- duration;
- unilateral versus bilateral;
- level.

### Clinical patterns

- acute obstruction: pain;
- ureteric stone: renal colic;
- unilateral obstruction: may be silent because opposite kidney compensates;
- complete bilateral obstruction: oliguria/anuria and life-threatening renal
  failure.

## 5.31 Urolithiasis

Four principal stone groups in the source:

1. calcium stones;
2. magnesium ammonium phosphate/struvite stones;
3. uric-acid stones;
4. cystine stones.

The deck gives approximate proportions, with calcium most common.

## 5.32 Calcium stones

Usually calcium oxalate with or without calcium phosphate.

Associations:

- hypercalciuria;
- sometimes hypercalcaemia;
- dehydration and urine chemistry factors.

Calcium oxalate stones can occur even without high serum calcium.

## 5.33 Struvite stones

Magnesium ammonium phosphate stones form with urease-positive infection.

Source organisms include Proteus and some staphylococci.

Mechanism:

```text
urease splits urea
→ ammonia rises
→ urine becomes alkaline
→ magnesium ammonium phosphate precipitates
→ stone enlarges
```

They can form branching staghorn calculi.

## 5.34 Uric-acid stones

Associations:

- hyperuricaemia;
- gout;
- high cell turnover such as leukaemia;
- acidic urine.

They may not be visible on ordinary radiographs.

## 5.35 Cystine stones

Caused by inherited defects in renal amino-acid transport leading to
cystinuria.

Cystine has poor solubility and can form stones, often in younger patients.

## 5.36 Clinical effects of stones

- asymptomatic;
- severe colicky flank pain;
- haematuria;
- obstruction;
- infection;
- recurrent renal damage.

Pain often radiates along the ureter toward groin as smooth muscle contracts
around an obstructing stone.

## 5.37 Benign renal tumours

### Renal papillary adenoma

Small benign papillary epithelial lesion.

### Angiomyolipoma

Contains:

- blood vessels;
- smooth muscle;
- fat.

The name itself encodes its three components.

Larger lesions can bleed.

## 5.38 Renal-cell carcinoma

RCC occurs most often in older adults and is more common in males in the source.

Features:

- often arises at a pole;
- may remain silent until large;
- haematuria;
- flank pain;
- palpable mass;
- constitutional symptoms.

The classic triad is not present in every patient and often indicates advanced
disease.

Associations in the source:

- von Hippel–Lindau syndrome;
- Birt–Hogg–Dubé syndrome.

Renal tumours can also cause paraneoplastic hormone effects, though the deck
does not expand them here.

## 5.39 Wilms tumour

Wilms tumour/nephroblastoma is a childhood renal malignancy, commonly presenting
between ages 2 and 5 in the source.

Presentation:

- large abdominal mass;
- haematuria;
- abdominal pain;
- possible obstruction.

It can be bilateral, synchronously or metachronously.

### Associated syndromes

#### WAGR

- Wilms tumour;
- aniridia;
- genitourinary anomalies;
- intellectual disability.

The ocular sign **aniridia** is highly relevant to optometry.

#### Denys–Drash

- gonadal dysgenesis;
- early nephropathy;
- Wilms risk.

#### Beckwith–Wiedemann

- organomegaly;
- macroglossia;
- hemihyperplasia;
- omphalocele;
- tumour predisposition.

The deck lists WT1, WT2-region and PAX6-related genetic relationships.

> **Terminology note:** These notes use “intellectual disability” rather than
> the outdated term appearing parenthetically in the source slide.

## Worked example: nephritic versus nephrotic

### Patient A

- cola-coloured urine;
- RBC casts;
- hypertension;
- reduced GFR;
- moderate proteinuria.

Pattern:

```text
nephritic syndrome
```

### Patient B

- massive proteinuria;
- low albumin;
- generalised oedema;
- hyperlipidaemia.

Pattern:

```text
nephrotic syndrome
```

The syndromes classify a presentation; biopsy or serology may still be needed
for the exact disease.

## Worked example: stone mechanism

A patient has recurrent UTI with Proteus and a large branching renal stone.

Reasoning:

```text
Proteus urease
→ ammonia and alkaline urine
→ magnesium ammonium phosphate precipitation
→ struvite/staghorn stone
```

The infection is part of the stone mechanism rather than merely a consequence.

## Common traps

### “The nephron is only the glomerulus.”

It includes glomerulus and tubular segments through the collecting system.

### “Fenestrated endothelium means blood cells normally enter urine.”

Fenestrae permit water and solutes, while the complete barrier retains cells.

### “The GBM alone is the protein barrier.”

Endothelium, glycocalyx, GBM and podocyte slit diaphragms work together.

### “Azotaemia and uraemia are synonyms.”

One is biochemical; the other is a symptomatic systemic syndrome.

### “Nephritic and nephrotic syndromes are the same.”

Nephritic is inflammatory/haematuric; nephrotic is heavy protein loss.

### “Every AKI patient is anuric.”

Urine output can be reduced, normal or occasionally increased depending on
cause.

### “Pyelonephritis is infection of the bladder only.”

It involves renal pelvis, tubules and interstitium.

### “ADPKD affects only kidneys.”

It has important extrarenal cyst and vascular associations.

### “Hydronephrosis is a cystic genetic disease.”

It is pressure dilation from urinary obstruction.

### “All calcium-stone patients have hypercalcaemia.”

Many have hypercalciuria with normal serum calcium.

### “Struvite stones form in acidic sterile urine.”

They are linked to urease-positive infection and alkaline urine.

### “The RCC triad is an early universal presentation.”

Many tumours are silent or present with only one feature.

### “WAGR has no ocular relevance.”

Aniridia is a defining feature.

## Key terms

- **Nephron** — functional renal unit.
- **Glomerulus** — capillary tuft filtering plasma.
- **Podocyte** — visceral epithelial cell with foot processes.
- **Filtration slit** — gap between podocyte foot processes bridged by
  diaphragm.
- **Mesangial cell** — supportive, contractile and phagocytic glomerular cell.
- **GBM** — glomerular basement membrane.
- **Bowman space** — urinary space receiving filtrate.
- **Juxtaglomerular apparatus** — macula densa, JG cells and associated
  mesangial region regulating renin.
- **GFR** — glomerular filtration rate.
- **Azotaemia** — biochemical retention of nitrogenous waste.
- **Uraemia** — symptomatic systemic syndrome of severe renal failure.
- **Nephritic syndrome** — inflammatory glomerular syndrome with haematuria and
  reduced filtration.
- **Nephrotic syndrome** — heavy proteinuria with hypoalbuminaemia and oedema.
- **AKI** — rapid decline in kidney function.
- **CKD** — persistent kidney damage or reduced GFR.
- **Crescent** — proliferative lesion filling Bowman space in severe GN.
- **Acute tubular injury** — ischaemic or toxic tubular epithelial damage.
- **Pyuria** — leukocytes in urine.
- **Pyelonephritis** — inflammation/infection of renal pelvis, tubules and
  interstitium.
- **Nephrosclerosis** — sclerosis of renal small vessels associated with
  hypertension.
- **ADPKD** — autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease.
- **ARPKD** — autosomal recessive polycystic kidney disease.
- **Hydronephrosis** — pressure dilation of pelvis and calyces from obstruction.
- **Renal colic** — severe spasmodic pain from urinary obstruction.
- **Struvite** — magnesium ammonium phosphate infection stone.
- **Cystinuria** — inherited renal amino-acid transport defect.
- **Angiomyolipoma** — benign tumour containing vessels, muscle and fat.
- **Renal-cell carcinoma** — common adult renal epithelial malignancy.
- **Wilms tumour** — childhood nephroblastoma.
- **Aniridia** — absent or severely hypoplastic iris, associated with WAGR.

## One-minute summary

- Kidney disease can be localised to glomeruli, tubules/interstitium, vessels,
  urinary outflow or parenchymal cyst/tumour.
- Renal blood flows through afferent arteriole, glomerular tuft and efferent
  arteriole.
- The filtration barrier combines fenestrated endothelium, GBM and podocyte slit
  diaphragm.
- The JGA links distal salt/perfusion sensing to renin.
- Azotaemia is biochemical; uraemia is a clinical syndrome.
- Nephritic disease causes haematuria, casts, reduced GFR and hypertension;
  nephrotic disease causes heavy protein loss, hypoalbuminaemia, oedema and
  hyperlipidaemia.
- Immune deposition and podocyte injury are major glomerular mechanisms.
- Acute tubular injury can be ischaemic or toxic.
- Pyelonephritis affects pelvis, tubules and interstitium, usually through
  ascending infection.
- Hypertension causes small-vessel sclerosis and chronic ischaemic kidney
  damage.
- ADPKD uses PKD1/PKD2 and has berry-aneurysm risk; ARPKD uses PKHD1.
- Prolonged obstruction causes hydronephrosis and parenchymal atrophy.
- Calcium stones are common; struvite stones require urease-positive infection;
  uric-acid and cystine stones have distinct metabolic causes.
- RCC is an adult tumour that may remain silent; Wilms is a childhood tumour
  associated with WAGR, Denys–Drash and Beckwith–Wiedemann patterns.
