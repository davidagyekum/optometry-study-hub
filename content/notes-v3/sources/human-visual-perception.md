## How to use these notes

Human visual perception is not produced by the eyeball alone. It emerges from a
connected system:

```text
environmental light
→ ocular media
→ retina
→ optic nerve, chiasm and tract
→ lateral geniculate nucleus
→ primary visual cortex
→ extrastriate and association areas
→ recognition, spatial guidance and action
```

The central learning principle is:

```text
anatomical organisation
→ determines physiological processing
→ shapes perceptual experience
→ predicts the deficit produced by disease
```

The focus labels mean:

- **Must know:** central structures, pathways and mechanisms.
- **Should know:** supporting detail needed to explain the core model.
- **Useful extension:** clinically valuable depth that should not replace the
  main sequence.

> **Course boundary:** These notes cover the supplied introduction, retina,
> LGN/V1 and extrastriate teaching block. The course schedule also names later
> blocks on depth perception, stereopsis, colour testing, motion perception,
> entoptic phenomena and additional illusions. Those later topics are not
> silently expanded here beyond concepts already introduced in the supplied
> decks.

# 1. Sensation, Perception and Visual-System Organisation

## Big picture

**Sensation** is the detection and neural encoding of physical stimulation.
**Perception** is the selection, organisation and interpretation of that sensory
information.

The two are connected but not identical. A retina may register light and a
visual pathway may carry signals normally, yet a patient can still fail to
recognise an object, attend to one side of space or interpret motion. Conversely,
the brain may use context to assign meaning to an incomplete or ambiguous
retinal signal.

The course therefore treats perception as a useful window into how the brain
works. Three broad conditions must cooperate:

```text
appropriate anatomy
+ functioning physiology
+ central processing
→ useful visual perception
```

## What to focus on

### Must know

- sensation versus perception;
- physical stimulus, transduction, detection and interpretation;
- bottom-up and top-down processing;
- figure–ground organisation;
- why an illusion can reveal a normal perceptual rule;
- the eye as only one component of the visual system;
- the main image-forming pathway;
- non-image-forming retinal pathways;
- why intact acuity does not guarantee intact perception;
- why cortical damage can spare the pupillary light reflex.

### Should know

- context, attention and prior knowledge as influences on perception;
- reversible figures;
- perceptual completion at the physiological blind spot;
- ocular-media abnormalities as changes to the sensory input;
- the broad posterior-to-anterior progression from sensory analysis to action
  planning.

### Useful extension

- severe visual impairment and visual hallucinations as different from
  psychosis;
- why two patients with similar acuity may report very different visual
  experiences;
- how lesion localisation follows the failed perceptual operation.

## 1.1 From environmental event to percept

A visual event begins outside the nervous system. Light reflected or emitted
from an object reaches the eye. The ocular media form an image on the retina.
Photoreceptors convert optical energy into a neural signal. Retinal circuits
compare and reorganise that signal before ganglion-cell axons send it toward the
brain.

A simplified sequence is:

```text
physical light
→ optical image
→ photoreceptor transduction
→ retinal coding
→ thalamic and cortical analysis
→ meaningful percept
```

### Sensation

Sensation includes operations such as:

- a photopigment absorbing photons;
- a photoreceptor changing its membrane response;
- a ganglion cell altering its firing;
- a change in brightness being detected.

It answers the question:

```text
Was stimulation registered and encoded?
```

### Perception

Perception includes operations such as:

- separating figure from background;
- grouping edges into an object;
- recognising a face;
- judging that an object is approaching;
- using context to interpret an ambiguous symbol.

It answers:

```text
What does the sensory pattern mean?
```

## 1.2 Bottom-up and top-down processing

### Bottom-up processing

**Bottom-up processing** begins with features in the incoming stimulus.

Examples include:

- local contrast;
- line orientation;
- colour differences;
- motion;
- binocular disparity.

The system builds from simpler encoded features toward more complex
representations.

```text
local sensory evidence
→ feature extraction
→ grouping
→ object or scene representation
```

### Top-down processing

**Top-down processing** uses information already available to the observer:

- expectations;
- attention;
- memory;
- language;
- context;
- task goals.

Top-down influence does not mean that the brain invents everything. It means
that interpretation is constrained by both sensory evidence and prior
knowledge.

### Their interaction

Most normal perception combines the two.

A poorly printed word may still be read because:

```text
partial letter features
+ sentence context
+ language knowledge
→ likely word interpretation
```

If the sensory evidence becomes too weak or contradictory, top-down prediction
may become less reliable.

## 1.3 Figure and ground

A visual scene contains more information than can be treated as equally
important at once. The perceptual system assigns one region as **figure** and
another as **ground**.

The figure usually appears:

- more object-like;
- more shaped;
- closer;
- more memorable.

The ground appears to continue behind it.

### Reversible figures

In Rubin’s vase, the same border can belong either to:

- a central vase;
- two facing profiles.

The retinal image does not need to change for the percept to alternate.

This demonstrates:

```text
same sensory pattern
→ different border ownership
→ different figure–ground assignment
→ different conscious percept
```

An illusion is therefore not simply a “mistake.” It can expose the assumptions
the visual system normally uses to create a stable world.

## 1.4 Perceptual organisation and ambiguity

The visual system tends to organise fragments into coherent forms. Although the
supplied block introduces selected organisation principles rather than a
complete Gestalt syllabus, the central point is that perception operates on
relationships, not isolated pixels.

The system asks implicitly:

- which edges belong together?
- which region is likely to be one surface?
- what continues behind an obstruction?
- which interpretation best fits the context?

This helps explain why incomplete drawings, occluded objects and ambiguous
symbols can still be recognised.

## 1.5 The visual system is more than the eye

The older course overview explicitly states that the eyeball is only a small
part of the human visual system.

The system includes:

- ocular adnexa and optical media;
- retina;
- optic nerve;
- optic chiasm;
- optic tract;
- lateral geniculate nucleus;
- optic radiations;
- primary visual cortex;
- extrastriate visual cortex;
- parietal, temporal and frontal association systems;
- non-image-forming retinal targets.

### Main image-forming pathway

The course uses the retino-geniculo-cortical pathway as the main image-forming
route:

```text
retina
→ optic nerve
→ optic chiasm
→ optic tract
→ LGN
→ optic radiations
→ V1
→ extrastriate cortex
```

The deck uses an approximate statement that about 90% of retinal signals follow
the LGN–V1 route. Learn this as a course-scale description, not as an exact
count for every ganglion-cell class.

### Non-image-forming routes

A smaller proportion of retinal output supports functions not primarily aimed
at conscious image formation.

Two course examples are:

- retina → suprachiasmatic nucleus: circadian photoentrainment;
- retina → pretectal region: pupillary light responses.

This distinction becomes clinically useful in cortical blindness: the patient
may lack conscious visual perception while direct and consensual pupillary
responses remain intact.

## 1.6 Ocular media shape the sensory input

Perception begins with the optical signal delivered to the retina. Abnormal
adnexa or media can change that input before cortical interpretation begins.

The course provides examples such as:

- tear-film deficiency reducing image quality;
- corneal oedema producing coloured fringes or haloes;
- cataract producing fog, reduced contrast and glare;
- post-refractive-surgery ghost images;
- posterior vitreous or retinal traction producing photopsia.

The correct reasoning is:

```text
optical or retinal disturbance
→ altered sensory signal
→ altered perceptual experience
```

This differs from a higher cortical disorder, where early visual input may be
adequate but interpretation fails.

## 1.7 The physiological blind spot

The optic disc contains exiting retinal ganglion-cell axons and no
photoreceptors. It therefore corresponds to a **blind spot in the visual field**.

The blind spot is not a dark patch visible on the retina. It is a field
location from which no photoreceptor signal is generated.

Why is it usually unnoticed?

- the other eye often views the corresponding part of space;
- surrounding patterns permit perceptual completion;
- stable scenes and eye movements reduce awareness of the gap;
- the observer is not normally directing attention to it.

### Common trap

**Wrong idea:** “The blind spot is a black spot inside the eye.”

**Correction:** The optic disc is an anatomical retinal location; the
physiological blind spot is the corresponding missing region of visual field.

## 1.8 Acuity and perception are not interchangeable

Visual acuity measures the ability to resolve fine detail under specified
conditions. It does not by itself test:

- object recognition;
- face recognition;
- motion perception;
- visual attention;
- spatial guidance;
- integration with memory.

A patient can therefore have useful acuity but severe perceptual dysfunction.

The reverse is also important: poor acuity can make recognition difficult
without implying a cortical agnosia.

Clinical interpretation must separate:

```text
input quality
from
higher interpretation
```

## 1.9 Attention

Attention selects which information receives priority. It can change:

- detection;
- speed of response;
- competition between objects;
- awareness of one region of space.

Hemispatial neglect later demonstrates that a major failure of spatial
attention can occur even when early sensory pathways are not completely
destroyed.

## 1.10 Visual illusions as evidence

The course introduces illusions because they reveal normal computations.

An illusion may show that the brain uses:

- context;
- surrounding contrast;
- perspective cues;
- expected object relationships;
- border ownership;
- prior experience.

The lesson is not that vision is unreliable. The lesson is that the brain uses
efficient rules that are usually useful but can be exposed by specially
constructed stimuli.

## 1.11 Clinical comparison: sensory loss versus perceptual loss

### Case A: degraded input

A patient with cataract reports fog, glare and loss of contrast. Recognition
improves when the object is enlarged and contrast is increased.

Interpretation:

```text
optical input is degraded
→ sensory representation is weak
→ perception improves when the input is strengthened
```

### Case B: selective recognition failure

A patient sees an object clearly enough to describe its colour and shape but
cannot identify what it is.

Interpretation:

```text
early visual analysis is partly preserved
→ meaningful object identification is impaired
→ higher visual association dysfunction is possible
```

The second patient should not be labelled as having “poor vision” without
specifying the failed operation.

## Worked example: analysing an ambiguous symbol

A symbol can be read as one letter in a word and as a number in a numerical
sequence.

Reasoning:

1. The line pattern reaching the retina is similar.
2. Bottom-up processing supplies edges and shape.
3. Surrounding symbols create linguistic or numerical context.
4. Top-down expectation selects the most plausible interpretation.
5. Conscious perception changes even though the central symbol does not.

This is a compact demonstration of perception as constrained inference.

## Common traps

### “Perception is a photograph made by the brain.”

Perception is an active, organised interpretation of encoded signals.

### “Top-down processing means imagination.”

Top-down influence uses context and prior knowledge while remaining constrained
by sensory evidence.

### “A normal acuity score proves normal visual perception.”

Acuity tests one important but limited visual function.

### “An illusion demonstrates a diseased brain.”

Many illusions reveal rules used by a healthy perceptual system.

### “The pupillary light reflex proves conscious sight.”

The pretectal reflex pathway can remain functional when occipital cortex is
damaged.

### “Every topic named in the course timetable is fully covered by the supplied
slides.”

The current source block does not provide complete later teaching on depth,
full colour testing, entoptic phenomena or all motion topics.

## Key terms

- **Stimulus** — physical event capable of activating a sensory system.
- **Sensation** — detection and neural encoding of stimulation.
- **Transduction** — conversion of one form of energy into a neural signal.
- **Perception** — selection, organisation and interpretation of sensory
  information.
- **Bottom-up processing** — analysis driven primarily by incoming stimulus
  features.
- **Top-down processing** — influence of context, expectation, attention and
  knowledge on interpretation.
- **Figure–ground organisation** — assignment of one region as object and
  another as background.
- **Reversible figure** — stimulus supporting alternating valid percepts.
- **Perceptual completion** — representation of a continuous object or pattern
  despite missing sensory input.
- **Visual acuity** — ability to resolve fine spatial detail.
- **Image-forming pathway** — retinal route supporting conscious visual
  representation through LGN, V1 and higher cortex.
- **Non-image-forming pathway** — retinal output supporting functions such as
  circadian entrainment or the light reflex.
- **Physiological blind spot** — field location corresponding to the
  photoreceptor-free optic disc.
- **Photopsia** — perception of flashes without an external matching light.

# 2. Retinal Organisation, Coding and Adaptation

## Big picture

The retina is neural tissue. It does not merely record an optical image; it
transduces light and begins extracting useful visual relationships before any
signal reaches the brain.

The supplied course gives the retina three broad functions:

```text
transduce light into electrochemical activity
→ perform initial processing
→ convert the processed activity into ganglion-cell output
```

The key flow is:

```text
photoreceptors
→ bipolar cells
→ retinal ganglion cells
→ optic nerve
```

Horizontal and amacrine cells modify this vertical route. They help create
contrast, timing and spatial relationships rather than simply forwarding
brightness values.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- the ten retinal layers in order;
- direction of incident light versus direction of neural signalling;
- rods, cones and intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells;
- rhodopsin, cone photopigments and melanopsin;
- foveal versus peripheral specialisation;
- optic disc and retinal nerve-fibre layer;
- bipolar, horizontal, amacrine and ganglion-cell roles;
- convergence and the sensitivity–acuity trade-off;
- ON-centre and OFF-centre organisation;
- centre–surround antagonism and lateral inhibition;
- midget, parasol and small bistratified pathways;
- scotopic, mesopic and photopic vision;
- light and dark adaptation.

### Should know

- retinal pigment epithelium and outer-segment support;
- macular pigments lutein and zeaxanthin;
- the qualified S-cone distribution at the foveal centre;
- the near one-to-one foveal cone pathway;
- why the retinal image is spatially inverted but perception is not experienced
  as upside down;
- RNFL appearance and its clinical assessment.

### Useful extension

- OCT and ophthalmoscopic analysis of the RNFL;
- ipRGC contribution to circadian and pupillary functions;
- how receptive fields predict simultaneous contrast and edge enhancement;
- why rod and cone adaptation have different time courses.

## 2.1 The ten retinal layers

The course lists the retinal layers from the outer retinal side toward the
vitreous:

1. retinal pigment epithelium;
2. photoreceptor layer;
3. outer limiting membrane;
4. outer nuclear layer;
5. outer plexiform layer;
6. inner nuclear layer;
7. inner plexiform layer;
8. ganglion-cell layer;
9. nerve-fibre layer;
10. inner limiting membrane.

### A structure–function map

| Layer | Principal course relationship |
|---|---|
| Retinal pigment epithelium | supports photoreceptor outer segments and absorbs stray light |
| Photoreceptor layer | rod and cone inner/outer segments |
| Outer limiting membrane | junctional boundary near photoreceptor cell bodies |
| Outer nuclear layer | photoreceptor nuclei |
| Outer plexiform layer | photoreceptor synapses with bipolar and horizontal cells |
| Inner nuclear layer | bipolar, horizontal, amacrine and Müller-cell nuclei |
| Inner plexiform layer | bipolar–amacrine–ganglion synaptic interactions |
| Ganglion-cell layer | retinal ganglion-cell bodies |
| Nerve-fibre layer | unmyelinated ganglion-cell axons travelling to optic disc |
| Inner limiting membrane | innermost retinal boundary adjacent to vitreous |

The layer list is easier to remember if linked to signal flow rather than
memorised as ten unrelated names.

## 2.2 Why the retina is described as “inverted”

Light entering from the vitreous side passes through inner retinal tissue before
reaching photoreceptor outer segments near the RPE.

Neural output then travels in the opposite direction:

```text
light:
vitreous side → outer retina → photoreceptors

neural signal:
photoreceptors → bipolar cells → ganglion cells → optic nerve
```

The arrangement supports intimate contact between photoreceptor outer segments,
RPE and choroidal metabolic support.

### Common trap

**Wrong idea:** “Because the retina is inverted, the brain must physically turn
a photograph upright.”

**Correction:** Retinal and cortical maps preserve spatial relationships. The
system learns stable correspondences between visual direction, eye movement and
action; perception is not experienced as a photograph being rotated.

## 2.3 Retinal pigment epithelium

The **retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)** lies adjacent to photoreceptor outer
segments.

Within the supplied visual-system block, its important relationships include:

- absorbing scattered light;
- supporting photoreceptor outer-segment function;
- maintaining the outer retinal environment;
- participating in exchange with the choroid.

The RPE is not a photoreceptor layer, but normal photoreceptor function depends
on it.

## 2.4 Phototransduction

**Phototransduction** converts light into a change in photoreceptor electrical
activity.

The course-level sequence is:

```text
photon reaches photopigment
→ photopigment changes state
→ intracellular signalling changes
→ membrane current changes
→ transmitter release changes
→ bipolar-cell response changes
```

A critical physiological feature is that rods and cones respond to light by
reducing their dark current and hyperpolarising relative to the dark state.
They therefore signal through changes in graded membrane potential and
glutamate release rather than by firing ordinary action potentials down a long
axon.

## 2.5 Rods, cones and ipRGCs

### Rods

Rods are specialised for high sensitivity in dim illumination.

They support:

- scotopic vision;
- detection of weak light;
- broad peripheral sensitivity;
- motion or change detection under low-light conditions.

They provide little useful colour discrimination because the rod system has one
principal spectral photopigment class.

The rod photopigment is **rhodopsin**.

### Cones

Cones support:

- photopic vision;
- high spatial acuity;
- rapid temporal responses;
- colour discrimination.

The course describes three cone classes:

- S-cones: relatively short-wavelength sensitivity;
- M-cones: medium-wavelength sensitivity;
- L-cones: long-wavelength sensitivity.

Calling them simply blue, green and red cones is a useful classroom shorthand,
but each class responds over a range of wavelengths and colour perception
depends on comparison among cone outputs.

### Intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells

**ipRGCs** contain **melanopsin** and can respond to light independently of
ordinary rod and cone input, while also receiving synaptic input from them.

They are especially important for:

- circadian entrainment;
- sustained irradiance signalling;
- pupillary light responses.

> **Course correction:** One supplied deck uses “melanin” in this context. The
> photopigment is **melanopsin**. Melanin is a different pigment.

### Beyond the phrase “duplex retina”

The traditional phrase **duplex retina** describes rod and cone subsystems.
The course notes that ipRGCs mean retinal photosensitivity is not limited to
only two photoreceptor classes.

For the module, retain both ideas:

```text
duplex retina
= useful rod-versus-cone functional model

ipRGC discovery
= additional intrinsic photosensitivity with non-image-forming importance
```

## 2.6 Photopigments and macular pigment

The course names:

- rhodopsin for rods;
- cone pigments associated with S, M and L spectral sensitivities;
- melanopsin for ipRGCs;
- lutein and zeaxanthin as macular xanthophyll pigments.

Macular pigments are not cone photopigments. They filter and absorb selected
short-wavelength light in the macular region.

## 2.7 Distribution of rods and cones

Photoreceptors are not evenly distributed.

### Foveal region

The foveal centre is specialised for maximum spatial resolution:

- very high cone packing;
- rods excluded from the central foveola;
- inner retinal layers displaced away from the optical axis;
- no retinal vasculature crossing the foveal avascular centre;
- low convergence through midget pathways;
- macular pigment.

### Peripheral retina

The peripheral retina has:

- more rods;
- greater convergence;
- larger receptive fields;
- higher sensitivity to weak light;
- lower fine spatial resolution.

### The sensitivity–acuity trade-off

```text
many photoreceptors converge on fewer output cells
→ weak signals summate
→ sensitivity improves
→ exact spatial origin is less distinct
→ acuity falls
```

At the fovea:

```text
low convergence and dense cone sampling
→ spatial origin is preserved
→ acuity improves
→ sensitivity to very weak light is lower
```

## 2.8 Foveal architecture

### Displaced inner layers

The foveal pit displaces inner retinal elements away from the central cone
mosaic. This reduces light scatter and allows a more direct optical path.

### Cone packing

Dense cone sampling gives a small spatial sampling interval and supports high
acuity.

### Foveal avascularity

Retinal vessels do not cross the foveal centre. Outer retinal metabolism is
supported principally from the choroid, and nearby retinal capillaries support
surrounding inner tissue.

### Near one-to-one connectivity

The source uses a nearly 1:1:1 cone–bipolar–ganglion relationship to explain
foveal acuity.

> **Course qualification:** This is a powerful simplified model, especially for
> central midget circuitry. It should not be read as a universally exact
> one-cone-to-one-bipolar-to-one-ganglion rule for every cone, cell subtype and
> eccentricity.

### S-cones at the centre

The deck describes an absence of S-cones at the fovea.

> **Course qualification:** The defensible statement is that S-cones are sparse
> or absent in the very central foveola and become more represented outside it.
> Do not generalise this to the entire anatomical fovea.

## 2.9 Optic disc and RNFL

The **optic disc** is where ganglion-cell axons leave the globe. It contains no
rods or cones and creates the physiological blind spot.

Before reaching the disc, the unmyelinated axons travel across the inner retina
as the **retinal nerve-fibre layer (RNFL)**.

The course emphasises that axons from different retinal regions follow ordered
paths to the disc.

### Clinical connection

RNFL loss is an important sign in glaucoma and other optic neuropathies.
Clinicians assess it through:

- ophthalmoscopic appearance;
- retinal photography;
- optical coherence tomography.

The RNFL is neural output tissue, not a layer of photoreceptors.

## 2.10 Vertical and lateral retinal pathways

### Vertical pathway

```text
photoreceptor
→ bipolar cell
→ ganglion cell
```

This carries information toward the optic nerve.

### Horizontal pathway

Horizontal cells connect across the outer retina and influence
photoreceptor-to-bipolar transmission.

### Amacrine pathway

Amacrine cells act within the inner retina between bipolar and ganglion cells.
Different types contribute to timing, motion, rod signalling and other
modulation.

The important principle is:

```text
retina does not send raw brightness values
→ neighbouring and temporal signals interact
→ ganglion cells encode contrast and change
```

## 2.11 Bipolar cells

Bipolar cells receive photoreceptor signals and transmit them toward ganglion
cells.

Functionally, ON and OFF pathways respond differently to a reduction in
photoreceptor glutamate.

### ON pathway

An ON bipolar pathway becomes relatively more active when light increases in
its receptive-field centre.

### OFF pathway

An OFF bipolar pathway becomes relatively more active when light decreases in
its receptive-field centre.

This creates parallel signalling for increments and decrements rather than one
single brightness channel.

## 2.12 Retinal ganglion cells

Retinal ganglion cells are the output neurons of the retina. Their axons form
the optic nerve and project to several brain targets.

The course highlights several common classes.

### Midget ganglion cells

- small receptive fields, especially centrally;
- linked strongly to parvocellular processing;
- preserve fine spatial and red–green opponent information.

### Parasol ganglion cells

- larger receptive fields;
- linked strongly to magnocellular processing;
- sensitive to temporal change, contrast and motion-related information.

### Small bistratified ganglion cells

- linked to koniocellular pathways;
- contribute to short-wavelength colour-opponent signalling.

### ipRGCs

- contain melanopsin;
- support irradiance, circadian and pupillary functions.

> **Course correction:** The correct broad relationship is:
>
> ```text
> midget → parvocellular
> parasol → magnocellular
> small bistratified → koniocellular
> ```

## 2.13 Receptive fields

A cell’s **receptive field** is the region of visual space in which stimulation
changes that cell’s response.

Retinal and classical LGN receptive fields often have antagonistic centre and
surround regions.

### ON-centre/OFF-surround

- light in centre tends to increase response;
- light in surround tends to suppress it.

### OFF-centre/ON-surround

- darkness or light decrement in centre tends to increase response;
- light in surround produces the opposite influence.

The strongest response often occurs when centre and surround receive different
stimulation.

## 2.14 Centre–surround antagonism

Centre and surround do not act as independent spots. Their opposing influences
make ganglion cells sensitive to spatial contrast.

```text
uniform illumination of centre and surround
→ opposing influences partly cancel
→ modest response

edge crossing receptive field
→ centre and surround differ
→ stronger response
```

This is why retinal output emphasises borders and changes rather than reporting
absolute illumination alone.

## 2.15 Lateral inhibition

**Lateral inhibition** is the suppression of a neuronal response by activity in
neighbouring pathways.

Horizontal-cell interactions contribute to outer-retinal lateral influence.
The result can include:

- edge enhancement;
- simultaneous brightness contrast;
- improved detection of local differences.

### Worked perceptual link

A grey patch can appear lighter on a dark background and darker on a light
background.

Reasoning:

```text
same central luminance
+ different surrounding luminance
→ different surround inhibition
→ different encoded contrast
→ different brightness percept
```

The percept depends on relationships, not only the patch’s physical luminance.

## 2.16 Convergence and summation

### Spatial convergence

Several photoreceptors may influence one bipolar or ganglion pathway.

### Spatial summation

Weak simultaneous inputs can combine to reach a detectable response.

This is useful in dim light but reduces localisation.

### Temporal summation

Signals arriving close together in time can combine.

The course-level rule is:

```text
more summation
→ more sensitivity
→ less spatial or temporal precision
```

## 2.17 Scotopic, mesopic and photopic vision

### Scotopic vision

- very low illumination;
- rod-dominated;
- high sensitivity;
- poor colour discrimination;
- lower acuity.

### Photopic vision

- bright illumination;
- cone-dominated;
- high acuity;
- colour discrimination.

### Mesopic vision

- intermediate illumination;
- rods and cones both contribute;
- colour and contrast performance can differ from bright-light expectations.

This matters clinically when a patient reports adequate daytime vision but poor
night performance.

## 2.18 Light adaptation

**Light adaptation** occurs when the visual system moves from dark to bright
conditions.

The system reduces excessive sensitivity through:

- rapid neural gain changes;
- photopigment bleaching and recovery balance;
- pupil constriction;
- increasing cone dominance.

Useful vision in bright light develops relatively quickly.

## 2.19 Dark adaptation

**Dark adaptation** is the recovery of sensitivity after moving from bright to
dark conditions.

The course emphasises two phases:

1. a faster cone-dominated phase;
2. a slower but more sensitive rod-dominated phase.

A typical dark-adaptation curve therefore shows a cone–rod break as rod
sensitivity surpasses cone sensitivity.

```text
bright exposure
→ photopigment bleaching and reduced sensitivity
→ cone recovery first
→ rod pigment and rod sensitivity recover more slowly
→ final threshold becomes much lower
```

The precise time varies with bleaching, wavelength, retinal location and
individual physiology.

## 2.20 Why night blindness occurs

Night blindness can arise when rod function, rod photopigment availability or
rod pathways are impaired.

The perceptual complaint is therefore not just “poor acuity.” It is failure to
reach adequate sensitivity under scotopic conditions.

The supplied deck raises the question but does not provide a complete
differential diagnosis, so these notes do not turn it into an exhaustive disease
list.

## Worked example: predicting performance from retinal organisation

Two targets have the same size.

- Target A is viewed centrally in bright light.
- Target B is viewed peripherally in dim light.

Prediction:

### Target A

Dense foveal cones and low convergence support fine detail and colour.

### Target B

Peripheral rods and convergence improve detection of weak light, but precise
form and colour are poorer.

The “better” retinal region therefore depends on the task.

## Common traps

### “Photoreceptors send action potentials directly into the optic nerve.”

Photoreceptors use graded responses; ganglion cells generate the major retinal
action-potential output.

### “Only rods and cones are light sensitive.”

ipRGCs also contain an intrinsic photopigment, melanopsin.

### “The fovea contains no S-cones anywhere.”

S-cones are especially sparse or absent in the central foveola, not necessarily
across the entire foveal region.

### “Every foveal cone has an exact universal one-to-one chain.”

Near one-to-one midget circuitry is a useful central model, not an absolute for
every cell.

### “More convergence always improves vision.”

It improves sensitivity while sacrificing spatial precision.

### “Ganglion cells simply report local brightness.”

Centre–surround organisation makes them strongly responsive to contrast and
edges.

### “The optic disc is a dark patch that the person constantly sees.”

It produces a field gap that is normally masked by binocular viewing and
perceptual completion.

### “Light and neural signals travel through the retina in the same direction.”

They travel in opposite directions through the layered retina.

# 3. Lateral Geniculate Nucleus and Primary Visual Cortex

## Big picture

Retinal ganglion-cell axons do not project randomly into cortex. The main
image-forming pathway preserves visual-field position, keeps eye-of-origin
signals organised and carries partly specialised M, P and K streams through the
lateral geniculate nucleus to primary visual cortex.

The course sequence is:

```text
retinal ganglion cells
→ optic nerve
→ optic chiasm
→ optic tract
→ LGN
→ optic radiations
→ V1
```

The LGN continues many retinal operations, including centre–surround and
colour-opponent signalling. V1 then transforms the representation: many neurons
prefer edges or bars of a particular orientation, direction, disparity and
retinal location.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- nasal fibres crossing and temporal fibres remaining uncrossed;
- each optic tract carrying the opposite visual hemifield;
- six principal LGN layers;
- crossed-eye and uncrossed-eye layer pattern;
- magnocellular, parvocellular and koniocellular organisation;
- midget–P, parasol–M and small-bistratified–K relationships;
- LGN centre–surround and colour opponency;
- V1 as striate cortex/Brodmann area 17;
- V1 location around the calcarine sulcus;
- M input to layer 4Cα and P input to 4Cβ;
- retinotopy;
- orientation and direction selectivity;
- ocular dominance and ocular-dominance columns;
- binocular disparity tuning;
- sensitive-period deprivation and amblyopia.

### Should know

- the distinction between eye of origin and visual hemifield;
- K layers lying between principal M and P layers;
- simple, complex and end-stopped cortical responses;
- blobs and interblobs;
- hypercolumn as an organisational model;
- cortical magnification of central vision;
- feedback and modulatory input to the LGN.

### Useful extension

- why a post-chiasmal lesion produces a homonymous field pattern;
- why binocular neurons are important for stereopsis;
- why early deprivation changes cortical allocation;
- why V1 is not simply an enlarged LGN.

## 3.1 From retina to the optic chiasm

Each optic nerve contains ganglion-cell axons from one eye.

At the **optic chiasm**:

- nasal retinal fibres cross;
- temporal retinal fibres remain on the same side.

Because the retinal image reverses left and right:

- the left visual field falls on the right halves of both retinas;
- the right visual field falls on the left halves of both retinas.

After the chiasm:

```text
right optic tract
→ left visual field from both eyes

left optic tract
→ right visual field from both eyes
```

### Eye of origin versus field of origin

An LGN receives signals from both eyes, but all signals within one LGN
represent the contralateral visual hemifield.

For example, the right LGN receives:

- uncrossed fibres from right temporal retina;
- crossed fibres from left nasal retina;
- both representing the left visual field.

### Common trap

**Wrong idea:** “The right optic tract carries information only from the right
eye.”

**Correction:** It carries the left visual hemifield represented by both eyes.

## 3.2 The lateral geniculate nucleus

The **lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN)** is a thalamic visual relay. The supplied
deck describes it as a kidney-shaped dorsal thalamic nucleus.

The six principal layers are numbered from 1 to 6.

- layers 1 and 2 are magnocellular;
- layers 3, 4, 5 and 6 are parvocellular;
- thin koniocellular zones lie between the principal layers.

## 3.3 Eye-specific LGN layers

The course gives this pattern:

```text
crossed nasal-retinal fibres
→ layers 1, 4 and 6

uncrossed temporal-retinal fibres
→ layers 2, 3 and 5
```

This keeps the two eyes anatomically separate at the level of the principal
LGN layers.

The LGN therefore simultaneously preserves:

- visual-field position;
- eye of origin;
- pathway type.

Signals from the two eyes become extensively integrated in cortex, but their
organised separation at earlier stages remains important.

## 3.4 M, P and K pathways

### Magnocellular pathway

Broad course features:

- relatively large cells;
- relatively large receptive fields;
- high temporal sensitivity;
- strong responses to luminance contrast and movement-related change;
- little classical colour opponency;
- lower fine spatial resolution than P pathways.

The major retinal input is from **parasol ganglion cells**.

### Parvocellular pathway

Broad course features:

- smaller cells;
- smaller receptive fields;
- high spatial resolution;
- sustained responses useful for fine form;
- strong red–green opponent signals in many cells;
- dense central representation.

The major retinal input is from **midget ganglion cells**.

### Koniocellular pathway

Broad course features:

- thin interlaminar zones;
- small and functionally varied cells;
- important short-wavelength colour-related input in several channels;
- projection to cortical regions including blobs.

A major retinal input is from **small bistratified ganglion cells**.

> **Course correction:** One line in the supplied LGN deck says midget RGC
> input forms the magnocellular pathway. The corrected relationship used
> throughout these notes is:
>
> ```text
> midget → parvocellular
> parasol → magnocellular
> small bistratified → koniocellular
> ```

## 3.5 Parallel does not mean isolated

M, P and K streams carry partly different emphases, but perception does not
result from three completely independent channels.

They interact in cortex. Motion can depend on form; object recognition can
depend on colour and contrast; a visually guided action can depend on fine
spatial detail.

Use the pathways to understand relative specialisation, not rigid exclusivity.

## 3.6 LGN receptive fields

Classical LGN receptive fields retain a concentric centre–surround
organisation similar to retinal ganglion cells.

Examples include:

- ON-centre/OFF-surround;
- OFF-centre/ON-surround;
- red–green opponency;
- blue–yellow opponency.

The course contrasts this with V1, where the preferred stimulus can be an
oriented line or edge.

> **Course qualification:** Centre–surround fields are the standard organising
> model. Avoid the absolute statement that no LGN cell can show any
> orientation-biased response under any condition.

## 3.7 The LGN is an active relay

Calling the LGN a relay does not mean it is a passive cable junction.

Its output is influenced by:

- retinal input;
- cortical feedback;
- brainstem state;
- attention and arousal-related modulation;
- local inhibitory circuits.

The supplied course focuses primarily on laminae and pathway identity, so these
notes keep modulation as supporting context rather than expanding into a full
thalamic physiology unit.

## 3.8 Primary visual cortex

**Primary visual cortex** is also called:

- V1;
- striate cortex;
- Brodmann area 17.

It lies mainly along the **calcarine sulcus** of the occipital lobe.

The term **striate** refers to the visible stripe of myelinated fibres associated
with V1.

## 3.9 V1 layers and LGN input

V1 has six cortical layers. The course emphasises layer 4 and its subdivisions.

Important input relationships:

```text
LGN M layers
→ V1 layer 4Cα

LGN P layers
→ V1 layer 4Cβ
```

Koniocellular input reaches superficial cortical compartments, including blobs,
through a more varied route.

This laminar organisation preserves stream identity initially while allowing
later cortical interaction.

## 3.10 Retinotopy

**Retinotopy** is the orderly mapping of neighbouring retinal or visual-field
locations onto neighbouring neural locations.

If two points are close in visual space, their early cortical representations
are generally close in V1.

Retinotopy is not a uniform scale map.

### Cortical magnification

The central visual field occupies disproportionately large cortical territory
because:

- foveal ganglion-cell density is high;
- central vision supports fine discrimination;
- many cortical neurons are allocated to small central regions.

This helps explain why a small foveal lesion can have a major effect on detailed
vision.

## 3.11 From circular fields to oriented fields

Retinal and LGN centre–surround fields can converge in a linear arrangement on
a cortical neuron.

A simplified model is:

```text
several aligned LGN receptive-field centres
→ converge on one V1 neuron
→ a bar crossing the aligned centres produces strong input
→ the neuron becomes orientation selective
```

The preferred orientation is the edge or bar angle producing the strongest
response.

The deck notes that a relatively small orientation change can markedly reduce a
cell’s response.

## 3.12 Orientation selectivity

An **orientation-selective** neuron prefers a line or edge at a particular
angle.

Examples:

- vertical;
- horizontal;
- oblique.

Orientation is different from direction.

```text
orientation
= angle of the line

direction
= way the line moves
```

A vertical bar can move left, right, up or down while retaining vertical
orientation.

## 3.13 Direction selectivity

A **direction-selective** neuron responds more strongly when a stimulus moves in
one direction than the opposite direction.

Example:

```text
vertical bar moving left → right
= strong response

same vertical bar moving right → left
= weak or absent response
```

### Course terminology qualification

The slide uses “direction columns” while discussing groups with the same
orientation preference.

The clearer term is **orientation columns** for cortical columns organised by
edge orientation. Direction preference is a related but distinct response
property.

## 3.14 Simple, complex and end-stopped cells

### Simple cells

A simple cell has spatially organised ON and OFF subregions and prefers an edge
of a particular orientation at a particular position.

### Complex cells

A complex cell also prefers orientation and often direction but is less tied to
one exact position within its receptive field.

### End-stopped cells

An end-stopped cell responds best to a bar, edge or corner of limited length and
can be inhibited when the stimulus extends beyond the preferred region.

The historical term **hypercomplex** appears in the course material.

> **Course qualification:** Modern teaching usually treats “hypercomplex” as
> the older name for end-stopped response properties rather than a wholly
> separate fundamental cell class.

## 3.15 Ocular dominance

Many V1 neurons receive binocular influence, but one eye may drive the neuron
more strongly.

**Ocular dominance** describes the relative preference for input from one eye or
both eyes.

Possible responses include:

- driven only by one eye;
- driven by either eye but dominated by one;
- driven similarly by both;
- especially responsive to binocular stimulation.

Ocular dominance is not the same as clinical sighting-eye dominance.

## 3.16 Ocular-dominance columns

Cells with similar eye preference are organised in vertically oriented cortical
groups called **ocular-dominance columns**.

Within layer 4C, left- and right-eye LGN input remains segregated into
alternating territories.

Later cortical circuits combine these signals for binocular processing.

## 3.17 Orientation columns and hypercolumns

Cells with similar orientation preference are also grouped.

A **hypercolumn** is a useful organisational model representing a small region
of visual space with:

- a range of orientation preferences;
- input from both eyes;
- related colour and feature compartments.

It is a conceptual map of local cortical processing rather than a rigid box
visible as one anatomical structure.

## 3.18 Blobs and interblobs

In layers 2 and 3, cytochrome-oxidase-rich clusters are called **blobs**. Areas
between them are called **interblobs**.

The course associates:

- blobs with colour-related processing and K input;
- interblob regions more with form/orientation relationships.

These are relative functional emphases, not absolute isolation of colour from
form.

## 3.19 Binocular disparity

The two eyes view the world from slightly different positions. A point not on
the fixation plane forms images at different horizontal retinal positions.

This difference is **binocular disparity**.

Some cortical neurons are tuned to:

- near/crossed disparity;
- far/uncrossed disparity;
- zero disparity.

### Course model

```text
crossed disparity
→ object nearer than fixation

uncrossed disparity
→ object farther than fixation

zero disparity
→ object at approximately the fixation distance
```

Disparity-selective neurons provide a neural basis for stereoscopic depth.

The current source block introduces disparity tuning but does not replace the
later full stereopsis practical.

## 3.20 Monocular deprivation and amblyopia

The course uses early monocular deprivation experiments to show that visual
cortex is not fully allocated at birth.

If one eye is deprived during a sensitive period:

```text
reduced patterned input from one eye
→ fewer effective cortical connections for that eye
→ dominance shifts toward the better-stimulated eye
→ persistent reduced visual function can develop
```

This provides a physiological model for **amblyopia**.

### Sensitive period

Early treatment is important because plasticity is greatest during development.
The deck also notes that some adult plasticity remains and that selected
amblyopia improvements can occur later.

The balanced lesson is:

- early intervention is strongly favoured;
- cortical plasticity does not become literally zero in adulthood;
- treatment expectations depend on cause, severity and individual context.

## 3.21 V1 lesions and visual fields

Because each V1 represents the opposite visual hemifield:

- left V1 damage threatens the right visual field;
- right V1 damage threatens the left visual field.

Bilateral severe V1 damage can cause cortical blindness.

A post-chiasmal field defect tends to be **homonymous** because corresponding
field information from both eyes travels together after the chiasm.

The exact field pattern depends on lesion location and extent.

## Worked example: tracing one point in the left visual field

A light appears left of fixation.

1. It falls on the right hemiretina of each eye.
2. In the left eye, this is nasal retina, so fibres cross.
3. In the right eye, this is temporal retina, so fibres remain uncrossed.
4. Both sets enter the right optic tract.
5. They relay in eye-specific layers of the right LGN.
6. They project through right optic radiations to right V1.
7. Higher right-hemisphere visual systems process the signal.

This explains why a right post-chiasmal lesion can affect the left field of both
eyes.

## Worked example: orientation versus direction

A neuron responds to a vertical bar moving rightward but not to:

- a horizontal bar moving rightward;
- a vertical bar moving leftward.

Interpretation:

- failure for the horizontal bar reflects orientation selectivity;
- failure for leftward motion reflects direction selectivity.

The same neuron can therefore encode more than one stimulus property.

## Common traps

### “Each LGN receives information from only one eye.”

Each LGN receives the contralateral visual hemifield from both eyes, while
keeping eye inputs in separate layers.

### “Nasal fibres remain uncrossed.”

Nasal retinal fibres cross; temporal retinal fibres remain uncrossed.

### “Midget cells form the magnocellular pathway.”

Midget cells are associated mainly with parvocellular layers.

### “M is motion only and P is colour only.”

The streams have relative specialisations and interact extensively.

### “LGN receptive fields are identical to V1 receptive fields.”

Classical LGN fields are centre–surround; many V1 neurons prefer oriented edges.

### “Orientation and direction are synonyms.”

Orientation is an edge angle; direction is motion trajectory.

### “Hypercomplex is a completely separate modern class.”

The more current term is end-stopped response.

### “Ocular dominance is the same as the dominant eye used for aiming.”

Cortical ocular dominance describes neuronal eye preference.

### “Adult cortex has no plasticity.”

Developmental plasticity is greatest early, but some plasticity persists.

# 4. Extrastriate Processing and Perceptual Disorders

## Big picture

V1 is an early cortical stage, not the final location of vision. Signals pass
into multiple extrastriate areas that represent increasingly complex
relationships.

The course frames the hierarchy as:

```text
V1
→ V2 and V3
→ specialised regions such as V4, MT/V5 and MST
→ temporal and parietal association systems
→ frontal systems for decisions, eye movements and action planning
```

Two broad interacting streams organise much of higher visual processing:

```text
ventral stream
→ form, object and identity
→ temporal cortex
→ vision for perception

dorsal stream
→ spatial relationships and visually guided action
→ parietal cortex
→ vision for action
```

Selective damage can therefore impair one visual operation while sparing
others.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- extrastriate cortex as visual cortex beyond V1;
- increasing representational complexity along the hierarchy;
- ventral and dorsal streams;
- object identity versus spatial/action guidance;
- interaction rather than rigid isolation of the streams;
- MT/V5 and motion processing;
- fusiform-region involvement in face recognition;
- cortical blindness;
- hemispatial neglect;
- akinetopsia;
- prosopagnosia;
- visual agnosia;
- image-forming versus non-image-forming pathways;
- clinical localisation from the failed visual operation.

### Should know

- V2, V3, V4, MT/V5 and MST as broad course landmarks;
- parietal, temporal and frontal progression;
- frontal eye fields and supplementary eye fields;
- optic ataxia as a defensible dorsal-stream example;
- audiovisual and multisensory integration;
- preserved pupil responses in cortical blindness.

### Useful extension

- feedforward and feedback interaction;
- why recognition can be category selective;
- why neglect is an attentional disorder rather than a simple retinal field
  defect;
- how higher visual complaints should be separated from ocular blur or language
  disorders.

## 4.1 What “extrastriate” means

**Extrastriate visual cortex** refers to visual areas beyond striate cortex
(V1).

The course identifies a progression through:

- V2;
- V3;
- V4;
- V5/MT;
- MST;
- additional parietal, temporal and frontal visual areas.

The exact numbering and boundaries are more complex than a single linear chain.
For this module, the durable principle is distributed specialisation with
extensive interconnection.

## 4.2 Hierarchical processing

As signals move through the system, receptive fields tend to become larger and
the represented features more complex.

A simplified hierarchy is:

```text
local contrast and edge
→ contour and surface relationships
→ colour, motion and depth relationships
→ object, face or spatial representation
→ decision and action
```

Higher areas combine information over wider portions of the visual field and
with:

- memory;
- attention;
- body position;
- motor plans;
- other sensory systems.

“Higher” does not mean that information only travels one way. Feedback from
later areas influences earlier processing.

## 4.3 From sensation toward action

The supplied extrastriate deck describes a broad posterior-to-anterior
progression:

```text
posterior sensory regions
→ association regions
→ frontal decision and motor-planning regions
```

Visual signals can guide:

- recognising an object;
- choosing where to look;
- reaching and grasping;
- navigating;
- reading another person’s movement;
- avoiding an approaching vehicle.

This is why vision cannot be understood as conscious picture formation alone.

## 4.4 The ventral stream

The **ventral visual stream** projects broadly from occipital visual areas
toward inferior temporal cortex.

It supports:

- object form;
- identity;
- detailed shape;
- colour–form relationships;
- face and category recognition;
- “what is it?” judgements.

The course labels this **vision for perception** or the “what” pathway.

### A ventral-stream computation

```text
edges and colour patches
→ combined into contours and surfaces
→ matched to stored object representations
→ object identity becomes available
```

A person can therefore see lines and colours yet fail to identify the object if
higher ventral processing is disrupted.

## 4.5 The dorsal stream

The **dorsal visual stream** projects broadly from occipital areas toward
posterior parietal cortex.

It supports:

- spatial location;
- movement relationships;
- visually guided reaching and grasping;
- eye and body orientation;
- navigation;
- “where is it?” and “how do I act toward it?” operations.

The course labels this **vision for action** or the “where” pathway. “Where/how”
is often a more useful description because dorsal processing is closely linked
to action.

### A dorsal-stream computation

```text
target position and motion
+ gaze and body coordinates
→ spatial transformation
→ hand or eye movement plan
```

## 4.6 The streams interact

The two-stream model is a framework, not a pair of sealed tubes.

To pick up a cup:

- ventral processing helps identify it as a cup;
- dorsal processing estimates position, orientation and movement;
- frontal and motor systems select and execute the action;
- feedback updates the plan.

The supplied deck explicitly notes constant interaction.

### Common trap

**Wrong idea:** “The ventral stream never processes spatial information, and the
dorsal stream never uses object form.”

**Correction:** The streams show relative specialisation while sharing and
exchanging information.

## 4.7 V2 and V3

V2 receives strong V1 input and distributes information to several later
pathways. V3 is associated broadly with form, spatial and motion relationships
depending on subdivision.

The course does not require a complete atlas of every subdivision. Learn them
as intermediate extrastriate stages between V1 and more specialised networks.

## 4.8 V4 and colour/form

V4 is commonly associated with colour and form processing.

A useful course-level relationship is:

```text
cone-opponent retinal/LGN signals
→ V1 colour compartments
→ V2/V4 and related ventral areas
→ colour constancy and object-related colour representation
```

The current source block introduces higher colour processing but does not
replace the later dedicated colour-perception and colour-testing lectures.

## 4.9 MT/V5 and motion

The middle temporal area, **MT/V5**, has a major role in visual motion
processing.

It receives motion-related input through multiple routes and represents:

- direction;
- speed;
- coherent movement;
- local-to-global motion relationships.

MST and related areas contribute to more complex motion patterns, including
optic-flow relationships important for self-movement.

### Course boundary

The present notes explain the cortical motion relationship needed for the
extrastriate disorder section. They do not silently expand into the full later
motion-perception block listed in the timetable.

## 4.10 Frontal eye fields and supplementary eye fields

The course identifies frontal visual-motor areas such as:

- **frontal eye fields (FEF)**;
- **supplementary eye fields (SEF)**.

These areas contribute to:

- selecting a saccadic target;
- voluntary eye movements;
- sequencing gaze;
- linking visual attention to motor planning.

They demonstrate the progression from sensory representation to organised
behaviour.

## 4.11 Multisensory integration

Higher association cortex can combine visual information with:

- sound;
- touch;
- vestibular input;
- language;
- memory.

Seeing a speaker’s lips and hearing speech is an audiovisual event. Normal
perception uses both streams and multiple senses rather than processing each in
complete isolation.

## 4.12 Cortical blindness

**Cortical blindness** is severe loss of conscious vision from bilateral
occipital visual-cortex damage.

The course emphasises a characteristic dissociation:

```text
visual acuity response may be no light perception
but
direct and consensual pupillary light responses can remain intact
```

Reason:

```text
retina and pretectal pupil pathway remain functional
while
bilateral visual cortex is damaged
```

There should be no relative afferent pupillary defect if the retinal and
pretectal afferent pathways are symmetric and intact.

### Clinical localisation

Preserved pupil responses do not prove that the patient is consciously seeing.
They prove that part of the subcortical light-response pathway is functioning.

## 4.13 Hemispatial neglect

**Hemispatial neglect** is failure to attend or respond adequately to one side
of space, commonly after right parietal network damage producing left-sided
neglect.

The neglected side may affect:

- visual search;
- drawing;
- reading;
- grooming;
- eating;
- awareness of the body.

Neglect is not simply the same as hemianopia.

### Field loss versus neglect

| Feature | Homonymous hemianopia | Hemispatial neglect |
|---|---|---|
| Main problem | missing visual-field representation | failure of spatial attention/awareness |
| Typical pathway | post-chiasmal visual pathway | often parietal attentional network |
| Search behaviour | may compensate by scanning | may fail to orient to neglected side |
| Other modalities | mainly visual field | can involve tactile, auditory and body-space awareness |
| Awareness | often recognised | may include reduced awareness of the deficit |

The two can coexist, so bedside behaviour and formal field testing are both
needed.

## 4.14 Akinetopsia

**Akinetopsia** means severe impairment of visual motion perception and is also
called motion blindness.

The course links it to MT/V5 damage.

Reported difficulties can include:

- perceiving moving objects as a sequence of static positions;
- judging when a cup is full while liquid is poured;
- reading lip movement;
- judging approaching traffic.

Akinetopsia is not ordinary blur from a moving target. The central problem is
the cortical representation of motion.

## 4.15 Prosopagnosia

**Prosopagnosia** is a disproportionate difficulty recognising familiar faces.

The course links it to damage involving the fusiform region within the ventral
stream.

A person may still be able to:

- see that a stimulus is a face;
- describe facial features;
- recognise the person by voice, clothing or context.

The deficit concerns identity from facial information, not necessarily all
object recognition.

### Common trap

**Wrong idea:** “Prosopagnosia means the patient cannot see eyes, nose or
mouth.”

**Correction:** Visual features may be seen, but their configuration does not
reliably activate familiar identity.

## 4.16 Visual agnosia

**Visual agnosia** is failure to recognise or assign meaning to visual stimuli
despite sufficient early vision for the task and without the deficit being
explained solely by language, memory or general intellectual failure.

The broad course distinction is:

```text
object is detected
→ features may be described
→ meaningful recognition fails
```

Different agnosias can affect objects, faces, words or other categories, but the
source block focuses on the general localisation principle rather than a full
taxonomy.

## 4.17 Optic ataxia

**Optic ataxia** is a useful dorsal-stream clinical extension: the patient has
difficulty using vision to guide reaching, especially in parts of the visual
field, despite being able to recognise the object.

It illustrates:

```text
object identity preserved
but
visual coordinates are not transformed effectively into action
```

This extension supports the course’s vision-for-action framework.

## 4.18 Reading difficulties and visual stress

The supplied deck lists visual dyslexia, attentional difficulties and
Meares–Irlen/visual-stress claims among perceptual phenomena.

These topics require careful wording.

- Reading is a distributed skill involving vision, language, attention and
  learned symbol processing.
- Ocular blur, binocular difficulty or visual discomfort can interfere with
  reading performance.
- A visual complaint should be assessed rather than dismissed.
- The source block does not establish that a single visual-stress mechanism
  explains most developmental dyslexia or that one coloured-filter treatment is
  universally causal or effective.

> **Course qualification:** Learn the reported phenomena and the need for
> differential assessment. Do not convert the slide list into an unsupported
> claim that dyslexia is primarily an ocular disorder.

## 4.19 Charles Bonnet-type visual hallucinations

The older overview notes visual hallucinations in some people with severe visual
impairment.

The important distinction is:

```text
reduced visual input
→ spontaneous or released visual imagery may occur
→ insight can be preserved
```

Such hallucinations should not automatically be equated with a primary
psychiatric disorder. The source does not provide a full diagnostic protocol,
so the note retains this only as a clinical distinction.

## 4.20 Clinical localisation by failed operation

A useful approach is to ask what the patient can and cannot do.

### Motion failure

- sees stationary form;
- cannot judge smooth movement;
- consider motion-processing network.

### Face-identity failure

- sees facial components;
- cannot recognise familiar face;
- consider ventral/fusiform network.

### Spatial-attention failure

- ignores one side;
- may not compensate;
- consider parietal attention network.

### Visually guided reaching failure

- recognises object;
- misreaches under visual guidance;
- consider dorsal visuomotor network.

### Conscious visual loss with intact pupils

- consider bilateral cortical visual damage after ocular and afferent pathway
  assessment.

## Worked example: cup recognition versus cup use

### Patient A

The patient recognises a cup but reaches beside it and rotates the hand
incorrectly.

Likely failed operation:

```text
dorsal visuomotor transformation
```

### Patient B

The patient reaches accurately toward the object but cannot identify it as a
cup from vision.

Likely failed operation:

```text
ventral object recognition
```

The dissociation supports specialised but interacting higher visual systems.

## Worked example: darkness on the left side

A patient consistently leaves food on the left side of the plate and draws only
the right half of a clock. Basic ocular examination is adequate.

Reasoning:

1. The behaviour is organised around one side of space.
2. It affects attention to multiple objects rather than one retinal location.
3. The patient may show little awareness of the omission.
4. Right parietal attentional dysfunction and left hemispatial neglect become
   important considerations.
5. Formal visual-field testing is still needed because hemianopia can coexist.

## Common traps

### “V1 is where all visual perception is completed.”

V1 is an early cortical stage whose output feeds distributed higher systems.

### “The dorsal stream is only ‘where,’ and the ventral stream is only ‘what.’”

Dorsal processing strongly supports action, and both streams interact.

### “Akinetopsia is simply low visual acuity.”

It is a selective cortical motion-perception disorder.

### “Prosopagnosia means all objects are unrecognisable.”

It can be disproportionately face specific.

### “Neglect and hemianopia are identical.”

One is primarily attentional/awareness failure; the other is a visual-field
pathway defect.

### “Normal pupils prove normal vision.”

Subcortical pupil pathways can be intact in cortical blindness.

### “Visual agnosia can be diagnosed whenever a blurred patient cannot name an
object.”

Adequate sensory input and alternative explanations must first be considered.

### “The course slide proves that visual stress causes developmental dyslexia.”

The source list raises a topic; it does not establish that broad causal claim.

## Key terms

- **Extrastriate cortex** — visual cortex beyond V1.
- **Visual hierarchy** — progression toward larger fields and more complex
  representations.
- **Ventral stream** — occipitotemporal pathway supporting object and identity
  perception.
- **Dorsal stream** — occipitoparietal pathway supporting spatial guidance and
  action.
- **V2/V3** — intermediate extrastriate visual areas.
- **V4** — extrastriate region associated broadly with colour and form.
- **MT/V5** — motion-sensitive extrastriate region.
- **MST** — higher motion-related region associated with complex flow.
- **Frontal eye field** — frontal region supporting voluntary saccade selection.
- **Supplementary eye field** — medial frontal region contributing to
  internally organised eye-movement sequences.
- **Cortical blindness** — loss of conscious vision from bilateral visual-cortex
  damage.
- **Hemispatial neglect** — failure to attend adequately to one side of space.
- **Akinetopsia** — severe impairment of visual motion perception.
- **Prosopagnosia** — impaired recognition of familiar faces.
- **Visual agnosia** — failure to recognise visual stimuli despite adequate
  elementary vision for the task.
- **Optic ataxia** — impaired visually guided reaching.
- **Multisensory integration** — combination of information from different
  senses.
- **Photoentrainment** — alignment of circadian rhythm to environmental light.
- **Pretectal pathway** — subcortical route involved in pupillary light
  responses.

## Memory hook

Two streams: **T-P**

```text
Temporal = Tell me what it is
Parietal = Point and act toward it
```

Disorder localisation: **M-F-S-A**

```text
Motion → MT/V5
Face → fusiform/ventral
Space → parietal attention
Action guidance → dorsal visuomotor
```

## One-minute summary

- Visual processing continues from V1 into distributed extrastriate and
  association cortex.
- Higher areas represent increasingly complex relationships and interact with
  attention, memory and action systems.
- The ventral stream supports form, object and identity processing toward
  temporal cortex.
- The dorsal stream supports spatial representation and visually guided action
  toward parietal cortex.
- The streams are specialised but continuously interacting.
- MT/V5 is strongly associated with motion processing; V4 with colour/form
  relationships.
- FEF and SEF link visual selection with eye-movement planning.
- Bilateral V1 damage can cause cortical blindness with preserved pupillary
  responses.
- Hemispatial neglect is an attentional disorder and is not identical to
  hemianopia.
- Akinetopsia, prosopagnosia, visual agnosia and optic ataxia illustrate
  selective higher visual failures.
- Reading complaints require differential assessment; the supplied slides do
  not establish one visual cause for most developmental dyslexia.
- Later full depth, colour, motion and entoptic blocks remain outside the
  complete scope of the supplied source set.
