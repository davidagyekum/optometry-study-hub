## How to use these notes

Environmental Vision is not simply a collection of facts about light, computers,
injuries, and protective equipment. It asks one central question:

> **How do the worker, the visual task, and the environment interact to produce
> safe and effective visual performance?**

Read each section in three passes:

1. **Understand the model.** Follow the explanation and mechanism.
2. **Learn the distinctions.** Use the tables and highlighted terms.
3. **Retrieve without looking.** Answer the checkpoint questions aloud.

The labels mean:

- **Must know:** central knowledge expected in almost every application.
- **Should know:** important supporting detail.
- **Useful extension:** helpful for deeper understanding but less central.

# 1. Physical Optics and Visual Function

## Big picture

Light is the part of the physical environment that makes vision possible, but
not every wavelength interacts with the eye in the same way. The wavelength
determines three important things:

1. the **energy** carried by each photon;
2. the **ocular tissue** that absorbs the radiation;
3. whether the result is useful vision, a thermal effect, or tissue injury.

Physical optics also explains why the eye has limits. Interference, diffraction,
aberrations, polarization, contrast, and the geometry of two-eye viewing all
shape what can be seen.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- the order and approximate wavelength ranges of the optical-radiation bands;
- shorter wavelength means greater photon energy;
- which ocular structures absorb ultraviolet, visible, and infrared radiation;
- why a very small pupil increases diffraction;
- the meaning of resolving power;
- why polarized lenses reduce reflected glare;
- the difference between colour discrimination and stereopsis.

### Should know

- common clinical colour-vision and stereoacuity tests;
- fluorescence and its application in fluorescein angiography;
- the trade-off between diffraction at small pupils and aberration at large
  pupils.

### Useful extension

- how aphakia or an ultraviolet-transparent intraocular lens changes the
  wavelengths that can reach the retina;
- why some multifocal intraocular lenses intentionally use diffraction.

## 1.1 Light and the optical spectrum

The lecture defines **light** as energy to which the human eye is sensitive.
This definition is useful clinically because it emphasizes the biological
response rather than treating light as an abstract wave only.

**Optical radiation** occupies the region of the electromagnetic spectrum
between X-rays and microwaves. For study purposes, it is divided into
ultraviolet, visible, and infrared wavebands.

| Band | Course wavelength range | Main study idea |
|---|---:|---|
| UV-C | approximately 100/200–280 nm | high-energy ultraviolet; absorbed very anteriorly |
| UV-B | 280–315 nm | mainly absorbed by cornea and anterior tissues |
| UV-A | 315–400 nm | strongly absorbed by the crystalline lens |
| Visible | approximately 400–780 nm | stimulates retinal photoreceptors |
| IR-A | 780–1400 nm | near infrared; a substantial part can reach deeper ocular tissues |
| IR-B | 1400–3000 nm | increasing anterior absorption and thermal effect |
| IR-C | 3000 nm upward | strongly absorbed by anterior tissues; thermal hazard |

> **Course qualification:** One Physical Optics slide begins UV-C at 200 nm,
> while the Eye Hazards deck uses 100–280 nm. Learn the boundaries as
> conventional approximate bands, not as perfectly sharp biological walls.

### The wavelength–energy relationship

For electromagnetic radiation:

> **The shorter the wavelength, the greater the energy of each photon.**

This is why ultraviolet radiation is associated mainly with **photochemical**
injury, whereas infrared exposure is associated mainly with **thermal** injury.
The tissue effect still depends on intensity, exposure duration, focusing, and
which structure absorbs the wavelength.

### Memory hook

**Short = strong photon. Long = lower photon energy, often more thermal.**

This is a cue, not a complete safety rule. A very intense visible or infrared
source can still cause severe damage.

## 1.2 Where optical radiation is absorbed

The eye behaves like a set of wavelength-selective filters.

### Ultraviolet

- **UV-C and much of UV-B** are absorbed by the cornea and conjunctiva.
- **UV-A** is strongly absorbed by the crystalline lens.
- In the normal phakic eye, the lens reduces the amount of near-ultraviolet
  radiation reaching the retina.

### Visible and near infrared

Wavelengths in roughly the **400–1400 nm** range pass through the ocular media
more effectively. Visible wavelengths stimulate the photoreceptors. Near
infrared does not produce ordinary visual sensation but may cause thermal
effects.

The refractive system of the eye focuses transmitted radiation. Focusing is
beneficial for vision, but it can concentrate hazardous radiation on a small
retinal area. This explains how intense sunlight, an eclipse, a laser, or a
strong industrial source may produce retinal damage.

### Aphakia and pseudophakia

The source notes explain that retinal photoreceptors may respond to some
near-ultraviolet wavelengths if those wavelengths reach the retina. In a normal
phakic eye, the crystalline lens absorbs much of this radiation. After lens
removal, or with an implant that provides inadequate ultraviolet filtration,
the person may perceive unusual blue-violet sensations and the retina may
receive more ultraviolet exposure.

### Cause–effect chain

```text
Wavelength
→ determines tissue transmission and absorption
→ determines where energy is deposited
→ produces photochemical or thermal change
→ produces the clinical pattern of injury
```

## 1.3 Colour vision

The colour of an object depends on the wavelengths it emits or reflects.
Human colour perception is based on three cone populations with peak
sensitivities in the short-, medium-, and long-wavelength regions.

- **Short-wavelength cone system:** commonly associated with blue.
- **Medium-wavelength cone system:** commonly associated with green.
- **Long-wavelength cone system:** commonly associated with red.

These labels are convenient but simplified. A perceived colour normally depends
on the relative responses across cone classes, not the activity of one cone
type in isolation.

### Congenital defects

| Term | Meaning in the course |
|---|---|
| Protanopia | absent red/long-wavelength cone function |
| Deuteranopia | absent green/medium-wavelength cone function |
| Tritanopia | absent blue/short-wavelength cone function |
| Protanomaly | shifted or abnormal long-wavelength sensitivity |
| Deuteranomaly | shifted or abnormal medium-wavelength sensitivity |
| Tritanomaly | shifted or abnormal short-wavelength sensitivity |

The suffix helps:

- **-anopia:** a cone-function class is absent.
- **-anomaly:** the spectral sensitivity is shifted or abnormal.

### Acquired colour defects

Colour loss can also be acquired through retinal or optic-nerve disease. The
important distinction is that an acquired defect may reflect active ocular
pathology rather than a lifelong inherited variation.

### Tests listed in the lecture

- Ishihara pseudoisochromatic plates;
- Farnsworth–Munsell 100 Hue test;
- D-15 test;
- Lanthony New Colour Test.

Do not treat all colour tests as interchangeable. They differ in purpose,
difficulty, and the type of error pattern they reveal.

## 1.4 Fluorescence

**Fluorescence** is the emission of light at a longer wavelength after a molecule
has been stimulated by shorter-wavelength light.

The energy of the emitted photon is lower than the energy absorbed; therefore,
the emitted wavelength is longer.

### Clinical application

In **fluorescein angiography**, fluorescein is administered systemically and its
passage through the retinal and choroidal circulation is photographed. The
principle connects physical optics to vascular assessment: excite the dye with
one wavelength range and detect its longer-wavelength emission.

## 1.5 Interference

**Interference** occurs when two waves superpose.

- **Constructive interference:** the waves reinforce one another and produce a
  greater resultant amplitude.
- **Destructive interference:** the waves oppose one another and reduce the
  resultant amplitude.

Interference helps explain optical coatings and wave-based optical design. The
essential idea is not simply “waves meet”; it is that the phase relationship
determines the resultant amplitude.

## 1.6 Diffraction and pupil size

**Diffraction** is the spreading or bending of light as it passes an edge or a
small aperture.

Diffraction becomes more important as the aperture becomes smaller. Therefore,
when the pupil is very small, diffraction limits image sharpness.

A larger pupil reduces diffraction but exposes more of the peripheral optical
system, increasing aberrations. The eye therefore faces a trade-off:

| Pupil condition | Advantage | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Very small | reduces some aberrations; increases depth of focus | diffraction becomes prominent |
| Moderate | balances diffraction and aberration | usually best compromise |
| Very large | less diffraction | more aberration and stray light |

### Common trap

**Wrong idea:** “The smaller the pupil, the sharper the image without limit.”

**Correction:** A moderately smaller pupil may improve optical quality, but a
very small pupil increases diffraction and eventually reduces resolution.

Some multifocal intraocular-lens designs deliberately use diffraction to divide
light into more than one focus. In that setting diffraction is an engineered
feature rather than merely an optical error.

## 1.7 Resolving power

**Resolving power** is the ability of an optical system to form two discernible
images from two close points.

The **limit of resolution** is the smallest angular separation at which the
points can still be distinguished. In clinical practice, visual acuity and
contrast sensitivity provide different views of the patient’s resolving
performance.

A person may read high-contrast letters well yet struggle with low-contrast
detail. Therefore, acuity alone does not completely describe functional vision.

## 1.8 Polarization

Ordinary light vibrates in many planes. A polarizing material preferentially
transmits vibration in one plane, producing **polarized light**.

Because horizontal surfaces such as water and wet roads produce strongly
polarized reflected glare, polarized sunglasses can selectively reduce much of
that reflection.

### Clinical and occupational uses

- reducing glare from water or wet roads;
- improving comfort during outdoor work or driving;
- dissociating the two eyes in some binocular-vision tests, such as the Titmus
  test.

### Common trap

**Wrong idea:** A polarizer changes the colour spectrum in the same way as a
coloured filter.

**Correction:** A polarizer primarily selects the plane of vibration and reduces
radiant intensity; its defining action is not selective wavelength removal.

## 1.9 Stereoscopic vision

**Stereopsis** is depth perception produced by combining slightly dissimilar
images from the two eyes. The images stimulate corresponding or near-
corresponding retinal areas within **Panum’s fusional area**, allowing one
single percept with depth.

Fine stereoacuity is greatest when the relevant images fall on the macular
regions, where spatial resolution is best.

### Tests listed in the lecture

- Titmus test;
- TNO test;
- Frisby test;
- Lang stereotest.

### Distinguish

| Concept | Core question |
|---|---|
| Visual acuity | How fine a detail can one eye or both eyes resolve? |
| Colour vision | Can wavelength-related differences be discriminated? |
| Stereopsis | Can binocular disparity be converted into perceived depth? |

## Key terms

- **Optical radiation:** ultraviolet, visible, and infrared radiation relevant
  to ocular transmission and biological effect.
- **Photon:** a quantum of electromagnetic energy.
- **Diffraction:** spreading of light at an edge or aperture.
- **Interference:** combination of waves to form a resultant amplitude.
- **Resolving power:** ability to distinguish close points as separate.
- **Polarization:** restriction of light vibration to a particular plane.
- **Fluorescence:** longer-wavelength emission following shorter-wavelength
  excitation.
- **Stereopsis:** depth perception from binocular retinal disparity.
- **Aphakia:** absence of the crystalline lens.
- **Pseudophakia:** replacement of the crystalline lens by an intraocular lens.

## One-minute summary

Wavelength controls photon energy and ocular absorption. UV is absorbed mainly
by anterior tissues and the lens; visible and near-IR penetrate more deeply.
Small apertures increase diffraction, large pupils increase aberrations, and
resolution depends on more than high-contrast acuity. Polarization reduces
reflected glare, fluorescence supports angiography, colour depends on cone
comparisons, and stereopsis depends on binocular disparity.

## Active recall checkpoint

1. Why does UV-B usually injure a different ocular structure from near infrared?
2. Why does a pinhole stop improving clarity once it becomes extremely small?
3. Distinguish protanopia from protanomaly.
4. Explain why polarized sunglasses are especially useful over water.
5. What is the difference between resolving power and stereopsis?

<details>
<summary>Reveal answers</summary>

1. The wavelengths differ in ocular transmission and absorption, so their energy
   is deposited in different tissues.
2. A very small aperture increases diffraction, which spreads the retinal image.
3. Protanopia is absence of long-wavelength cone function; protanomaly is an
   abnormal or shifted long-wavelength sensitivity.
4. Horizontal reflected glare is strongly polarized, so a suitably oriented
   polarizer selectively attenuates it.
5. Resolving power concerns separating close spatial detail; stereopsis concerns
   deriving depth from binocular disparity.

</details>

# 2. Visual Task Analysis

## Big picture

A visual task analysis asks whether a person can perform a specific job safely,
comfortably, and efficiently under the actual conditions in which the job is
done.

It is not enough to measure acuity in a clinic and declare a worker “fit” or
“unfit.” The analysis must connect:

```text
the worker
+ the critical detail of the task
+ the working environment
+ the consequence of error
```

The goal is to match the visual demands of the job to the worker and to remove
correctable environmental barriers.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- the purpose of a workplace walk-through survey;
- the meaning of **critical detail** and **working distance**;
- the nine major visibility factors;
- the relationship among object size, distance, retinal image size,
  accommodation, and convergence;
- why visual-task analysis must occur before screening requirements are set.

### Should know

- far, intermediate/near, and very-near working-distance categories;
- static acuity versus vernier acuity;
- the contributions of motivation, general health, attention, and time.

### Useful extension

- how multidisciplinary teams contribute to a full job analysis;
- why two physically different tasks can require similar acuity but different
  accommodation and convergence.

## 2.1 Why analyse the task?

The lecture repeatedly links task analysis to three outcomes:

> **Efficiency, productivity, and safety.**

A good analysis helps the employer place a suitably prepared person in a
position where the task can be performed effectively. It also identifies
conditions that should be changed rather than blaming the worker’s eyes for an
environmental problem.

### Example: crane operator versus radio assembler

A crane operator may need to monitor a restricted area 18–30 metres away. A
radio assembler may need to inspect tiny parts and wires at a very short
distance.

Both jobs require vision, but not the same vision:

- the crane operator emphasizes distance resolution, scanning, field awareness,
  and movement;
- the assembler emphasizes near detail, accommodation, convergence, posture,
  illumination, and sustained viewing.

This is a **qualitative** difference in the type of skill and a **quantitative**
difference in the level of that skill.

## 2.2 The workplace survey

The analysis should ideally be performed at the workplace. A walk-through
survey identifies the job stages, the smallest critical detail, sources of
visual difficulty, and sources of injury.

### Six basic hazard categories in the task-analysis deck

1. impact;
2. heat;
3. chemical exposure;
4. dust;
5. optical radiation;
6. contusion.

The analyst should also identify motion, high temperatures, electrical hazards,
layout, co-worker position, and ergonomic stress.

### Practical survey sequence

1. **Observe the complete job**, not only the worker’s final station.
2. **Speak with the worker and supervisor** to learn what errors actually occur.
3. **Identify the critical detail** that determines success or safety.
4. **Measure working distances** and note whether they change.
5. **Record visibility conditions:** illumination, contrast, glare, colour,
   atmosphere, movement, and viewing time.
6. **Identify hazards and consequences of error.**
7. **Assess the worker’s relevant visual capabilities.**
8. **Separate person-related limitations from environment-related limitations.**
9. **Recommend controls, task changes, optical correction, training, or referral.**

## 2.3 Critical detail

The **critical detail** is the smallest feature that must be seen or
discriminated to perform the task correctly.

Examples include:

- a hairline mark on a gauge;
- the edge of a fine wire;
- a colour-coded resistor band;
- a pedestrian at a distance;
- the alignment of two precision parts.

The critical detail determines the visual requirement more accurately than the
general job title.

## 2.4 The visibility model

The lecture lists nine major factors that influence visibility:

1. size of the task;
2. distance of the task;
3. illumination;
4. glare;
5. contrast;
6. colour;
7. time available to view;
8. movement;
9. atmospheric conditions.

These factors interact. A smaller target can sometimes be made more visible by
bringing it closer, magnifying it, improving contrast, increasing appropriate
illumination, reducing glare, slowing the task, or clearing the viewing path.

### Cause–effect model

```text
Poor visibility
→ more visual effort and slower recognition
→ more errors or compensatory posture
→ discomfort, reduced productivity, or injury risk
```

## 2.5 Size, distance, and retinal image

The angular size of a target depends on both its physical size and its distance
from the eye. A small nearby object and a much larger distant object may form
similar retinal image sizes.

However, they do not create the same near-vision demand. Bringing a very small
object close enlarges its retinal image but increases accommodation and
convergence.

### Working-distance categories in the lecture

| Category | Approximate distance | Examples |
|---|---:|---|
| Far | more than 2 m | driving, flying, crane operation |
| Intermediate to near | 30 cm to 2 m | office work, display use, lathe operation |
| Very near | less than 30 cm | sewing, watch repair, microelectronics |

### Common trap

**Wrong idea:** If two tasks require the same acuity, they have the same visual
demand.

**Correction:** Similar angular detail can occur at different distances, but the
nearer task imposes greater accommodation and convergence and may produce
different postural demands.

## 2.6 Accommodation, convergence, and age

**Accommodation** changes the optical focus for near objects.
**Convergence** turns the eyes inward so the images remain single.

The amount of accommodation available reduces with age. A worker may therefore
need an appropriate near addition, different working distance, larger detail,
or more task illumination.

A prescription should match the actual task distance. A generic reading
addition may not be optimal for an intermediate display or an unusually close
precision task.

## 2.7 Static and vernier acuity

**Static visual acuity** is the ability to distinguish detail in a stationary
object.

**Vernier acuity** is the ability to detect a small break in alignment or
contour. It matters in tasks such as reading precision gauges or aligning
components.

A person can have adequate letter acuity yet struggle with a task whose critical
information is alignment rather than letter identification.

## 2.8 Illumination

Appropriate illumination improves recognition, but the benefit depends on how
visually difficult the task is and how much visual performance limits the whole
job.

Illumination has a larger effect when:

- the critical detail is small;
- contrast is low;
- accuracy is important;
- the worker is older or visually limited;
- the visual component determines the speed of the job.

Simply increasing light without controlling glare can worsen visibility.

## 2.9 Glare and reflection

Glare can reduce comfort, reduce contrast, or both.

A bright luminaire reflected from a glossy surface may superimpose light over
the task, reducing edge contrast. Visual discomfort also accumulates during the
day.

Useful controls include:

- repositioning the display relative to windows;
- blinds or window films;
- indirect lighting;
- shielding and louvers;
- dull, textured surfaces;
- lower ambient illumination with adjustable task lighting;
- task rotation when better engineering control is temporarily impossible.

## 2.10 Contrast and luminance

The visual system detects an object largely through differences at its edges.
**Contrast** is the difference that makes the object distinguishable from its
background.

When contrast is low, increasing appropriate illumination or changing the
background can improve recognition. If an error would be costly or dangerous,
the visibility margin should be increased.

Luminance helps only to a point. Beyond an optimum, additional brightness may
produce glare and reduce performance.

## 2.11 Colour

Colour discrimination is influenced by illumination and age. The lecture notes
that hue errors, particularly in some blue-green and red regions, may increase
with age.

A colour-dependent job should therefore be evaluated under the actual lamp
spectrum, illuminance, surface colours, and viewing time.

## 2.12 Time, movement, and atmosphere

A target is harder to recognize when:

- exposure time is short;
- it is moving;
- the observer is also moving;
- dust, smoke, fog, or steam absorbs and scatters light.

The visual system receives enormous sensory input, but performance is also
limited by attention, processing, decision-making, and motor response.
Therefore, increasing target exposure time can improve performance even when
the eye’s optical resolution is unchanged.

## Worked example

A worker repeatedly misreads a small moving label on a conveyor.

A poor analysis says: “The worker needs stronger glasses.”

A proper analysis asks:

- Is the label the true critical detail?
- What angular size does it subtend?
- Is the conveyor too fast?
- Is the contrast adequate?
- Is reflected glare masking the print?
- Is the illumination spectrum suitable?
- Is the viewing angle or posture poor?
- Does the worker’s correction match the distance?

The final intervention might be larger print, slower inspection speed, a matte
label, repositioned lighting, task magnification, or an optical correction—or a
combination.

## Key terms

- **Visual task analysis:** systematic evaluation of a job’s visual demands,
  viewing conditions, hazards, and worker requirements.
- **Critical detail:** smallest feature that determines successful performance.
- **Working distance:** eye-to-task distance.
- **Static acuity:** resolution for a stationary target.
- **Vernier acuity:** ability to detect small misalignment.
- **Contrast:** luminance or colour difference that makes an object visible.
- **Illuminance:** light arriving at a surface.
- **Glare:** brightness that causes discomfort or reduces visibility.

## Memory hook

Use **S-D-I-G-C-T-M-A**:

```text
Size
Distance
Illumination
Glare
Contrast
Time
Movement
Atmosphere
```

Add **Colour** between contrast and time when recalling the full lecture list.

## One-minute summary

Visual task analysis starts with the task, not with a generic eye test.
Identify the critical detail, measure its distance and visibility, analyse the
environment and hazards, then match the worker and controls to the real demand.
Size, distance, illumination, glare, contrast, colour, time, movement, and
atmosphere act together.

<details>

2. The critical detail is the smallest feature that must be seen to perform the
   task correctly.
3. It enlarges the retinal image but increases accommodation and convergence
   demand.
4. Static acuity resolves stationary form; vernier acuity detects alignment or a
   break in contour.
5. Examples: improve task illumination, reduce reflected glare, change the

</details>

# 3. Visual Ergonomics

## Big picture

**Ergonomics** means adapting the work environment to the worker’s capabilities
and limitations. It is not primarily the process of forcing the worker to adopt
one rigid “correct” posture.

Visual ergonomics links the eyes, head, neck, shoulders, hands, task material,
display, furniture, and lighting. A change that helps one component may harm
another, so the workstation must be evaluated as a system.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- why viewing distance affects accommodation and convergence;
- why a slightly lower display may reduce exposed ocular surface and visual
  strain;
- why the display should generally tilt away at the top rather than toward the
  user;
- how glare and reflected contrast impair screen visibility;
- why posture variation is better than one fixed ideal posture.

### Should know

- when screen and document distance should be similar;
- why dark text on a light background may improve performance in a normally
  lit office;
- the role of adjustable task lighting.

### Useful extension

- the lecture’s numerical monitor-placement guidance;
- how presbyopia changes display and document setup.

## 3.1 Course monitor-placement guidance

The lecture summarizes the following guidelines:

- eye-to-screen distance: at least about **25 inches**, preferably more when the
  content remains readable;
- display viewing area: approximately **15°–50° below horizontal eye level**;
- monitor tilt: top slightly farther from the eyes than the bottom;
- lighting: preferably ceiling-suspended indirect lighting, with outside light
  controlled;
- screen polarity: dark letters on a light background.

> **Interpretation:** These are design guidelines, not rigid rules for every
> person. Readability, body size, task type, correction, furniture, and symptoms
> determine the final setup.

## 3.2 Viewing distance

Near viewing requires accommodation and convergence. The closer the display,
the greater these demands generally become.

Moving the display farther away can reduce the demand, but only if the text and
critical detail remain visible. If the user leans forward to read, the nominally
“farther” display has failed ergonomically.

### Practical rule

```text
Increase distance
+ preserve angular text size
+ preserve comfortable posture
= useful reduction in near demand
```

This may require larger text, a larger display, or a different document layout.

## 3.3 Display and hard-copy distance

Early advice often demanded that the monitor and document be at the same
distance. The lecture explains that users may prefer a more distant monitor and
that unequal distances do not automatically increase eyestrain.

However, when a data-entry job requires rapid repeated shifts between screen
and document, similar distances may reduce accommodative refocusing time. A
good solution is often to enlarge the document and place it near the screen at
a comfortable shared distance.

## 3.4 Vertical position and downward gaze

A modest downward gaze is common during ordinary near work. The lecture links
downward gaze with:

- easier accommodation and convergence for many users;
- less exposed ocular surface;
- lower tear evaporation;
- more options for head and neck position.

A lower screen can therefore help some users with dryness or eyestrain.

### Important balance

A screen should not be placed so low that the user is forced into sustained
extreme neck flexion. The goal is a comfortable eye position and a range of
acceptable postures.

## 3.5 Posture is dynamic

The lecture criticizes the idea that one 90°-angle posture should be maintained
all day.

The memorable statement is:

> **The best posture is the next posture.**

This means that even a good posture becomes uncomfortable when held without
variation. The workstation should allow the worker to change position without
losing visual access to the task.

### Common trap

**Wrong idea:** If a posture looks perfectly upright, it must be ergonomically
correct for prolonged work.

**Correction:** Comfort and safety depend on task access, support, movement, and
duration. A rigid “ideal” posture can itself become a source of discomfort.

## 3.6 Monitor tilt

The lecture recommends tilting the monitor so the top is slightly farther from
the eyes than the bottom.

This resembles the natural way a person holds a book or magazine. Tilting the
top toward the user may worsen visual and neck discomfort, particularly when a
low monitor is tipped forward merely to avoid glare.

Glare should usually be corrected at its source by repositioning lighting,
controlling windows, shielding fixtures, or changing surface reflectance.

## 3.7 Lighting and screen reflections

The display presents useful contrast—text against its background. Reflections
create competing contrast on the glass surface.

A bright patch reflected from a ceiling fixture or window can:

- attract attention away from the task;
- wash out screen detail;
- cause the user to change posture;
- reduce productivity.

Indirect lighting reduces sharp bright patches in the ceiling. Lower ambient
light combined with individually controlled task lighting can support workers
with different needs.

### What matters most

Absolute brightness matters, but the **contrast of the reflection** against the
surrounding screen is often the key problem.

## 3.8 Screen colours

The lecture supports dark letters on a light background in a normally lit
office.

A light background can reduce the luminance difference between the display and
the surrounding room and make reflected images less prominent. Early dark-
background displays partly reflected the technical limits of older monitors.

This is not a rule that every user must prefer the same colour theme. The
functional question is whether the chosen polarity supports legibility,
comfort, and the ambient-light environment.

## Workstation audit

When evaluating a workstation, ask:

### Display

- Is the distance comfortable?
- Is text large enough at that distance?
- Is the viewing area slightly below eye level?
- Is the screen tilted appropriately?
- Are reflections visible?

### Document

- Is it close to the screen when rapid alternation is required?
- Is the print large enough?
- Is a document holder needed?

### Input devices

- Are keyboard and mouse at a height that avoids sustained wrist extension?
- Are the upper arms relaxed?
- Does the worker reach excessively?

### Body and furniture

- Is the worker supported?
- Can posture change?
- Are feet and forearms supported when needed?
- Is the screen position forcing head rotation?

### Lighting

- Are windows controlled?
- Is the ambient level excessive?
- Is task lighting adjustable?
- Are glossy surfaces creating reflections?

## Key terms

- **Ergonomics:** designing work and equipment to fit human capability.
- **Accommodation:** change in ocular focus for near viewing.
- **Convergence:** inward rotation of the eyes for a near target.
- **Gaze angle:** viewing direction relative to horizontal eye level.
- **Visual fatigue:** discomfort associated with sustained visual demand.
- **Display polarity:** relationship between text brightness and background
  brightness.
- **Reflected glare:** light reflected from a surface into the observer’s eyes.

## Memory hook

**Farther, lower, tilted back, light controlled, posture changed.**

Use this only as a starting checklist. The real test is whether the worker can
perform effectively without injury or avoidable discomfort.

## One-minute summary

Visual ergonomics adapts the task to the worker. Use a readable viewing
distance, a comfortable downward gaze, a monitor tilted slightly away at the
top, controlled reflections, adjustable lighting, and a workstation that
permits movement. Do not solve a lighting problem by creating a posture problem.

## Active recall checkpoint

1. Why can moving a monitor farther away reduce visual demand?
2. Why might a lower monitor help dry-eye symptoms?
3. Why is one “perfect posture” not enough?
4. What is wrong with tipping a monitor downward to escape glare?
5. Why can a light screen background reduce the impact of reflections?

<details>
<summary>Reveal answers</summary>

1. It reduces accommodation and convergence demand, provided the content remains
   readable without leaning.
2. Downward gaze exposes less ocular surface and may reduce tear evaporation.
3. Any fixed posture becomes fatiguing; the workstation should support safe
   variation.
4. It may create an unnatural visual plane and greater neck/postural discomfort;
   the glare source should be controlled.
5. It reduces the luminance contrast between reflected room images and the
   useful display background.

</details>

# 4. Ocular Hazards and Injury Patterns

## Big picture

A **hazard** is a source or condition with the potential to cause harm. An
**injury** is the actual tissue damage that results.

The lecture divides ocular hazards into:

- **mechanical:** blunt impact, particles, penetrating trauma;
- **non-mechanical:** chemical, radiation, thermal, and electrical trauma.

The pattern of injury reflects the physical mechanism, the energy delivered,
the ocular structure exposed, and the time before effective first response.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- hazard versus injury;
- blunt trauma versus penetrating/perforating trauma;
- why alkali burns are generally more dangerous than most acid burns;
- immediate irrigation as the first priority in chemical exposure;
- UV as mainly photochemical and IR as mainly thermal;
- typical tissues affected by wavelength;
- common short- and long-term effects.

### Should know

- siderosis and chalcosis;
- UV latent period;
- thermal and electrical complications.

### Useful extension

- clinical grading of ocular-surface burns;
- specific industrial sources of UV and IR.

## 4.1 Mechanical injury

### Blunt trauma

Blunt trauma applies force to the globe without necessarily creating a full-
thickness wound. It may result from sports, industrial objects, falls, or
assault.

Possible consequences include:

- eyelid or conjunctival injury;
- subconjunctival haemorrhage;
- hyphaema;
- traumatic uveitis;
- retinal damage;
- traumatic cataract;
- severe globe disruption.

A normal-looking external surface does not exclude serious internal injury.

### Penetrating and perforating injury

Use the standard distinction:

- **Penetrating injury:** one full-thickness entry wound.
- **Perforating injury:** full-thickness entry and exit wounds.

A penetrating injury may contain an **intraocular foreign body**.

> **Terminology clarification:** Some lecture slides use “perforating” broadly
> for sharp injury. In these notes and the curated bank, penetrating and
> perforating are kept distinct as above.

### Foreign bodies

Foreign bodies may be:

- subtarsal conjunctival;
- corneal;
- intraocular.

High-velocity power-tool injuries are particularly concerning because the
entry wound can be small while a particle penetrates deeply.

#### Metallic intraocular foreign bodies

- **Iron** can produce **siderosis**, with toxic degeneration of ocular tissues.
- **Copper** can produce **chalcosis**, including characteristic lens changes.

The material, location, reactivity, and associated structural damage influence
management.

## 4.2 Chemical injury

Severity depends on:

- chemical identity;
- pH;
- concentration;
- contact duration;
- amount retained under the lids or in the fornices;
- time to irrigation.

### Acid versus alkali

Most acids cause protein coagulation at the surface, which may limit deeper
penetration. Alkalis saponify cell membranes and penetrate rapidly, producing
progressive tissue damage.

| Feature | Acid injury | Alkali injury |
|---|---|---|
| Early interaction | protein coagulation | cell-membrane disruption |
| Penetration | often more limited | often rapid and deep |
| Typical severity | variable | frequently more severe |
| Key exception | hydrofluoric acid may penetrate deeply | strong alkalis remain major emergencies |

### Alkali injury stages in the lecture

1. **Acute stage**
   - conjunctival ischaemic necrosis;
   - loss of corneal epithelium;
   - tissue opacification;
   - acute iritis.

2. **Repair stage**
   - epithelial regeneration;
   - corneal vascularization;
   - resolution or persistence of inflammation.

3. **Late complications**
   - symblepharon;
   - recurrent ulceration;
   - corneal vascularization;
   - uveitis;
   - secondary glaucoma;
   - cataract.

### Immediate response

> **Safety priority: begin copious irrigation immediately.**

Do not delay irrigation while searching for the ideal solution or taking a
complete history. Remove contact lenses and retained particulate matter when
feasible, continue irrigation, reassess pH, and arrange urgent ophthalmic
evaluation.

Do not attempt to neutralize an acid with an alkali or an alkali with an acid.

## 4.3 Ultraviolet radiation

UV injury is mainly **photochemical**.

### Sources in the lecture

- unprotected welding;
- germicidal or exposure lamps;
- dental curing sources;
- dermatological treatment sources;
- chronic sunlight exposure.

### Ocular absorption

- cornea is especially vulnerable to shorter UV-C and UV-B;
- lens absorbs much UV-A;
- very little UV reaches the retina in the normal phakic eye.

### Acute effects

Symptoms may be delayed by approximately 6–12 hours.

- conjunctival injection;
- lacrimation;
- blepharospasm;
- photokeratitis/keratitis;
- periocular redness.

The delay means a worker may feel well immediately after exposure and become
symptomatic later.

### Chronic associations listed in the lecture

- pterygium;
- cataract;
- cumulative ocular-surface change;
- retinal injury under unusual exposure conditions.

## 4.4 Infrared radiation

IR injury is mainly **thermal**.

### Sources

- furnaces;
- arc lamps;
- metal-inert-gas welding;
- carbon-dioxide lasers;
- other high-temperature industrial processes.

### Absorption and effects

Different IR bands are absorbed by the cornea, iris, lens, and deeper tissues.
IR-A is transmitted more effectively by the cornea than longer IR bands. The
iris and lens absorb a substantial part, and remaining radiation may reach the
retina and choroid.

Possible effects include:

- corneal opacification at strongly absorbed bands;
- iris swelling, hyperaemia, and miosis;
- aqueous flare;
- cataract;
- retinal burns.

IR-associated cataract may reflect heat transfer from the iris or direct
absorption within the lens.

## 4.5 Visible-light hazard

Visible light is necessary for vision, but intense or prolonged exposure may
damage the retina, especially when optical systems focus the source.

Examples include:

- eclipse viewing;
- intense operating microscopes;
- high-intensity industrial sources;
- improperly controlled lasers.

The relevant risk depends on wavelength, radiance, exposure time, pupil size,
and retinal focusing.

## 4.6 Thermal trauma

Flame or hot material may injure the eyelids, lashes, conjunctiva, and cornea.

- Blink reflex often protects the globe but exposes the lids.
- Flame burns may cause lash loss, lid damage, and later tearing problems from
  lacrimal scarring.
- Molten metal or high-temperature solids can produce severe contact burns,
  corneal scarring, or loss of the eye.
- Less intense burns may still cause oedema, opacification, and symblepharon.

## 4.7 Electrical trauma

High-tension electrical injury or lightning can produce damage throughout the
eye.

The lecture lists complications from anterior to posterior:

- surface and eyelid burns;
- corneal oedema or opacity;
- hyphaema;
- iritis and miosis;
- subcapsular cataract;
- vitreous opacity;
- optic-disc oedema;
- retinal detachment or oedema;
- chorioretinal atrophy.

## Key terms

- **Hazard:** source with potential to cause harm.
- **Injury:** actual tissue damage.
- **Contusion:** blunt-force injury.
- **Penetrating injury:** full-thickness entry wound only.
- **Perforating injury:** full-thickness entry and exit wounds.
- **Intraocular foreign body:** retained object within the globe.
- **Siderosis:** ocular toxicity from retained iron.
- **Chalcosis:** ocular toxicity from retained copper.
- **Symblepharon:** adhesion between palpebral and bulbar conjunctiva.
- **Photokeratitis:** acute UV-related corneal epithelial injury.

## Memory hook

For major hazards, remember **M-C-R-T-E**:

```text
Mechanical
Chemical
Radiation
Thermal
Electrical
```

For chemical exposure, remember:

```text
Irrigate first. Investigate second.
```

## One-minute summary

Ocular injury patterns follow the mechanism. Blunt trauma may damage internal
structures without an open wound. Penetrating injury has an entry wound;
perforation has entry and exit wounds. Alkali burns penetrate deeply and require
immediate irrigation. UV injury is mainly photochemical with delayed
photokeratitis; IR injury is mainly thermal. Thermal and electrical injuries may
affect structures from lid to retina.

## Active recall checkpoint

1. Distinguish a hazard from an injury.
2. Distinguish penetrating from perforating trauma.
3. Why are most alkali burns more dangerous than most acid burns?
4. Why can a welder feel normal immediately after harmful UV exposure?
5. What is the first action after a chemical splash?

<details>
<summary>Reveal answers</summary>

1. A hazard has the potential to cause harm; an injury is the resulting tissue
   damage.
2. Penetrating trauma has an entry wound only; perforating trauma has entry and
   exit wounds.
3. Alkali disrupts cell membranes and penetrates rapidly and deeply.
4. UV photokeratitis commonly has a latent period of several hours.
5. Begin immediate copious irrigation.

</details>

# 5. Eye and Face Protection

## Big picture

Personal protective equipment is selected **after** the hazard is identified.
The best-looking or largest protector is not automatically the right one.

Protection works as part of a hierarchy:

```text
Eliminate or substitute the hazard
→ engineering controls
→ administrative controls and training
→ correctly selected PPE
```

PPE is essential when exposure remains, but it should not be the only control
when the process can be made safer.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- conduct a hazard assessment before selecting PPE;
- spectacles, goggles, face shields, welding helmets, and filter lenses provide
  different protection;
- face shields and welding helmets are generally secondary protectors;
- side protection is required for flying-object exposure;
- chemical splash requires sealed goggles, with a face shield when facial
  protection is needed;
- laser protection must match wavelength and optical density;
- prescription users still require compliant protection.

### Should know

- training and retraining requirements;
- comfort, fit, cleaning, maintenance, useful life, and disposal;
- how dust, heat, impact, and optical radiation change the device choice.

### Useful extension

- the specific regulatory standards listed in the source decks;
- program documentation and compliance responsibilities.

## 5.1 Hazard assessment first

The employer should determine whether hazards are present or likely to be
present, including emergency situations.

The deck’s hazard table links hazard type to common work:

| Hazard | Examples of source tasks |
|---|---|
| Impact | chipping, grinding, machining, drilling, riveting, sanding |
| Heat | furnaces, pouring, casting, hot dipping, welding |
| Chemicals | acid handling, degreasing, plating, blood exposure |
| Dust | woodworking, buffing, dusty operations |
| Optical radiation | welding, brazing, cutting, soldering, lasers |

A good assessment also considers direction, velocity, particle size, splash
pressure, temperature, wavelength, exposure duration, and whether more than one
hazard exists.

## 5.2 Training and retraining

Workers should know:

- when PPE is necessary;
- which PPE is necessary;
- how to put it on, remove it, adjust it, and wear it;
- its limitations;
- how to care for, clean, maintain, store, and dispose of it.

Retraining is required when:

- the workplace changes;
- the PPE changes;
- observed behaviour shows that the worker has not retained the required
  knowledge or skill.

A device is not protective merely because it was issued. It must be worn
correctly and remain in serviceable condition.

## 5.3 General qualities of a protector

A protector should:

- match the hazard;
- be safely designed;
- fit securely without unnecessarily interfering with movement;
- be reasonably comfortable;
- be durable;
- be cleanable and disinfectable where needed;
- be clearly marked;
- be compatible with other PPE.

Poor fit creates gaps, slippage, fogging, distraction, and non-use.

## 5.4 Prescription lenses and contact lenses

Workers who require a prescription should use:

- protective eyewear incorporating the prescription; or
- protective eyewear designed to fit over prescription spectacles without
  disturbing either device.

Ordinary dress spectacles are not automatically safety spectacles.

Contact lenses do not replace eye protection. The hazard assessment determines
the required protector.

## 5.5 Match the device to the hazard

### Impact

**Safety spectacles with side shields** protect against many flying fragments
and particles approaching from the front and side.

**Goggles** fit around the orbital area and form a seal, reducing entry around
the edges.

**Face shields** protect a larger facial area but do not provide sufficient
primary eye protection from impact when worn alone. Combine them with
spectacles or goggles.

### Heat and molten material

Use primary eye protection plus a heat-reflective or wire-screen face shield
when facial heat exposure exists.

For molten splash, goggles that seal around the eyes are particularly important.

### Chemical splash

Use chemical safety goggles that protect the eyes and immediate surrounding
area. Add a face shield when the entire face may be exposed.

A face shield alone leaves gaps and is secondary protection.

### Dust

Use goggles that seal against dust. Ventilation must prevent dust entry; an
open vent can defeat the purpose.

### Welding and optical radiation

Use the correct filter shade for the process and radiant-energy level.
A welding helmet protects the face from radiation, heat, and impact but is used
with primary eye protection.

### Lasers

Laser goggles must match:

- the specific wavelength;
- the required optical density;
- the beam and task conditions.

“Dark” eyewear is not automatically laser protection.

### Glare

Glare may be controlled through:

- changes in the work area or lighting;
- special-purpose spectacles or filters;
- tinted lenses or visors where appropriate.

The first question should still be whether the glare source can be eliminated,
shielded, repositioned, or reduced.

## Worked PPE choices

| Scenario | Primary choice | Additional control |
|---|---|---|
| Metal grinding | impact-rated spectacles with side shields or goggles | machine guard; face shield for added facial protection |
| Acid transfer | sealed chemical goggles | face shield, process enclosure, eyewash |
| Dusty woodworking | sealed or appropriately vented dust goggles | extraction ventilation |
| Welding | suitable primary eye protection with correct filter | welding helmet as secondary protector |
| Laser alignment | wavelength- and optical-density-specific goggles | beam enclosure, interlocks, controlled area |
| Furnace work | primary eye protection | heat-reflective face shield and process controls |

## Common misconceptions

### “A face shield protects everything.”

A face shield provides broad facial coverage but is normally secondary
protection. Particles or liquid can travel around it.

### “The darkest filter is the safest.”

An excessively dark filter can prevent the worker from seeing the task and may
encourage unsafe behaviour. The shade must match the source.

### “Prescription glasses count as safety glasses.”

Only if they meet the relevant protective requirements.

### “Once trained, the worker never needs retraining.”

Workplace, equipment, and behaviour changes can make retraining necessary.

## Standards note

The supplied slides quote older ANSI Z87.1 editions. The durable learning point
is to use protection meeting the currently applicable standard and hazard
requirements. Do not memorize obsolete purchase-date rules as though they are a
clinical principle.

## Key terms

- **PPE:** personal protective equipment.
- **Primary protector:** device worn directly to protect the eyes.
- **Secondary protector:** additional face protection used with primary
  protection.
- **Side shield:** lateral impact protection on spectacles.
- **Filter shade:** attenuation level selected for an optical-radiation source.
- **Optical density:** logarithmic measure of how strongly a filter attenuates a
  specified wavelength.
- **Hazard assessment:** systematic identification and characterization of
  exposure before control selection.

## Memory hook

**Assess → Control → Select → Fit → Train → Maintain.**

## One-minute summary

PPE selection begins with the hazard. Engineering and administrative controls
come first where feasible. Spectacles, goggles, face shields, welding helmets,
and filters are not interchangeable. Side shields address lateral impact,
goggles provide a seal, and face shields are secondary protection. Prescription
and contact-lens users still need proper PPE. Training includes use,
limitations, care, and retraining.

## Active recall checkpoint

1. Why is a face shield not enough for chemical splash?
2. What two properties must laser eyewear match?
3. When are side shields important?
4. Why is PPE considered the last layer of control?
5. What should happen when workers repeatedly misuse an issued protector?

<details>
<summary>Reveal answers</summary>

1. Liquid can pass around the shield; sealed chemical goggles provide primary
   eye protection.
2. The laser wavelength and the required optical density.
3. When flying objects can approach from the side.
4. Elimination, substitution, engineering, and administrative controls can
   reduce exposure at the source and protect more reliably.
5. Investigate fit and task problems and provide retraining or different
   protection as needed.

</details>

# 6. Workplace Lighting

## Big picture

The purpose of workplace lighting is not to achieve a number on a meter. It is
to make the critical detail visible, support safe performance, control glare and
shadow, and accommodate the worker.

**Illuminance** is measured in **lux**:

> **1 lux = 1 lumen per square metre.**

More light is not always better. Lighting must be appropriate in amount,
distribution, direction, spectrum, and stability.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- the meaning of illuminance and lux;
- human, area, task, and equipment factors in lighting assessment;
- direct versus indirect lighting;
- direct versus reflected glare;
- why critical-detail size, contrast, time, and movement influence light needs;
- the danger of flicker around rotating machinery;
- the importance of emergency lighting and maintenance.

### Should know

- task-lighting strategy;
- needs of older workers and workers with visual limitations;
- the role of surface reflectance and cleanliness.

### Useful extension

- example illuminance ranges in the lecture;
- formal lighting survey documentation.

## 6.1 Benefits of proper lighting

Good lighting:

- reduces strain;
- improves task speed and accuracy;
- highlights hazards;
- reduces errors and accidents;
- supports a pleasant and productive environment.

Poor lighting may cause shadow-driven awkward posture, visual fatigue, missed
hazards, and compensatory leaning.

## 6.2 Four assessment domains

The lecture organizes assessment around:

1. **Human factors**
   - age;
   - visual limitation;
   - clarity and comfort;
   - need for adjustment.

2. **Area**
   - room size and shape;
   - surfaces;
   - daylight;
   - access and circulation.

3. **Task**
   - detail size;
   - contrast;
   - colour;
   - speed;
   - accuracy;
   - time of day.

4. **Equipment and furniture**
   - glossy surfaces;
   - displays;
   - rotating machinery;
   - task position;
   - shadows.

## 6.3 Example illuminance levels

The slides provide examples such as:

- good simple detail: approximately 240 lux;
- average detail: approximately 320 lux;
- poor or fine detail: approximately 600 lux.

Another task table ranges from tens of lux for simple areas to thousands for
very demanding inspection.

> **Interpretation:** Do not treat one number as universally correct. The
> correct level depends on detail, contrast, speed, accuracy, worker needs,
> glare, and the governing workplace standard.

## 6.4 Direct and indirect lighting

### Direct lighting

A direct system sends most light downward toward the work area.

Advantages:

- efficient delivery to the task;
- useful where strong local illumination is needed.

Risks:

- hard shadows;
- bright luminaires in the field of view;
- strong reflected glare.

### Indirect lighting

An indirect system sends most light toward the ceiling and walls, which then
reflect it into the workspace.

Advantages:

- more diffuse distribution;
- reduced sharp source contrast;
- less screen reflection when correctly designed.

Limitations:

- depends on suitable ceiling and wall reflectance;
- may require task lights for fine work.

### Task lighting

Local adjustable light allows the worker to increase illumination at the task
without making the entire room excessively bright.

## 6.5 Visibility and brightness

The amount of light needed increases when:

- detail is smaller;
- contrast is lower;
- the target moves;
- viewing time is shorter;
- colour discrimination is important;
- error is dangerous or expensive;
- the worker is older or visually limited.

Brightness is often controllable, but excessive local brightness creates glare
for the worker or nearby colleagues.

## 6.6 Direct glare

**Direct glare** occurs when a bright source lies in the field of view, such as
an unshielded lamp or window.

Controls include:

- shields;
- louvers;
- reflectors;
- blinds and curtains;
- changing fixture position;
- changing the workstation orientation.

## 6.7 Reflected glare

**Reflected glare** occurs when light bounces from a surface into the eyes.
Glossy desks, glass, polished material, and display screens are common sources.

It may be especially troublesome because the reflection lies near the line of
sight to the task.

Controls include:

- moving the source or task;
- using indirect lighting;
- reducing source intensity;
- positioning the worker sideways to a window;
- using partitions;
- matte paper and surfaces;
- anti-glare screen treatment after the source is assessed.

## 6.8 Flicker and rotating machinery

Fluorescent or other periodic lighting can create a **stroboscopic effect**. A
rotating or oscillating machine may appear slower or stationary even while it is
moving.

This is not merely discomfort; it is a safety hazard. Lighting selection and
maintenance must account for machinery speed and flicker characteristics.

## 6.9 Maintenance

Light output falls as lamps age and fixtures or surfaces become dirty.

A lighting program should include:

- regular illuminance measurement;
- cleaning fixtures, walls, and work surfaces;
- replacing lamps before widespread failure;
- checking adjustable task lights;
- testing emergency systems;
- training workers to report defects.

## 6.10 Emergency lighting

Emergency lighting should support safe exit during power failure. Exit signs,
stairs, routes, and underground walkways must remain visible under the relevant
local standard.

The source deck gives 10 lux as an example average at floor or tread level.
Treat this as a course example; the applicable legal requirement must be
verified for the jurisdiction and building type.

## Workplace checklist

- Are work areas free of disabling shadows?
- Can workers see without straining?
- Is task lighting adjustable?
- Are screens and work surfaces free from glare?
- Are hazards well lit?
- Is adaptation time allowed between bright and dim areas?
- Is task/background contrast adequate?
- Are older and visually limited workers accommodated?
- Are levels measured?
- Are fixtures and surfaces cleaned?
- Are lamps replaced?
- Does emergency lighting work?
- Have workers received information and training?

## Key terms

- **Illuminance:** luminous flux incident on a surface.
- **Lux:** one lumen per square metre.
- **Direct lighting:** system sending most light toward the task.
- **Indirect lighting:** system reflecting most light from ceiling or walls.
- **Task lighting:** local illumination directed at the work area.
- **Direct glare:** bright source directly within the field of view.
- **Reflected glare:** source reflected from a surface into the eye.
- **Stroboscopic effect:** false appearance of slowed or stopped motion under
  periodic illumination.

## Memory hook

**Human – Area – Task – Equipment.**

For glare control:

**Move it, shield it, dim it, diffuse it, or change the surface.**

## One-minute summary

Lighting must support the task, not merely meet a meter reading. Assess the
worker, area, task, and equipment. Fine or low-contrast detail needs more useful
illumination, but excessive brightness creates glare. Direct light is efficient
but may create shadows and reflections; indirect light is diffuse and often
better for displays. Maintain fixtures and verify emergency lighting. Treat
flicker near machinery as a safety problem.

5. Give four items from a workplace lighting checklist.

<summary>Reveal answers</summary>

   square metre.
2. Age-related visual change can increase the light needed for detail while
