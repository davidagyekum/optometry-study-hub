## How to use this module

The lecture groups together lenses used when ordinary single-vision or multifocal dispensing is not enough. The central questions are:

- Is the problem excessive thickness or weight?
- Is retinal image size unequal between eyes?
- Is protection from impact, radiation, or glare required?
- Is temporary or high prism needed?
- Is vertical imbalance occurring at near?

> **Source approach:** Several slides contain historical, commercial, or internally inconsistent statements. The notes preserve the lecture's examinable concepts while clearly flagging claims that require lecturer or current-standard verification.

<a id="lenticular-aspheric"></a>
# 1. Lenticular and Aspheric Lenses

## 1.1 Lenticular lens

A **lenticular lens** concentrates the required prescription into a relatively small central optical zone. The surrounding **carrier** has little or no correcting power and supports the central portion.

The source presents lenticular lenses mainly for:

- very high hyperopia, including aphakic correction when an implant is not feasible;
- very high myopia in a minus lenticular form.

If the full power extended across the entire lens, thickness and weight would become excessive.

## 1.2 Structure

```text
central bowl or optical aperture -> carries most of the prescription
carrier -> supports the optical zone and fits the frame
junction -> boundary between optical portion and carrier
```

For a high-plus lenticular lens, the central optical zone is thick and magnifying. For a high-minus form, the central zone is strongly concave and minifying.

## 1.3 Advantages

- reduces total mass compared with full-aperture high-power lenses;
- limits extreme thickness to a smaller zone;
- makes otherwise impractical powers wearable;
- can be combined with careful frame and material selection.

## 1.4 Limitations

- restricted field of clear view;
- visible boundary between optical zone and carrier;
- ring scotoma or “jack-in-the-box” effects in high plus;
- cosmetic magnification/minification of the eyes;
- greater need for accurate centration and stable fit.

## 1.5 Aspheric lens

The lecture introduces **aspheric lenses** largely through diagrams. An aspheric surface changes curvature progressively away from the centre rather than maintaining one spherical curvature.

In dispensing, aspheric design may:

- flatten a high-plus lens;
- reduce centre thickness, weight, and bulging appearance;
- improve off-axis performance compared with an equivalent simple spherical form;
- reduce spectacle-eye magnification in some high-plus corrections.

The patient must look through the intended optical centre; decentration and poor frame adjustment can reduce the benefit.

<a id="aniseikonia"></a>
# 2. Anisometropia, Aniseikonia and Iseikonic Lenses

## 2.1 Definitions

**Anisometropia** is a significant difference in refractive error between the two eyes.

**Antimetropia** is anisometropia in which the two eyes require opposite lens signs, for example:

```text
OD +2.00 D
OS -2.00 D
```

**Aniseikonia** is unequal perceived retinal image size between the eyes that interferes with comfortable binocular vision.

**Iseikonic/size lenses** are designed to alter retinal image size, ideally without changing image position or the required refractive correction.

## 2.2 Source model: size lenses as afocal telescopes

The deck describes size lenses as miniature afocal telescopes. Turning the convex side away from the eye produces slight magnification; reversing the element produces slight minification. Because they are afocal, small percentage elements can be stacked, for example 1% and 2% to estimate 3%.

## 2.3 When symptoms can occur

Aniseikonic symptoms require useful binocular vision. If one eye has poor correctable acuity and fusion is not occurring, matching image sizes may not produce a useful binocular benefit.

The source gives a rule of thumb that special image-size correction is generally not appropriate when one eye cannot be corrected to approximately 6/18 (20/60) or better. Treat this as course guidance rather than a universal threshold.

## 2.4 Symptoms listed in the source

- headache and eyestrain;
- reading difficulty;
- dizziness;
- photophobia;
- motility difficulty;
- nervousness;
- distortion of space;
- aching, burning, pulling, itching, or tiring sensations;
- a report that the spectacles “do not feel right.”

These symptoms are non-specific and can resemble uncorrected ametropia or heterophoria.

## 2.5 Identifying a suspicious prescription

A large interocular difference raises suspicion, for example:

```text
OD +1.00 D
OS +5.00 D
```

For astigmatic prescriptions, the deck uses spherical equivalent:

\[
SE=S+\frac{C}{2}
\]

Example:

```text
OD +2.50/+0.50 x 090 -> SE +2.75 D
OS plano/+5.00 x 090 -> SE +2.50 D
```

Although the written cylinders look very different, the spherical equivalents are similar. The lecture uses this to show why the Rx must be analysed rather than judged by cylinder magnitude alone.

## 2.6 Prescription alone is not proof

Some patients adapt to large image-size differences over years. The source advises that a successful existing pair should be duplicated as closely as possible:

- frame style and size;
- vertex distance;
- base curve;
- material/index;
- centre thickness;
- lens form.

Changing one variable can change spectacle magnification and produce symptoms.

## 2.7 Treatment hierarchy in the lecture

The deck states that contact lenses are usually the treatment of choice for refractive aniseikonia caused by anisometropia, unless contraindicated, because they sit close to the eye and reduce spectacle magnification differences.

Other options listed include:

- afocal size lenses over the refraction;
- spectacles in axial anisometropia;
- changing vertex distance differently between eyes;
- contact lens plus spectacle combinations;
- increasing thickness for the eye with the smaller image;
- decreasing refractive index to increase shape magnification;
- changing base curve.

The goal is not always perfect equality; it may be sufficient to reduce the difference until comfortable fusion is restored.

<a id="spectacle-magnification"></a>
# 3. Spectacle Magnification

## 3.1 Components

The source states that spectacle magnification depends on:

- dioptric power;
- vertex distance;
- base curve/front surface power;
- centre thickness;
- refractive index.

It divides the effect into:

- **power factor (P)**;
- **shape factor (S)**.

Total spectacle magnification:

\[
SM=S\times P
\]

Percentage magnification:

\[
Mag\%=\left(SM-1\right)\times100
\]

A result above 1 is magnification; below 1 is minification.

## 3.2 Power factor

\[
P=\left(1-dF_v\right)^{-1}
\]

where:

- \(d\) = vertex distance in metres;
- \(F_v\) = back vertex power in dioptres.

For a plus lens, increasing vertex distance increases magnification. Moving the lens closer to the eye decreases it. For a minus lens, the direction of image-size effect is reversed in practical terms: moving the lens closer reduces minification.

## 3.3 Shape factor

\[
S=\left[1-\left(\frac{t}{n}\right)F_1\right]^{-1}
\]

where:

- \(t\) = centre thickness in metres;
- \(n\) = refractive index;
- \(F_1\) = front surface power/base curve.

Increasing thickness or front curve, and decreasing refractive index, tends to increase shape magnification in a plus-form lens.

## 3.4 Worked example retained from the lecture

A +4.00 D lens has:

- front surface +10.00 D;
- centre thickness 6 mm;
- refractive index 1.50;
- vertex distance 15 mm.

### Shape factor

\[
S=\left[1-\left(\frac{0.006}{1.50}\right)(10.00)\right]^{-1}
=1.042
\]

### Power factor

\[
P=\left[1-(0.015)(4.00)\right]^{-1}=1.0638
\]

### Total

\[
SM=1.042\times1.0638=1.1085
\]

\[
Mag\%=(1.1085-1)\times100=10.85\%
\]

## 3.5 Case-study discrepancy in the source

The deck calculates magnification for a unilateral-cataract case and reports:

- OD approximately 2.4%;
- OS approximately 10.6%;
- difference approximately 8.2%.

Some intermediate substitutions and repeated denominators in the slides are inconsistent. The final teaching point is that the calculated difference is large enough to justify an iseikonic design in the case.

> **Course-source discrepancy:** Do not use the inconsistent intermediate arithmetic as a single-correct-answer calculation without lecturer review. Use the clearly defined formulas and the fully worked +4.00 D example for numerical assessment.

## 3.6 Variables available to the designer

To increase magnification of one lens:

- increase vertex distance for a plus lens;
- steepen the base curve;
- increase centre thickness;
- use a lower index, within practical limits.

To reduce magnification, apply the opposite changes. The desired design must also remain wearable, cosmetically acceptable, and optically safe.

<a id="safety-filters"></a>
# 4. Safety Lenses, Toughening, Filters and Coatings

## 4.1 Safety purpose

Ordinary glass is brittle and can form sharp fragments. A safety lens is made or treated to reduce injury risk from breakage or impact.

The source lists industrial protection areas including:

- welding filters;
- eye, face, and neck protection from welding radiation;
- steelwork protection;
- industrial impact protection;
- filters for intense sun glare.

> **Current-standard caution:** The deck does not identify current standard numbers or jurisdictions. Codex must not present the list as a verified 2026 compliance standard. The site should label it as lecture content and require current local standards for real procurement or occupational advice.

## 4.2 Thermally toughened glass

The source describes heating a finished glass lens to around its annealing range and rapidly cooling it. It states that toughened lenses:

- resist impact more than ordinary glass;
- resist scratching;
- break into relatively less dangerous fragments;
- require a minimum thickness around 2.5-3.0 mm.

> **Course-source typo flag:** One slide prints “7000C.” The intended temperature is unclear from the source and must not be silently normalised in course-aligned content. Questions should test the process, not that number, unless lecturer clarification is obtained.

## 4.3 Chemical toughening

The lecture describes hot dipping in a potassium-ion salt bath for a prolonged period, stated as approximately 16 hours. Ion exchange creates compressive surface stress.

## 4.4 Reflection, absorption, and transmission

When radiation reaches a lens:

- some is reflected at the surfaces;
- some is absorbed in the material or coating;
- the remainder is transmitted.

In simplified energy terms:

\[
incident=reflected+absorbed+transmitted
\]

## 4.5 Filters and tints

The deck presents filters for:

- high-intensity industrial radiation;
- glare control;
- low-vision support;
- traumatic brain injury-associated visual symptoms;
- orientation and mobility.

Filters may work through:

- absorption;
- reflection/metallic coating;
- a combination of both.

The source mentions grey and yellow/“blue-blocker” tints. Selection should be driven by the patient's task, comfort, transmission needs, and verified protection rather than colour alone.

## 4.6 Anti-reflection coating

A multiple anti-reflection coating reduces surface reflections and improves transmission/cosmesis. It does not replace impact protection or a verified radiation filter.

## 4.7 Photochromic lenses

The deck states that photochromic lenses change darkness with light exposure and are available in single vision, bifocal, and progressive forms.

> **Claim caution:** Statements such as “blocks 100%” or broad claims about blue-light health benefit should not be repeated as universal facts without product-specific transmission data and current evidence. Preserve them only as source claims when necessary.

<a id="fresnel-prism"></a>
# 5. Fresnel Prism

## 5.1 Structure and purpose

A **Fresnel prism** uses many small prismatic grooves to create prism in a thin, lightweight sheet. It can be attached temporarily to a spectacle lens.

Common practical uses include:

- trial prism before permanent manufacture;
- changing prism needs;
- large prism powers that would be thick in a ground lens;
- temporary management during recovery or investigation.

Limitations include reduced acuity/contrast, visible grooves, cleaning difficulty, and cosmetic appearance.

## 5.2 Base orientation from the source

The prism arrives with a border identifying the base.

| Required prism | Base location on inside of lens | Groove direction in source |
|---|---|---|
| Base out | toward temporal frame/arm | vertical |
| Base in | toward nose/pads | vertical |
| Base up | toward top rim | horizontal |
| Base down | toward bottom rim | horizontal |

## 5.3 Fitting procedure

1. Confirm eye, prism power, and base direction.
2. Hold the prism against the inside surface of the correct lens.
3. Trace the lens outline on the rough side with a non-permanent marker.
4. Cut carefully with small scissors.
5. Place a pool of clean water on the inside lens surface.
6. Press the smooth side of the Fresnel prism onto the water.
7. Squeeze out water and air bubbles.
8. Dry gently and verify orientation and clarity.

The source warns that repeated removal and cleaning can make the edges curl and prevent flat adhesion.

## 5.4 Cleaning and removal

- use a soft dry cloth for routine cleaning;
- use a soft toothbrush/nailbrush gently along grooves for dust;
- wash occasionally with water;
- remove by lifting an edge carefully with a fingernail, then wash with warm water and soap.

## 5.5 Verification

- confirm base orientation before cutting;
- make sure grooves are not rotated 90 degrees;
- verify the sheet is on the intended eye;
- check for trapped bubbles;
- assess vision and binocular response on the patient;
- document temporary prism power and base.

<a id="slab-off"></a>
# 6. Bicentric Grinding and Slab-Off

## 6.1 Why vertical imbalance occurs

When a multifocal wearer looks below the distance optical centres, each lens produces vertical prism. Unequal vertical powers create unequal prism at the near visual point.

Conditions listed as potential causes include:

- antimetropia;
- anisomyopia;
- anisohyperopia.

## 6.2 Bicentric grinding

**Bicentric grinding** creates two optical centres on one lens. It is also called **slab-off** and is used to neutralise vertical imbalance, often when the imbalance exceeds about 1.5 prism dioptres in the source.

## 6.3 Conventional and reverse slab-off

### Conventional slab-off

- produces base-up effect in the reading portion;
- applied to the most minus or least plus lens;
- source describes it as removal of base-down prism.

### Reverse slab-off

- produces base-down effect in the reading portion;
- applied to the least minus or most plus lens;
- manufactured on the opposite surface from conventional processing in the course description.

Mnemonic:

```text
Conventional: BU on most minus / least plus
Reverse: BD on least minus / most plus
```

## 6.4 Calculation workflow

1. Determine power in the 90-degree meridian of each lens.
2. Determine the reading drop from distance optical centre to NVP in centimetres.
3. Apply Prentice's rule to each eye.
4. Determine the vertical prism base direction.
5. Compare the two eyes:
   - same base direction -> subtract magnitudes;
   - opposite base directions -> add magnitudes.
6. Select conventional or reverse slab-off and the appropriate lens.

## 6.5 Power in the vertical meridian

For a sphero-cylinder lens, determine power in the 90-degree meridian. The source advises transposing to axis 90 when useful.

Example from the deck:

```text
OS -0.75/+2.50 x 180
transposes to +1.75/-2.50 x 090
```

The power in the 90-degree meridian is then +1.75 D.

## 6.6 Worked example: opposite base directions

```text
OD +1.00 DS
OS -2.50 DS
reading level 8 mm below OC
```

OD:

\[
P=0.8\times1.00=0.8\Delta\ BU
\]

OS:

\[
P=0.8\times2.50=2.0\Delta\ BD
\]

Because the bases are opposite, the imbalance compounds:

\[
0.8+2.0=2.8\Delta
\]

The deck orders conventional slab-off of 2.8 prism dioptres base up on OS, the most-minus lens.

## 6.7 Worked example: same base direction

```text
OD -3.75 DS
OS -2.75 DS
reading level 11 mm below OC
```

OD:

\[
P=1.1\times3.75=4.125\Delta\ BD\approx4.1\Delta\ BD
\]

OS:

\[
P=1.1\times2.75=3.025\Delta\ BD\approx3.0\Delta\ BD
\]

Same base direction, so subtract:

\[
4.1-3.0=1.1\Delta
\]

The source then recommends approximately 1.0 prism dioptre reverse slab-off base down on OS.

> **Course-source rounding flag:** The slides calculate 1.1 prism dioptres but show an order of 1.0 without explaining the rounding rule. Preserve the discrepancy and ask for lecturer/laboratory policy before turning it into an exact ordering requirement.

## 6.8 Another source example

```text
OD -1.25 DS
OS -0.75/+2.50 x 180 -> vertical meridian +1.75 D
NVP 10 mm below OC
```

OD gives 1.25 prism dioptres base down. OS gives 1.75 prism dioptres base up. Opposite bases compound to 3.0 prism dioptres.

The source gives two ways to neutralise:

- 3.0 prism dioptres conventional slab-off base up on OD;
- 3.0 prism dioptres reverse slab-off base down on OS.

## 6.9 Verification

The deck notes that slab-off can be checked with a lens clock/lens measure. In practice, verify:

- correct eye;
- correct base direction;
- slab line position;
- power and prism at the near reference level;
- cosmetic acceptability;
- binocular comfort.

## 6.10 Final mastery checklist

You should be able to:

- explain why lenticular lenses reduce weight and thickness;
- distinguish anisometropia, antimetropia, and aniseikonia;
- calculate spherical equivalent;
- calculate power factor, shape factor, and total spectacle magnification;
- identify variables used to design an iseikonic lens;
- distinguish safety, filter, AR, and photochromic functions;
- orient, fit, clean, and verify a Fresnel prism;
- calculate vertical prism at near;
- choose conventional versus reverse slab-off;
- identify the source's temperature, magnification, and rounding ambiguities.
