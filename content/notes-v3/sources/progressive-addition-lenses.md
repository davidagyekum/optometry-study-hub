## How to use this module

A progressive addition lens (PAL) succeeds only when four systems agree:

```text
prescription + design + frame position + wearer behaviour
```

A perfect prescription in a poorly adjusted frame can fail. A technically correct lens can also fail when the selected corridor does not suit the frame or task. The lecture therefore presents PAL fitting as both an art - communication, counselling, motivation - and a science - systematic measurement, design knowledge, and troubleshooting.

> **Source approach:** The source slides are practical fitting materials. Their recommendations are retained as course guidance. Manufacturer-specific minimum fitting heights and verification rules must still be checked on the actual layout chart supplied with the lens.

<a id="pal-principles"></a>
# 1. Progressive Lens Principles

## 1.1 Definition

A **progressive addition lens** provides a continuous increase in plus power from the distance zone through an intermediate progression to the near zone. Unlike a bifocal or trifocal, it has no abrupt dividing line.

The lens can be thought of as three functional regions:

1. distance zone;
2. progression/corridor;
3. near zone.

The progression contains continuously changing power rather than one fixed intermediate addition.

## 1.2 Why PALs are prescribed

The sources contrast three presbyopic options.

### Multiple single-vision pairs

- clear at one selected distance at a time;
- can be inconvenient and cumbersome;
- requires changing spectacles between tasks.

### Bifocals

- distance and near are available in one lens;
- visible junction;
- no full intermediate progression;
- image jump may occur.

### PALs

- continuous vision across many distances;
- no visible junction;
- no abrupt segment jump;
- cosmetically similar to single vision;
- convenient for general use.

## 1.3 Advantages listed in the course

- vision at distance, intermediate, and near;
- more continuous use of accommodation and gaze;
- absence of image jump from a segment boundary;
- no visible dividing line;
- no differential segment displacement as in lined multifocals;
- improved cosmetic acceptance.

## 1.4 Limitations listed in the course

- peripheral unwanted astigmatism and distortion;
- adaptation problems for some wearers;
- greater sensitivity to fitting errors;
- more critical frame and measurement requirements;
- higher cost.

## 1.5 Rate of power change

The main deck gives, for a linear progression:

\[
rate\ of\ power\ change=\frac{A}{h}
\]

where:

- \(A\) = total add power;
- \(h\) = progression length in millimetres.

Example: +2.00 D add over an 8 mm progression:

\[
\frac{2.00}{8}=0.25\text{ D per mm}
\]

A shorter corridor for the same add requires a faster change in power. The sources associate this with greater peripheral distortion and a more demanding adaptation experience.

## 1.6 Unwanted astigmatism

A progressive surface cannot increase power along the corridor without producing unwanted astigmatism to the sides. The main deck displays the expression:

\[
Astig=-\frac{2A}{h}
\]

> **Course-source notation flag:** The slide does not fully define the sign, units, or exact context of this expression. Preserve it as lecture notation, but do not build a high-stakes numerical item that assumes more than the slide explains.

## 1.7 Effect of cylinder

The *Progressive fitting cross* document compares a +5.00 D sphere with a +5.00/-2.00 cylinder prescription at the same add. It states that higher incorporated cylinder reduces visual performance because it combines with the lens's peripheral astigmatic distortion.

The practical lesson is not “never prescribe PALs with cylinder.” It is that:

- high cylinder may narrow useful zones;
- measurements and frame position become even more important;
- realistic counselling is needed;
- design choice and verification should be more deliberate.

<a id="pal-designs"></a>
# 2. Types and Use-Oriented Designs

The main deck lists several types. Some labels describe an optical design; others describe a use category or added lens treatment.

## 2.1 Standard progressive lenses

- general-purpose distance-to-near progression;
- often require a relatively generous frame depth;
- less customised than premium designs;
- commonly offer a broad reading area when adequately fitted.

## 2.2 Premium progressive lenses

The source describes premium PALs as more customised to:

- prescription;
- chosen frame;
- ocular anatomy;
- sometimes dominant-eye information.

They are intended to give smoother transitions and wider useful fields, at higher cost.

## 2.3 Short-corridor progressives

These are intended for shallow or small frames. The shorter vertical progression preserves the full add within limited depth but can produce a faster power gradient and greater perceived distortion for some wearers.

## 2.4 Computer/office progressives

The source calls these “computer progressive” or “near-variable-focus” lenses. They are designed for office and intermediate/near tasks, not for unrestricted general distance use. A patient may need a separate general-use pair.

## 2.5 Ground-view progressives

The deck describes ground-view designs as improving vision through the lower and side regions for activities such as walking, driving, computer use, and looking down.

## 2.6 Photochromic/“transition” progressives

The source correctly notes that photochromic change is a lens treatment/feature rather than a distinct progressive geometry. A PAL can be made in photochromic material.

## 2.7 Corridor and design selection

Match design to:

- frame depth and minimum fitting height;
- add power;
- working distances;
- dominant tasks;
- previous lens design;
- head and eye movement patterns;
- prescription, especially cylinder, anisometropia, and prism;
- cost and expectations.

<a id="reference-markings"></a>
# 3. Progressive Reference Markings

## 3.1 Why temporary and permanent markings exist

PAL power changes continuously, so the lens cannot be verified by looking for a visible segment. Manufacturers provide reference markings and a layout chart.

Temporary ink markings commonly identify:

- fitting cross;
- distance reference point/circle;
- prism reference point;
- near reference point/circle;
- horizontal alignment line.

Permanent laser engravings commonly identify:

- manufacturer or design marks;
- add power;
- fitting/alignment reference.

## 3.2 Fitting cross

The **fitting cross** is the principal patient-centred reference. It should be placed in front of the pupil centre in the frame's final wearing position unless the manufacturer's instructions specify otherwise.

The uploaded document shows the ideal fitting crosses aligned with the pupils and diagrams the major reference points of a progressive lens.

## 3.3 Distance reference point

The **distance reference point** is the area used to verify distance power. It is usually above the fitting cross.

## 3.4 Prism reference point

The **prism reference point** is used to verify prescribed prism and any design prism/thinning prism. It is usually near the horizontal reference line.

## 3.5 Near reference point

The **near reference point** is the area used to verify full near addition. It is below the fitting cross at the end of the progression.

## 3.6 Restoring erased markings

1. Locate permanent engravings under suitable illumination.
2. Place the lens on the correct manufacturer layout chart.
3. Align engravings and horizontal axis.
4. Re-ink the fitting cross, distance circle, prism point, and near circle.
5. Verify that right and left lenses have not been swapped.

## 3.7 Decentration interpretation

Because the fitting cross is compared with the pupil, an error can be:

- too high;
- too low;
- too far nasal;
- too far temporal;
- unequal between eyes;
- apparently correct before adjustment but wrong in the final wearing position.

<a id="patient-frame"></a>
# 4. Patient Assessment and Frame Selection

## 4.1 Identify requirements and expectations

The first fitting step in the practical deck is to identify the patient's needs. Ask about:

- occupation and hobbies;
- computer and phone use;
- reading distance and duration;
- driving, especially night driving;
- overhead or floor-level tasks;
- previous multifocal experience;
- reason for changing spectacles;
- cosmetic expectations;
- willingness to learn new head and eye movements.

The source advises the dispenser to judge motivation, highlight benefits, and also state limitations.

## 4.2 Likely successful wearers

The main deck lists:

- satisfied existing PAL wearers;
- young/early presbyopes receiving a first add;
- patients needing intermediate focus;
- patients who do not want visible dividing lines.

## 4.3 Cautions and relative contraindications in the practical deck

The slides flag:

- specialised field-of-view requirements;
- unusual direction of gaze;
- limited mobility or head movement;
- anisometropia or antimetropia;
- high cylinders;
- prism prescriptions.

These are not absolute bans. They signal that design choice, counselling, and verification need greater care and that another lens type may better match the task.

## 4.4 Frame selection

Select a frame that:

- provides enough vertical depth for the intended corridor and near zone;
- sits stably without slipping;
- allows appropriate vertex distance;
- permits suitable pantoscopic tilt;
- does not force excessive wrap unless the design compensates for it;
- can be adjusted repeatedly;
- places the pupils within a workable lens shape.

The practical deck prefers adjustable metal nose pads as a useful option because they permit vertical and vertex adjustments. This is practical guidance, not a universal requirement.

## 4.5 Adjust before measuring

Never take final monocular PDs or fitting heights in an unadjusted frame. Adjust:

- bridge/nose pads;
- temple length and bend;
- frame level;
- face form/wrap;
- vertex distance;
- pantoscopic tilt;
- frame height on the face.

The source recommends a short back vertex distance and a pantoscopic tilt around 10-12 degrees as a common target. Actual design requirements and patient anatomy still govern.

<a id="measure-order"></a>
# 5. Measurement and Ordering Workflow

## 5.1 Monocular PD

PALs require **monocular distance PDs** because the right and left eyes may not be equally spaced from the facial midline.

Record separately:

```text
right monocular PD / left monocular PD
```

A binocular PD divided by two is unsafe when facial asymmetry is present.

## 5.2 Fitting height

**Fitting height** is measured from the lowest point of the lens shape/eyewire to the pupil-centred fitting cross, separately for each eye.

### Practical method

1. Adjust final frame.
2. Seat patient in natural posture.
3. Sit or stand at the same eye level.
4. Ask the patient to look at your corresponding open eye.
5. Mark pupil centre with a fine non-permanent pen.
6. Step back about 1 metre and check binocularly.
7. Recheck after the patient moves and resettles the frame.
8. Measure each height independently.

The deck emphasises natural head posture. Do not make the patient raise or lower the chin to meet your measuring position.

## 5.3 Lens inset

The practical deck includes “marking the lens insert.” In PAL design, the near zone is normally inset nasally to align with convergence. Many modern designs calculate inset from monocular PD, fitting height, prescription, vertex distance, and working distance. The order must supply the measurements required by the manufacturer.

## 5.4 Check the cut-out

Before ordering:

- place the frame shape over the layout chart;
- align fitting cross and monocular PD position;
- confirm minimum fitting height;
- confirm sufficient near-zone coverage;
- confirm blank diameter and decentration;
- check both lenses separately.

A lens can have enough total frame depth yet still fail cut-out because the pupil is unusually high or low in the shape.

## 5.5 Order record

Record:

- full distance prescription and add;
- monocular PDs;
- right and left fitting heights;
- frame A, B, DBL/ED where required;
- vertex distance, pantoscopic tilt, and wrap for personalised designs;
- lens material, coating, design, corridor, and tint;
- occupational requirements;
- prior lens details when duplicating a successful pair.

<a id="verification-delivery"></a>
# 6. Verification and Delivery

## 6.1 Bench verification

Using restored markings and the layout chart, verify:

- lens identity and right/left assignment;
- distance power and cylinder axis at the distance reference point;
- prescribed prism and design prism at the prism reference point;
- add at the near reference point;
- fitting cross heights;
- monocular centration;
- lens material/coating/tint;
- surface quality and scratches;
- frame alignment, screws, and mounting stress.

## 6.2 On-eye assessment

Replace the frame in its correct wearing position and check:

- frame fit and stability;
- fitting cross in front of each pupil;
- distance vision in primary gaze;
- near vision with natural downgaze;
- intermediate task access;
- whether the patient can locate zones without extreme posture.

## 6.3 Delivery instructions

Explain that the wearer should:

- point the nose toward the object when using narrow peripheral zones;
- use the upper lens for distance;
- lower the eyes through the corridor for intermediate;
- lower the eyes further for near;
- move the head rather than looking far sideways through peripheral distortion;
- wear the new pair consistently during adaptation;
- take care on stairs and uneven ground until familiar.

The main deck suggests that adaptation may take at least two weeks and sometimes longer than a month. A persistent or severe problem should be investigated rather than dismissed as adaptation.

## 6.4 Communication at delivery

The practical deck recommends:

- positive language;
- removing temporary markings after final on-eye verification;
- maintaining the order record;
- explaining expected adaptation before a complaint develops.

<a id="pal-troubleshooting"></a>
# 7. Systematic PAL Troubleshooting

## 7.1 General rule

Do not begin by remaking lenses. Recreate the wearing conditions and isolate the cause.

### Troubleshooting sequence

1. Listen to the exact complaint.
2. Establish which distance and gaze direction fail.
3. Confirm the prescription and lens identity.
4. Restore and inspect reference markings.
5. Check monocular PDs and fitting heights on the face.
6. Reassess frame level, vertex distance, tilt, and wrap.
7. Observe the patient's head and eye movements.
8. Compare with previous successful lenses.
9. Adjust the frame where appropriate.
10. Refit/remake only after the cause is identified.

## 7.2 Source-listed error categories

- wrong heights;
- wrong monocular PDs;
- swapped lenses;
- wrong power or cylinder axis;
- poor frame adjustment;
- unsuitable design or add;
- incomplete counselling.

## 7.3 Complaint: blurry distance vision

Source-listed possible causes:

- fitting too high;
- incorrect prescription.

Possible response:

- lower frame/fitting position where adjustment is valid, for example by opening nose pads;
- verify refraction and remake if the prescription or manufactured power is wrong.

## 7.4 Complaint: blurry near vision

Possible causes:

- fitting too low;
- inadequate pantoscopic tilt;
- incorrect prescription/add.

Possible responses:

- raise frame, for example by bringing in nose pads;
- correct frame adjustment;
- remake when measurements or power are wrong.

## 7.5 Complaint: “swimmy” sensation

Possible causes listed:

- incorrect PDs or heights;
- inadequate pantoscopic tilt;
- facial wrap error;
- adaptation.

Possible responses listed:

- correct facial wrap;
- correct pantoscopic tilt;
- verify measurements and design;
- provide realistic adaptation guidance only after technical errors are excluded.

## 7.6 Complaint: reading area too small

Possible causes:

- incorrect PD;
- fitting height too low;
- design/corridor mismatch.

Possible responses:

- frame adjustment if it restores the intended height;
- refit/remake with corrected measurements or a more suitable design.

## 7.7 Complaint: must look to one side to read

Possible causes:

- incorrect monocular PD;
- right and left lenses swapped.

Response:

- refit with correct monocular PDs;
- swap lenses only when frame symmetry and lens geometry make that technically appropriate; otherwise remake.

## 7.8 Complaint: must tilt head far back to read

The source suggests:

- incorrect PD;
- add too weak;
- unsuitable design.

Responses include raising the frame/bringing in pads, reassessing add, and refitting.

## 7.9 Complaint: must tilt head backward for distance

The source associates this with:

- undercorrected hyperope;
- overcorrected myope.

Verify refraction and lens power.

## 7.10 Complaint: must tilt head forward for reading

The source associates this with an add that is too strong. Repeat near assessment and verify manufactured add.

## 7.11 Complaint: must tilt head forward for distance

The source associates this with fitting height too high. Reassess frame position and measured height.

## 7.12 Final mastery checklist

You should be able to:

- describe distance, corridor, near, and peripheral zones;
- calculate the lecture rate of power change;
- explain why short corridors are more demanding;
- locate all major PAL reference points;
- select and adjust a suitable frame before measuring;
- take monocular PDs and separate fitting heights;
- perform a cut-out check;
- verify a finished PAL at the correct reference points;
- teach a patient how to use the lens;
- troubleshoot complaints by linking symptom, gaze, measurement, and frame position;
- distinguish adaptation from a remediable technical error.
