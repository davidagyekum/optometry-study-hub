## How to use these notes

Ocular blood supply is easier to remember when it is organised by **territory**
rather than as a list of vessel names.

The eye uses two major arterial systems:

```text
central retinal circulation
→ mainly inner retina

posterior ciliary / choroidal circulation
→ choroid, outer retina, uvea and important optic-nerve-head regions
```

The two systems begin from branches of the ophthalmic artery but are not
interchangeable. An occlusion produces damage according to the tissue normally
supplied.

Study every vessel by asking:

1. Where does it arise?
2. What route does it take?
3. Which tissue does it supply?
4. Where does the venous blood leave?
5. What clinical pattern follows if flow or barrier function fails?

The focus labels mean:

- **Must know:** central vessel, territory, barrier or clinical relationship.
- **Should know:** supporting microscopic and anastomotic detail.
- **Useful extension:** additional depth that should not replace the main map.

> **Source approach:** The supplied Blood Supply to the Eye lecture is the
> primary course source. Repository-aligned clarifications are labelled where a
> phrase such as “no leakage” or “impermeable” could otherwise be learned as an
> absolute.

# 1. Arterial Origins and the Two Ocular Systems

## Big picture

The eye has a high demand for oxygen and glucose. Photoreceptors, retinal
neurons, the retinal pigment epithelium and the optic nerve head must receive
continuous perfusion.

The course traces the main ocular supply from:

```text
internal carotid artery
→ ophthalmic artery
→ central retinal artery
   + posterior ciliary arteries
```

These branches create two major systems:

- an **inner retinal system** supplied by the central retinal artery;
- a **uveal/choroidal system** supplied largely by short and long posterior
  ciliary arteries.

The outer retina depends mainly on diffusion from the choroid even though it is
part of the retina.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- ophthalmic artery as a branch of the internal carotid artery;
- central retinal artery (CRA);
- short posterior ciliary arteries (SPCAs);
- long posterior ciliary arteries (LPCAs);
- inner retinal versus uveal/choroidal supply;
- indirect choroidal supply of outer retina;
- why the two systems produce layer-specific ischaemia;
- retina and optic nerve head as metabolically vulnerable tissues.

### Should know

- ophthalmic artery as the common proximal ocular source;
- deeper optic-nerve-head contribution from posterior ciliary circulation;
- why “dual supply” does not mean every retinal layer has two equal backups.

### Useful extension

- how vascular territories help localise an occlusion;
- why a high-energy tissue can be injured rapidly when perfusion is interrupted.

## 1.1 Why the eye needs specialised circulation

Ocular tissues must preserve transparency while supporting intense metabolism.
The retina cannot simply be filled with large vessels because light must pass
through it.

The vascular design therefore separates supply:

- retinal vessels travel within the inner retina;
- the choroid forms a highly vascular bed behind the retinal pigment
  epithelium;
- the foveal centre remains avascular;
- the optic nerve head receives a regionally organised supply.

### Cause–effect chain

```text
high oxygen and glucose demand
+ limited energy reserve
→ continuous perfusion is required
→ arterial interruption rapidly impairs neuronal function
→ vision can fall suddenly
```

## 1.2 Ophthalmic artery

The **ophthalmic artery** arises from the internal carotid arterial system and
supplies the orbit and eye.

The source highlights three major branch groups for this module:

- central retinal artery;
- short posterior ciliary arteries;
- long posterior ciliary arteries.

Other orbital branches exist, but the present learning goal is the retinal and
uveal circulation.

## 1.3 Central retinal artery system

The **central retinal artery (CRA)** enters the optic nerve behind the globe,
travels within it and branches over the inner retinal surface after entering the
eye.

Its main territory is the inner retina.

A simple map is:

```text
ophthalmic artery
→ central retinal artery
→ retinal arterioles
→ capillary networks
→ venules
→ central retinal vein
```

The retinal circulation has few effective overlapping collaterals. This makes
arterial occlusion especially dangerous.

## 1.4 Posterior ciliary arterial system

Posterior ciliary arteries supply:

- choroid;
- ciliary body and iris through related anterior pathways;
- outer retina indirectly through choroidal diffusion;
- important regions of the optic nerve head.

The system includes:

- multiple short posterior ciliary arteries;
- two main long posterior ciliary arteries, medial and lateral.

## 1.5 Inner versus outer retina

The source states:

- retinal circulation supplies approximately the inner two-thirds of retinal
  depth;
- choroidal circulation nourishes photoreceptors and the outer retinal layers.

Treat “two-thirds” as an anatomical teaching approximation. The durable
territorial distinction is:

| Retinal region | Principal blood source |
|---|---|
| inner retinal neurons and nerve-fibre regions | central retinal circulation |
| photoreceptors and outer retinal layers | choriocapillaris/choroidal circulation |
| retinal pigment epithelium | receives metabolites from fenestrated choroid across Bruch/RPE relationships |

### Common trap

**Wrong idea:** “The central retinal artery supplies the whole retina.”

**Correction:** It mainly supplies inner retina. Outer retina depends largely
on choroidal circulation.

## 1.6 Uveal tract

The **uveal tract** comprises:

- iris;
- ciliary body;
- choroid.

The posterior ciliary circulation is central to this vascular system. Long
posterior ciliary and anterior ciliary arteries contribute to the anterior uveal
circulation and major arterial circle of the iris.

## 1.7 Optic nerve head

The source links short posterior ciliary arteries to the optic nerve head and
the **circle of Zinn–Haller**.

This explains why optic-nerve-head perfusion is not simply an extension of the
central retinal artery.

> **Course qualification:** Optic-nerve-head blood supply is regionally complex.
> For this module, remember the important posterior ciliary contribution rather
> than assuming a single vessel supplies every layer.

## 1.8 Dual supply does not mean equal redundancy

The eye has two systems, but each has its own territory. If one system fails,
the other cannot necessarily replace it.

```text
CRA occlusion
→ inner retinal ischaemia
→ choroid may remain perfused
→ outer-retinal supply alone cannot sustain the inner retina

choroidal hypoperfusion
→ photoreceptors/outer retina threatened
→ retinal vessels do not fully substitute
```

## Worked example: localising from retinal depth

A vascular insult produces severe inner-retinal ischaemia while the choroid
continues to fill.

Most likely system:

```text
central retinal circulation
```

A different insult selectively compromises photoreceptor and outer-retinal
support with relatively preserved inner retinal vessels.

Most likely system:

```text
choroidal/posterior ciliary circulation
```

## Common traps

### “Ophthalmic artery is a branch of the external carotid artery.”

The main ophthalmic artery arises from the internal carotid system.

### “Posterior ciliary arteries supply only the iris.”

They supply the choroid, ciliary/iris circulation, outer retina indirectly and
important optic-nerve-head regions.

### “The presence of two systems guarantees collateral protection.”

The systems serve different layers and have limited ability to substitute for
one another.

### “The retina is supplied entirely from vessels visible on funduscopy.”

Visible retinal vessels chiefly represent the inner-retinal circulation. Outer
retina depends on the choroid.

## Key terms

- **Internal carotid artery** — proximal arterial source of the ophthalmic
  artery.
- **Ophthalmic artery** — major arterial supply of the orbit and eye.
- **Central retinal artery (CRA)** — artery supplying inner retinal
  circulation.
- **Short posterior ciliary artery (SPCA)** — posterior ciliary branch supplying
  choroid and important optic-nerve-head regions.
- **Long posterior ciliary artery (LPCA)** — medial or lateral artery running
  forward toward ciliary body and iris.
- **Uveal tract** — iris, ciliary body and choroid.
- **Choroid** — vascular pigmented tissue supporting outer retina.
- **Perfusion** — delivery of blood through a tissue.
- **Ischaemia** — inadequate tissue blood supply.
- **Vascular territory** — tissue region supplied principally by a vessel or
  vessel system.

## Memory hook

**O-C-P**

```text
Ophthalmic artery
→ Central retinal system
→ Posterior ciliary system
```

Territories:

**Central = inner. Choroid = outer.**

## One-minute summary

- The ophthalmic artery arises from the internal carotid system.
- Its central retinal and posterior ciliary branches create two major ocular
  circulations.
- CRA supplies mainly inner retina.
- Posterior ciliary arteries supply choroid, anterior uveal structures, outer
  retina indirectly and important optic-nerve-head regions.
- The two systems serve different layers and are not complete substitutes.
- High metabolic demand makes retinal and optic-nerve tissue vulnerable to
  interrupted perfusion.

# 2. Ciliary and Choroidal Circulation

## Big picture

The posterior ciliary circulation supplies the vascular coat of the eye and
connects posterior structures to the anterior uvea.

The key comparison is:

```text
short posterior ciliary arteries
→ numerous, mainly posterior supply

long posterior ciliary arteries
→ two main vessels, run forward toward iris and ciliary body
```

The short and long systems communicate through the choriocapillaris and
anterior arterial circles. Venous blood leaves the choroid mainly through vortex
veins.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- SPCA versus LPCA;
- posterior versus anterior choriocapillaris;
- outer retinal support from choroid;
- optic-nerve-head/circle of Zinn–Haller relationship;
- LPCA plus anterior ciliary contribution to the major arterial circle of the
  iris;
- vortex veins as principal choroidal venous drainage;
- circumciliary flush in anterior uveal inflammation.

### Should know

- medial and lateral LPCAs;
- continuity between posterior and anterior choriocapillaris;
- why choroidal vessels are not seen like retinal vessels through a normal
  fundus;
- the distinction between deep anterior ciliary injection and superficial
  conjunctival redness.

### Useful extension

- how focal posterior ciliary compromise can affect choroid or optic nerve head;
- why anterior ciliary and long posterior ciliary networks provide anastomotic
  supply to iris/ciliary body.

## 2.1 Short posterior ciliary arteries

Multiple **short posterior ciliary arteries** enter the posterior globe around
the optic nerve.

The source assigns them three high-yield roles:

1. supply posterior choroid and choriocapillaris;
2. support the outer retina indirectly;
3. contribute to optic-nerve-head circulation and the circle of Zinn–Haller.

### Posterior choriocapillaris

The **choriocapillaris** is the innermost capillary layer of the choroid, lying
next to Bruch’s membrane and the RPE.

Its fenestrated capillaries support high metabolic exchange with the RPE and
photoreceptors.

## 2.2 Long posterior ciliary arteries

There are two major **long posterior ciliary arteries**:

- medial;
- lateral.

They pass forward through the choroidal/suprachoroidal region toward the ciliary
body and iris.

Their main course role is anterior uveal supply.

## 2.3 Major arterial circle of the iris

The source states that LPCAs and anterior ciliary arteries combine to form the
**major arterial circle of the iris (MCI)**.

This circle supplies iris and ciliary-body regions through branching networks.

```text
medial + lateral LPCA
+ anterior ciliary arteries
→ major arterial circle
→ iris and ciliary-body circulation
```

Anastomosis provides connection, but it does not make every segment immune to
ischaemia.

## 2.4 Anterior and posterior choriocapillaris

The deck describes:

- posterior choriocapillaris arising mainly from SPCAs;
- anterior choriocapillaris related to LPCA supply;
- continuity between the two.

This is a territorial teaching model. The choroid is a connected vascular bed,
but segmental watershed behaviour can still occur.

## 2.5 Circle of Zinn–Haller

The source links SPCAs with an arterial circle around the optic nerve head,
commonly called the **circle of Zinn–Haller**.

High-yield role:

- peripapillary/optic-nerve-head supply.

> **Course qualification:** The circle can be anatomically variable and its
> contribution is regional. Learn the posterior ciliary relationship rather
> than imagining one perfectly complete circular vessel in every eye.

## 2.6 Vortex veins

Venous drainage of the choroid occurs mainly through **vortex veins**.

A vortex vein collects blood from broad choroidal territories and exits through
the sclera.

### Artery–vein distinction

```text
posterior ciliary arteries
→ deliver blood to choroid

vortex veins
→ drain blood from choroid
```

This distinction is important because an aqueous slide uses “vortical veins” in
an alternate-outflow list. Their principal anatomical identity is choroidal
venous drainage.

## 2.7 Circumciliary flush

A **circumciliary flush/ciliary injection** is a deeper red–violaceous ring
around the limbus.

The source associates it with inflammation of:

- iris;
- ciliary body;
- anterior uvea.

It reflects dilation of deeper anterior ciliary/episcleral vascular networks
rather than only superficial conjunctival vessels.

### Compare redness patterns

| Feature | Circumciliary flush | Superficial conjunctival injection |
|---|---|---|
| Greatest colour | around limbus | often more diffuse or toward fornices |
| Vessel depth | deeper anterior ciliary/episcleral | superficial conjunctival |
| Typical concern | anterior uveitis, keratitis, acute angle disease | surface irritation/conjunctivitis patterns |
| Mobility with conjunctiva | relatively less mobile | superficial vessels may move with conjunctiva |

The complete diagnosis requires symptoms and examination, not colour alone.

## 2.8 Clinical territory map

| Vessel/bed | Principal course territory | Example consequence of compromise |
|---|---|---|
| SPCA | posterior choroid, outer retina indirectly, optic nerve head | choroidal or optic-nerve-head ischaemia |
| LPCA | forward route to ciliary body and iris | anterior uveal perfusion disturbance |
| Anterior ciliary arteries | anterior uvea and major circle contribution | deep anterior injection/inflammation relationship |
| Vortex veins | choroidal venous drainage | congestion if drainage is impaired |

## Worked example: distinguishing two red eyes

### Patient A

- diffuse superficial redness;
- discharge;
- minimal photophobia.

This pattern may favour conjunctival surface disease.

### Patient B

- deep red–violaceous ring around the limbus;
- pain and photophobia;
- anterior-chamber inflammation.

This pattern fits circumciliary flush from deeper anterior ciliary circulation.

The colour pattern guides localisation but does not replace examination.

## Common traps

### “Short and long posterior ciliary arteries are distinguished only by vessel
length.”

They differ in number, course and principal territory.

### “Vortex veins supply the choroid.”

They drain it.

### “The major arterial circle is formed only by LPCAs.”

Anterior ciliary arteries also contribute.

### “Circumciliary flush is simply severe conjunctivitis.”

It represents deeper perilimbal vascular injection and should raise concern for
anterior segment disease.

### “The circle of Zinn–Haller is the same as the major iris circle.”

The former is peripapillary/optic-nerve-head related; the latter is anterior
uveal.

## Key terms

- **Choriocapillaris** — fenestrated capillary bed of the inner choroid.
- **Short posterior ciliary artery (SPCA)** — one of multiple posterior vessels
  supplying choroid and optic-nerve-head regions.
- **Long posterior ciliary artery (LPCA)** — medial or lateral vessel running
  anteriorly toward ciliary body and iris.
- **Major arterial circle of the iris** — anastomotic ring formed by long
  posterior and anterior ciliary contributions.
- **Circle of Zinn–Haller** — peripapillary arterial anastomotic system related
  to SPCAs.
- **Vortex vein** — principal choroidal collecting vein.
- **Anterior ciliary artery** — anterior uveal vessel contributing to the major
  iris circle.
- **Circumciliary flush** — deep perilimbal injection associated with anterior
  uveal/corneal/angle disease.
- **Anastomosis** — connection between vessels.
- **Watershed zone** — boundary region between vascular territories that may be
  vulnerable when perfusion falls.

## Memory hook

**Short stays posterior; long goes forward.**

```text
SPCA → choroid + optic nerve head
LPCA → ciliary body + iris
Vortex → venous exit
```

## One-minute summary

- SPCAs are multiple posterior vessels supplying choroid, outer retina
  indirectly and optic-nerve-head regions.
- Two LPCAs run forward to anterior uveal structures.
- LPCAs and anterior ciliary arteries form the major arterial circle of the
  iris.
- Posterior and anterior choriocapillaris regions are continuous.
- Vortex veins drain the choroid.
- The circle of Zinn–Haller is peripapillary, not the iris circle.
- Circumciliary flush reflects deeper perilimbal circulation in anterior
  segment inflammation.

# 3. Retinal Circulation and the Foveal Avascular Zone

## Big picture

The central retinal artery enters the optic nerve behind the globe and
distributes over the retina as arterioles, capillaries and venules.

The retinal circulation is specialised for neural tissue:

- vessels are small and tightly regulated;
- capillaries are non-fenestrated;
- effective collateral overlap is limited;
- the foveal centre is avascular.

These features preserve visual function but make arterial occlusion dangerous.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- CRA entry into the optic nerve approximately 10 mm behind the globe;
- retinal arterial branching;
- central retinal vein as the main venous partner;
- limited effective anastomosis/collateral overlap;
- inner retinal territory;
- choroidal support of outer retina;
- foveal avascular zone (FAZ);
- why CRA occlusion is an ocular emergency.

### Should know

- arterioles flowing through capillary loops into venules;
- the optical benefit of an avascular foveal centre;
- the source observation of reduced capillary density around larger vessels;
- why vessel appearance in the fundus does not show the whole retinal supply.

### Useful extension

- branch-level versus central occlusion patterns;
- how FAZ alteration can affect central vision.

## 3.1 CRA course

The source states that the **central retinal artery** enters the optic nerve
about **10 mm posterior to the globe**.

It travels with the central retinal vein and emerges within the eye to branch
over the retinal surface.

> **Course qualification:** The exact entry distance varies. “Approximately
> 10 mm” is the lecture landmark.

## 3.2 Branching pattern

After entering the eye, the CRA divides into retinal arterial branches.
Arterioles feed capillary networks, which drain into venules and the central
retinal vein.

```text
CRA
→ retinal arterioles
→ capillary beds
→ venules
→ central retinal vein
```

Retinal vessels are visible because they lie within the transparent retina.

## 3.3 Limited collateral circulation

The lecture emphasises very few overlapping or redundant retinal anastomoses.

This means:

```text
arterial branch becomes occluded
→ downstream territory loses perfusion
→ neighbouring branches cannot reliably replace it
→ neural tissue becomes ischaemic
```

The word “end artery” is often used clinically to describe the limited
collateral behaviour of the CRA system.

## 3.4 Retinal depth

The course map is:

- central retinal circulation → inner retinal layers;
- choroidal circulation → photoreceptors and outer retina.

The outer retina is avascular but receives oxygen and nutrients by diffusion
from the choriocapillaris across RPE/Bruch relationships.

### Why this matters

A vascular event can be localised by the layers damaged.

- inner retinal swelling/ischaemia → central retinal circulation;
- photoreceptor/outer retinal dysfunction → choroidal circulation.

## 3.5 Foveal avascular zone

The **foveal avascular zone (FAZ)** is the capillary-free region at the centre
of the fovea.

Its functional significance includes:

- avoiding vascular obstruction of the central optical path;
- supporting specialised foveal architecture;
- allowing densely packed cones and inner-layer displacement.

The foveal centre still requires nutrients. It receives them largely by
diffusion from the choriocapillaris and surrounding retinal capillary networks.

### Common trap

**Wrong idea:** “Avascular means metabolically inactive.”

**Correction:** The fovea is highly specialised and metabolically active; it is
supported without vessels crossing its centre.

## 3.6 Perivascular avascular spaces

The source notes capillary-free areas around retinal arteries, suggesting that
oxygen diffusing from the larger vessel may reduce the need for adjacent
capillaries.

Treat this as a course observation. The exact capillary-free architecture
varies across retinal layers and regions.

## 3.7 Optical and metabolic compromise

The retinal vascular arrangement balances two competing needs:

```text
sufficient blood supply
+ minimal optical obstruction
```

The solution is a layered vascular network with an avascular foveal centre and
a separate choroidal supply behind the retina.

## 3.8 Central retinal artery occlusion

A CRA occlusion interrupts inner-retinal perfusion.

Typical high-yield presentation:

- sudden;
- painless;
- monocular;
- severe visual loss.

This requires urgent assessment because retinal neural tissue is highly
time-sensitive.

The notes do not provide a treatment protocol; the learning point is emergency
recognition and vascular localisation.

## Worked example: explaining the FAZ

A student asks, “If the fovea has no capillaries, why does it not die?”

Answer:

```text
foveal centre has no retinal capillaries
but
photoreceptors and outer retina receive choroidal diffusion
and
nearby capillary networks support surrounding inner retina
```

Avascularity protects optical quality; it does not mean absence of nutrient
delivery.

## Common traps

### “CRA enters the eye without travelling through the optic nerve.”

It enters and courses within the optic nerve before reaching the retina.

### “Retinal vessels have extensive collaterals.”

Effective overlap is limited, making occlusion hazardous.

### “FAZ has no blood supply of any kind.”

It lacks vessels crossing its centre but receives support by diffusion,
especially from choroid.

### “Outer retina is supplied by visible retinal arterioles.”

It depends mainly on choroidal circulation.

### “All sudden monocular loss is retinal artery occlusion.”

The presentation is a vascular emergency pattern, but differential diagnosis
requires urgent examination.

## Key terms

- **Central retinal artery (CRA)** — artery supplying inner retina.
- **Central retinal vein (CRV)** — principal venous drainage of retinal
  circulation.
- **Retinal arteriole** — resistance vessel branching from CRA.
- **Retinal venule** — vessel collecting capillary blood.
- **Collateral circulation** — alternate vascular route around an obstruction.
- **End-artery behaviour** — limited effective collateral replacement.
- **Foveal avascular zone (FAZ)** — capillary-free central foveal region.
- **Inner retina** — retinal layers supplied mainly by CRA circulation.
- **Outer retina** — photoreceptor-related layers supplied mainly by choroid.
- **Monocular** — affecting one eye.

## Memory hook

**CRA: Central artery, inner retina, abrupt risk.**

For the FAZ:

**No vessel in the centre, but diffusion still delivers.**

## One-minute summary

- CRA enters the optic nerve roughly 10 mm behind the globe.
- It branches into retinal arterioles, capillaries and venules.
- Retinal collateral overlap is limited.
- CRA circulation supplies mainly inner retina.
- Choroid supports photoreceptors and outer retina.
- The FAZ keeps vessels out of the foveal centre while diffusion sustains the
  tissue.
- CRA occlusion classically causes sudden painless monocular visual loss and
  requires urgent assessment.

# 4. Blood–Ocular and Blood–Retina Barriers

## Big picture

A vascular barrier is not a wall that blocks every molecule. It is a
specialised interface that controls what moves between blood and tissue.

The supplied lecture concentrates on the blood–retina barrier:

- **inner barrier:** tight junctions between non-fenestrated retinal capillary
  endothelial cells;
- **outer barrier:** tight junctions between retinal pigment epithelial cells.

Behind the outer barrier, the choriocapillaris is fenestrated and relatively
permeable so it can deliver metabolites to the RPE and photoreceptors.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- fenestrated versus continuous capillaries;
- retinal capillaries as continuous/non-fenestrated;
- choroidal and ciliary capillaries as fenestrated;
- inner blood–retina barrier;
- outer blood–retina barrier;
- RPE tight junctions;
- why choriocapillaris permeability is necessary;
- barrier breakdown and retinal oedema/leakage.

### Should know

- lipid-soluble molecules and gases crossing cell membranes;
- small and larger pore concepts in capillaries;
- why “no leakage” is a functional shorthand rather than absolute
  impermeability;
- relationship to angiographic leakage.

### Useful extension

- how barrier integrity preserves the neural retinal environment;
- distinction between blood–retina barrier and blood–aqueous barrier.

## 4.1 Why a barrier is needed

Retinal neurons require a stable extracellular environment.

Uncontrolled plasma entry would change:

- ion concentration;
- fluid balance;
- protein content;
- tissue transparency;
- neuronal signalling.

The barrier therefore permits regulated exchange while limiting paracellular
leakage.

## 4.2 Capillary types

### Fenestrated capillaries

Fenestrated capillaries contain pores through endothelial cells and permit
relatively rapid fluid and solute exchange.

Ocular examples in the source:

- choroid/choriocapillaris;
- ciliary body.

### Continuous capillaries

Continuous capillaries have non-fenestrated endothelial cells joined by tight
junctions.

Ocular example:

- retinal capillaries.

### Discontinuous capillaries

The source states that discontinuous/sinusoidal capillaries are not found as a
normal ocular capillary type in this context.

## 4.3 Molecular movement

The source notes that:

- lipid-soluble substances can cross endothelial cell membranes;
- gases such as oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse readily;
- water movement depends on capillary structure and pathways;
- junction size and pore characteristics influence solute passage.

### Course qualification

Do not learn continuous retinal endothelium as absolutely “impermeable to
water.” Water and selected solutes move through regulated cellular and
paracellular mechanisms. The key difference is restricted uncontrolled
leakage.

## 4.4 Inner blood–retina barrier

The **inner blood–retina barrier (inner BRB)** is formed principally by tight
junctions—zona occludens—between retinal capillary endothelial cells.

Additional supporting elements include:

- basement membrane;
- pericytes;
- interactions with glial and neural tissue.

The high-yield answer remains: non-fenestrated retinal endothelial tight
junctions.

## 4.5 Outer blood–retina barrier

The **outer BRB** is formed by tight junctions between adjacent **retinal
pigment epithelial (RPE)** cells.

The RPE sits between:

- fenestrated choriocapillaris;
- photoreceptors/outer retina.

It controls exchange from a highly permeable vascular bed into neural retina.

## 4.6 Why the choriocapillaris is permeable

Photoreceptors and RPE have high metabolic requirements. Fenestrated choroidal
capillaries permit delivery of:

- oxygen;
- glucose;
- retinoid-related nutrients such as vitamin A derivatives;
- other small molecules and plasma-associated substances.

The RPE then performs selective transport and barrier functions.

```text
fenestrated choriocapillaris
→ abundant exchange toward RPE
→ RPE tight junctions and transport regulate retinal entry
→ outer retinal environment remains controlled
```

## 4.7 “No leakage” as a teaching shorthand

The slide describes the inner and outer barriers as “no leakage.”

> **Course qualification:** Intact barriers prevent uncontrolled vascular
> leakage under normal conditions. They still allow regulated movement of water,
> gases and nutrients. “No leakage” should not be interpreted as zero molecular
> exchange.

## 4.8 Barrier breakdown

When tight junctions or supporting cells fail:

- plasma fluid enters retinal tissue;
- proteins may accumulate;
- retinal thickness increases;
- neural architecture and transparency are disturbed;
- angiography may show leakage.

Clinical consequences can include retinal oedema and reduced vision.

## 4.9 Inner versus outer barrier

| Feature | Inner BRB | Outer BRB |
|---|---|---|
| Barrier-forming cell | retinal capillary endothelial cell | RPE cell |
| Junction | endothelial tight junction | RPE tight junction |
| Adjacent vascular bed | retinal capillary | fenestrated choriocapillaris |
| Main controlled interface | blood to inner retina | choroid to outer retina |
| Failure result | inner retinal leakage/oedema | outer retinal/subretinal fluid disturbance |

## 4.10 Blood–aqueous distinction

The previous module described the **blood–aqueous barrier**, which regulates
entry into aqueous from iris/ciliary circulation.

Do not confuse:

- blood–aqueous barrier → aqueous chambers;
- blood–retina barriers → neural retina.

They share the principle of selective exchange but involve different
structures.

## Worked example: explaining fluorescein leakage

A retinal capillary has damaged endothelial tight junctions.

```text
inner BRB integrity falls
→ dye and plasma fluid escape beyond normal vascular boundary
→ angiographic leakage appears
→ retinal oedema may develop
```

If choriocapillaris is fenestrated normally, why does outer retina not fill with
plasma?

Because the RPE forms the outer BRB and actively regulates transport.

## Common traps

### “Fenestrated choroid means the retina has no barrier.”

The RPE forms the outer barrier.

### “The inner BRB is formed by retinal pigment epithelium.”

The inner barrier is retinal capillary endothelium; RPE forms the outer barrier.

### “A barrier prevents oxygen and glucose from crossing.”

It permits regulated metabolic exchange.

### “Retinal capillaries are fenestrated like choriocapillaris.”

They are continuous/non-fenestrated with tight junctions.

### “Blood–aqueous and blood–retina barriers are the same.”

They are distinct ocular interfaces.

## Key terms

- **Blood–retina barrier (BRB)** — regulated interface restricting uncontrolled
  movement from blood into retina.
- **Inner BRB** — tight-junction barrier of retinal capillary endothelium.
- **Outer BRB** — tight-junction barrier of RPE.
- **Fenestrated capillary** — capillary with endothelial pores.
- **Continuous capillary** — non-fenestrated capillary with continuous
  endothelium.
- **Zona occludens** — tight junction sealing the paracellular pathway.
- **Retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)** — pigmented epithelial monolayer
  supporting outer retina and forming outer BRB.
- **Choriocapillaris** — fenestrated choroidal capillary bed.
- **Permeability** — ease with which a substance crosses a barrier.
- **Oedema** — excess tissue fluid.
- **Angiographic leakage** — escape of contrast/dye beyond expected vascular
  boundaries.

## Memory hook

**Inner = endothelium. Outer = RPE.**

```text
retinal vessel tight junction
→ inner BRB

RPE tight junction
→ outer BRB
```

## One-minute summary

- Ocular barriers regulate exchange rather than blocking all molecules.
- Retinal capillaries are continuous and non-fenestrated.
- Choroidal and ciliary capillaries are fenestrated and more permeable.
- Inner BRB is formed mainly by retinal endothelial tight junctions.
- Outer BRB is formed by RPE tight junctions.
- Choriocapillaris permeability supports the high metabolic needs of RPE and
  photoreceptors.
- Barrier failure causes leakage, oedema and disturbed retinal function.
- Blood–aqueous and blood–retina barriers are distinct systems.

# 5. Retinal Arterioles and Capillaries

## Big picture

Retinal arterioles are **resistance vessels**: they regulate how much blood
reaches downstream tissue.

Retinal capillaries are **exchange vessels**: their thin walls place blood close
to neural tissue so gases, electrolytes and metabolites can move.

The change from arteriole to capillary is therefore a change from flow control
to tissue exchange.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- retinal arterioles as branches of CRA;
- approximate arteriole diameter near the optic nerve head;
- smooth-muscle layers decreasing toward the periphery;
- resistance-vessel function;
- capillary diameter and red-cell deformation;
- endothelial cell, basement membrane and pericyte;
- exchange-vessel function;
- pericyte/barrier relevance in diabetic retinopathy.

### Should know

- why capillary diameter approaches red-cell size;
- how arteriolar constriction changes downstream perfusion;
- basement membrane as structural support;
- distinction between arteriolar smooth muscle and capillary pericytes.

### Useful extension

- how microvascular injury can alter perfusion and permeability simultaneously;
- why capillary dropout near the fovea threatens central vision.

## 5.1 Retinal arterioles

Retinal arterioles arise from branches of the CRA.

The source gives an approximate lumen diameter near the optic nerve head of
about **100 μm**.

Their wall includes:

- endothelium;
- basement membrane;
- smooth muscle.

Near the optic nerve head, the source describes several smooth-muscle layers,
decreasing to fewer layers in peripheral branches.

> **Course qualification:** These numbers are anatomical approximations. The
> durable concept is progressive reduction in vessel calibre and muscular wall
> toward the capillary bed.

## 5.2 Resistance-vessel function

Smooth muscle changes arteriolar diameter.

Because resistance is highly sensitive to radius:

```text
arteriole narrows
→ resistance rises
→ downstream flow falls

arteriole widens
→ resistance falls
→ downstream flow can rise
```

The lecture calls retinal arterioles **resistance vessels**.

Do not confuse this use of “resistance” with trabecular aqueous-outflow
resistance; they belong to different systems.

## 5.3 Retinal capillaries

The source gives a capillary diameter of approximately **3.5–6 μm**.

A capillary wall includes:

- endothelial cells;
- basement membrane;
- intramural pericytes.

It lacks the multiple smooth-muscle layers of an arteriole.

## 5.4 Red-cell deformation

A red blood cell is similar in scale to or larger than the narrowest capillary
lumen. It must deform to pass through.

This close fit:

- slows and organises transit;
- places haemoglobin near the vessel wall;
- supports efficient gas exchange.

### Cause–effect chain

```text
very narrow capillary
→ red cell deforms and passes single file
→ short diffusion distance to tissue
→ oxygen and carbon dioxide exchange efficiently
```

## 5.5 Pericytes

**Pericytes** lie within or closely associated with the capillary basement
membrane.

High-yield roles include:

- capillary structural support;
- contribution to vascular stability;
- interaction with endothelial cells;
- influence on capillary tone and barrier integrity.

The lecture identifies pericytes as a defining capillary component.

### Repository-aligned clinical extension

Pericyte loss and endothelial dysfunction are important microvascular changes
in diabetic retinopathy. This can contribute to capillary instability,
microaneurysm formation, non-perfusion and leakage.

This clinical link is an extension of the source’s pericyte/barrier anatomy,
not a replacement for it.

## 5.6 Basement membrane

The capillary **basement membrane** supports endothelial cells and pericytes.

Pathologic thickening or biochemical change can impair normal exchange and
cell–cell relationships even when the lumen remains open.

## 5.7 Exchange-vessel function

Capillaries support movement of:

- oxygen and carbon dioxide;
- water and electrolytes;
- glucose and other metabolites;
- waste products.

Macromolecular movement is tightly restricted by the inner BRB under normal
conditions.

## 5.8 From arteriole to venule

```text
arteriole controls resistance
→ capillary network performs exchange
→ venule collects blood
```

A healthy microcirculation requires both adequate inflow and intact exchange
surfaces.

## 5.9 Microvascular failure patterns

### Reduced flow

Arteriolar occlusion or capillary non-perfusion deprives tissue of oxygen and
glucose.

### Excess leakage

Barrier/pericyte/endothelial damage permits fluid and protein to escape.

### Combined failure

Many diseases produce both ischaemia and oedema.

```text
capillary dropout
→ retinal hypoxia

remaining vessels become abnormal
→ permeability rises
→ oedema develops
```

## Worked example: resistance versus exchange problem

### Scenario A

A retinal arteriole constricts strongly.

Main immediate effect:

```text
increased resistance
→ reduced downstream perfusion
```

### Scenario B

Capillary endothelial tight junctions fail.

Main immediate effect:

```text
exchange barrier becomes abnormal
→ fluid/protein leak
→ retinal oedema
```

Both affect vision but through different mechanisms.

## Common traps

### “Retinal capillaries contain several smooth-muscle layers.”

Arterioles contain smooth muscle; capillaries contain endothelial cells,
basement membrane and pericytes.

### “Pericytes are free cells floating inside the lumen.”

They are mural cells associated with the outside of endothelial tubes within
the basement-membrane complex.

### “A larger arteriole is the main exchange site.”

Capillaries are the principal exchange vessels.

### “Narrow capillaries prevent red cells from entering.”

Red cells deform and pass through the lumen.

### “Microvascular disease causes either leakage or ischaemia, never both.”

The two commonly coexist.

## Key terms

- **Retinal arteriole** — small arterial resistance vessel branching from CRA.
- **Resistance vessel** — vessel whose calibre strongly regulates downstream
  flow.
- **Retinal capillary** — microscopic exchange vessel of inner retinal
  circulation.
- **Exchange vessel** — vessel specialised for movement between blood and
  tissue.
- **Endothelium** — cellular lining of a vessel.
- **Basement membrane** — extracellular support layer around vascular cells.
- **Pericyte** — mural capillary-associated cell supporting vascular stability.
- **Vasoconstriction** — reduction in vessel calibre.
- **Vasodilation** — increase in vessel calibre.
- **Capillary non-perfusion** — loss of effective blood flow through a capillary
  bed.
- **Microaneurysm** — focal capillary-wall outpouching associated with
  microvascular injury.

## Memory hook

**A-C-V**

```text
Arteriole = adjusts resistance
Capillary = carries out exchange
Venule = collects
```

## One-minute summary

- Retinal arterioles branch from CRA and regulate resistance.
- Their smooth-muscle wall becomes thinner toward the periphery.
- Capillaries are approximately red-cell scale and function as exchange vessels.
- Capillary walls contain endothelial cells, basement membrane and pericytes.
- Red cells deform to pass through narrow lumina.
- Pericytes support capillary stability and barrier relationships.
- Microvascular disease can cause both non-perfusion and leakage.
- Arteriolar and capillary disorders affect vision through different mechanisms.

<summary>Reveal answers</summary>

2. The number of smooth-muscle layers decreases as calibre falls.

# 6. Clinical Integration and Vascular Localisation

## Big picture

The clinical value of ocular vascular anatomy is localisation.

When vision changes or the eye becomes red, ask:

```text
which tissue is abnormal?
→ which vascular bed supplies it?
→ is the problem arterial inflow, venous drainage, microvascular exchange or
  barrier integrity?
```

The same symptom—reduced vision—can result from inner-retinal ischaemia,
outer-retinal/choroidal ischaemia, optic-nerve-head hypoperfusion, oedema or
haemorrhage. Anatomy narrows the possibilities.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- CRA compromise → inner retinal ischaemia;
- posterior ciliary/choroidal compromise → outer retina and selected optic
  nerve head regions;
- sudden painless monocular visual loss as an arterial emergency pattern;
- circumciliary flush as deep anterior segment vascular injection;
- barrier breakdown → retinal leakage/oedema;
- microcirculatory injury → ischaemia plus leakage;
- why urgent assessment is required for retinal arterial occlusion.

### Should know

- FAZ and central visual function;
- optic-nerve-head posterior ciliary contribution;
- anterior uveitis versus superficial conjunctival injection;
- pericyte loss and capillary non-perfusion in diabetic retinopathy.

### Useful extension

- how angiography reveals perfusion and leakage differently;
- why one vessel can affect a small sector while another affects most of the
  retina.

## 6.1 Central retinal arterial compromise

A central retinal arterial event primarily threatens the inner retina.

High-yield presentation:

- sudden onset;
- painless;
- one eye;
- marked visual loss.

The retina has limited collateral supply, so urgent evaluation is necessary.

### Anatomical reasoning

```text
CRA flow stops
→ inner retinal capillaries lose perfusion
→ inner retinal neurons fail
→ vision falls abruptly
```

The choroid may still be perfused, but it cannot fully rescue inner retina.

## 6.2 Branch-level compromise

If a branch rather than the central trunk is affected, injury follows that
branch territory.

The principle is:

```text
smaller branch occlusion
→ sectoral downstream ischaemia

central trunk occlusion
→ much broader inner-retinal ischaemia
```

This follows vascular mapping even though the supplied lecture focuses on the
central system.

## 6.3 Choroidal/posterior ciliary compromise

Posterior ciliary or choroidal hypoperfusion threatens:

- choroid;
- RPE;
- photoreceptors/outer retina;
- selected optic-nerve-head regions.

Symptoms and signs depend on the location and extent.

A normal-looking retinal arterial tree does not prove that choroidal perfusion
is normal.

## 6.4 Optic-nerve-head perfusion

The SPCA/Zinn–Haller relationship means posterior ciliary disease can affect
optic-nerve-head function.

This is a critical correction to the oversimplified idea that the CRA supplies
everything visible at the back of the eye.

## 6.5 Circumciliary flush and anterior uveitis

The source explicitly connects anterior uveitis with circumciliary flush.

Reasoning:

```text
iris/ciliary-body inflammation
→ deeper anterior ciliary vascular dilation
→ red–violaceous perilimbal injection
```

This is different from a purely superficial conjunctival pattern.

Redness alone is not diagnostic; pain, photophobia, corneal findings, pupil and
anterior-chamber signs matter.

## 6.6 Barrier breakdown and oedema

When inner or outer BRB integrity fails:

```text
fluid/protein leaves controlled vascular compartment
→ retinal tissue swells
→ neural alignment and optical transmission deteriorate
→ vision may blur or distort
```

Angiography can demonstrate leakage, while cross-sectional retinal imaging can
show thickening or fluid distribution.

The source emphasises the barrier mechanism; the imaging correlation is a
clinical extension.

## 6.7 Diabetic microvascular pattern

Repository-aligned clinical integration includes:

- pericyte loss;
- microaneurysms;
- endothelial dysfunction;
- capillary leakage;
- capillary non-perfusion;
- retinal ischaemia and oedema.

The important lesson is not a full diabetic-retinopathy classification. It is
that the capillary wall and barrier structures studied in the previous section
have direct disease consequences.

## 6.8 FAZ and central vision

The FAZ supports high-acuity architecture. Enlargement, distortion or oedema
near this zone can impair central vision.

The foveal centre depends on adjacent and choroidal supply, so both retinal and
choroidal microvascular disease can affect central performance.

## 6.9 Perfusion versus leakage on angiography

Two different questions can be asked:

### Is blood arriving?

Delayed or absent filling suggests a perfusion problem.

### Is dye staying within the vascular boundary?

Late spreading beyond normal vessel margins suggests leakage/barrier failure.

A vessel may fill but leak, fail to fill, or show both abnormalities.

## Worked cases

### Case 1: sudden painless monocular loss

- severe abrupt visual loss;
- no pain;
- concern for retinal arterial interruption.

Localisation:

```text
inner retina / central retinal arterial system
```

Action principle: urgent ocular and systemic vascular assessment.

### Case 2: deep perilimbal redness

- pain;
- photophobia;
- ciliary flush;
- anterior-chamber cells.

Localisation:

```text
iris/ciliary body and deeper anterior ciliary circulation
```

### Case 3: retinal thickening with dye leakage

- vessels fill;
- dye escapes;
- retinal oedema develops.

Localisation:

```text
blood–retina barrier / microvascular permeability failure
```

### Case 4: outer-retinal dysfunction with preserved visible retinal arteries

Localisation:

```text
choroidal/posterior ciliary circulation
```

## Common traps

### “All retinal vascular disease is visible as a blocked fundus artery.”

Choroidal and microvascular disease may not present as a visibly blocked major
retinal artery.

### “Sudden painless monocular loss can wait for routine review.”

It is an emergency vascular pattern.

### “Circumciliary flush is a diagnosis.”

It is a localisation clue requiring full anterior-segment assessment.

### “Leakage and non-perfusion are the same angiographic finding.”

Leakage means barrier failure; non-perfusion means inadequate filling.

### “If CRA is open, the optic nerve head is fully protected.”

Posterior ciliary circulation is important to optic-nerve-head regions.

## Key terms

- **Central retinal artery occlusion** — interruption of central retinal
  arterial flow.
- **Branch retinal artery occlusion** — interruption of a branch territory.
- **Choroidal ischaemia** — inadequate perfusion of choroidal tissue.
- **Optic-nerve-head ischaemia** — insufficient perfusion of optic disc regions.
- **Circumciliary flush** — deep perilimbal injection.
- **Barrier breakdown** — loss of controlled vascular permeability.
- **Retinal oedema** — excess fluid within retinal tissue.
- **Capillary dropout** — loss of functioning capillary segments.
- **Angiographic filling** — entry of contrast into a vascular bed.
- **Angiographic leakage** — escape of contrast beyond normal vessel/barrier
  confines.
- **Sectoral defect** — abnormality following part of a vascular territory.

## Memory hook

Localise with **I-O-B-A**:

```text
Inner retina → central retinal artery
Outer retina → choroid
Barrier failure → oedema/leakage
Anterior uvea → circumciliary flush
```

## One-minute summary

- CRA compromise primarily injures inner retina and can cause sudden painless
  monocular visual loss.
- Choroidal/posterior ciliary compromise threatens outer retina and selected
  optic-nerve-head regions.
- Branch occlusion follows a sector; central occlusion is broader.
- Circumciliary flush localises deeper anterior segment vascular involvement.
- BRB failure causes leakage and retinal oedema.
- Pericyte/endothelial injury can cause both capillary non-perfusion and
  leakage.
- FAZ distortion affects central visual architecture.
- Ocular vascular emergencies require urgent assessment.
