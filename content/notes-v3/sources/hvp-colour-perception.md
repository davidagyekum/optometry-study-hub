# HVP Notes V3 — Colour Perception

Source: `5.1- Colour Perception.pptx`, 44-slide lecture deck.

<a id="colour-foundations"></a>

## Big picture

Colour perception is the ability to discriminate differences in wavelength of light independent of light intensity. The lecture presents colour vision as the ability to respond to wavelength differences and to discriminate stimuli on the basis of hue regardless of brightness or number of quanta.

<a id="visible-spectrum-cones"></a>

## Big picture

Visible light is only a narrow part of the electromagnetic spectrum. The deck gives the visible range as approximately **380 nm to 780 nm**. The visual system codes different wavelengths as different colour experiences.

The lecture makes a strong conceptual point: **colour is a psychological phenomenon**. Objects are not coloured in themselves, and light rays are not literally coloured. Objects appear coloured because they reflect light from particular parts of the visible spectrum, and the nervous system creates the perceptual experience of colour. This framing should be taught carefully: it does not mean wavelengths are imaginary; it means colour is the perceptual code produced by the eye and brain.

<a id="importance-of-colour"></a>

## Big picture

The deck states that colour vision has high survival value in primates. Colour improves visibility, makes objects perceptually unique, helps detect borders and supports discrimination. It helps organisms identify food, mating partners, threats, predators, prey and biological signals. It also has aesthetic value.

The main practical message is that colour supports **detection and discrimination**. It makes objects easier to separate from their surroundings and helps define object borders.

<a id="colour-attributes"></a>

## Big picture

Colour is usually described along three perceptual dimensions: **hue**, **saturation** and **brightness**.

**Hue** is the attribute most closely associated with wavelength. It distinguishes red, yellow, green, blue, orange and other named hues. The lecture gives examples: 545 nm may be perceived as green, while 580 nm may be perceived as yellow. Hue and colour are not the same: colour is broader because it also includes saturation and brightness.

**Saturation** refers to wavelength purity or the amount of greyness mixed with a hue. A highly saturated colour has little or no grey and appears vivid. A desaturated colour appears pale or washed out.

**Brightness** or lightness refers to luminous intensity and the lightness or darkness of a hue, ranging from black to white.

A useful lecture-style analogy: hue distinguishes ripe and unripe bananas, saturation helps compare how diluted a drink looks, and brightness helps distinguish bread from toast or burnt toast.

<a id="colour-constancy"></a>

## Big picture

**Colour constancy** means that an object’s colour tends to remain stable even when the spectrum of illumination changes, such as moving from outdoors to indoors. Adaptation helps this stability. The lecture states that colour constancy evolved to compensate for small variations in natural lighting.

The deck also states an important limitation: colour constancy works best under broadband illumination. It can fail when objects are illuminated by restricted wavelength light. The lecture gives a supermarket example: lighting can be used to make meat look fresher.

<a id="trichromatic-theory"></a>

## Big picture

The trichromatic theory explains colour vision at the photoreceptor level. It says that colour perception begins with three cone mechanisms. The deck links these to **S**, **M** and **L** cones, which are sensitive to short, medium and long wavelengths. The balance of activity across these cone classes determines perceived colour.

The theory is associated with Young, Helmholtz and others. It explains colour matching and the ability to perceive many colours from combinations of three primary cone responses.

<a id="opponent-process-theory"></a>

## Big picture

The opponent-process theory explains colour phenomena that trichromatic theory alone cannot explain. It accounts for why people do not see reddish-green or bluish-yellow at the same place and time, and why complementary afterimages occur.

The lecture describes opponent channels as:
- red versus green,
- blue versus yellow,
- black versus white.

Activation of one member of an opponent pair inhibits the other. The red-green and blue-yellow channels code hue, while the black-white channel relates to light intensity.

The deck also links opponent processing to neural wiring. Cone signals are recombined: L-M supports red/green coding, L+M-S supports yellow/blue coding, and L+M+S contributes to the achromatic channel. The blue-yellow pathway is illustrated with S-cone bipolar cells and bistratified retinal ganglion cells.

<a id="zone-model"></a>

## Big picture

The lecture resolves the apparent conflict between trichromatic and opponent theories through the **zone model**. Both theories are needed, but they apply at different levels.

At the receptor level, three cone classes capture wavelength information. At post-receptoral levels, horizontal and amacrine cells help organize cone signals into opponent channels. Retinal ganglion cells and thalamic neurons then process colour along opponent pairs. Visual cortex contributes to the final experience of thousands of colours.

The high-yield summary is: primate colour vision is trichromatic, but trichromatic signals are encoded in opponent fashion.

<a id="clinical-considerations"></a>

## Big picture

The deck includes only a brief clinical section. It states that blue cones are more vulnerable and that this is used in psychophysical testing and equipment manufacture, including visual field analyzers in glaucoma and **SWAP** (Short Wavelength Automated Perimetry).

The objectives name common clinical colour perception tests: Ishihara, D-15, City University, HRR and FM-100.
