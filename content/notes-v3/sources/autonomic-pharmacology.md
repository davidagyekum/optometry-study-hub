## How to study autonomic pharmacology

Autonomic pharmacology becomes difficult when it is approached as a list of
drug names. It becomes much easier when every drug is placed on a pathway.

For each drug, ask five questions:

1. **Where does it act?**
2. **Is it an agonist, antagonist, release modifier, uptake modifier, or enzyme
   modifier?**
3. **Which transmitter or receptor changes?**
4. **What organ response follows?**
5. **Which therapeutic use and adverse effect follow from that response?**

Use this chain:

```text
drug
→ molecular target
→ transmitter/receptor effect
→ organ response
→ therapeutic use
→ predictable adverse effect or contraindication
```

The labels in these notes mean:

- **Must know:** central mechanism or distinction.
- **Should know:** supporting detail needed for confident application.
- **Useful extension:** additional depth.

# 1. Adrenergic Pharmacology

## Big picture

Adrenergic pharmacology concerns drugs that alter sympathetic transmission,
especially transmission mediated by **noradrenaline (norepinephrine)** and
**adrenaline (epinephrine)**.

A drug can increase adrenergic activity by:

- stimulating an adrenoceptor directly;
- increasing transmitter release;
- blocking transmitter reuptake;
- preventing transmitter metabolism.

A drug can reduce adrenergic activity by:

- inhibiting synthesis;
- depleting vesicular storage;
- inhibiting release;
- blocking alpha or beta receptors.

The same receptor may produce different clinical effects depending on the
tissue in which it is located.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- autonomic pathway organization and major transmitter exceptions;
- the five stages of adrenergic transmission;
- catecholamine-synthesis sequence and the rate-limiting step;
- vesicular storage, calcium-dependent release, reuptake, and metabolism;
- alpha-1, alpha-2, beta-1, beta-2, and beta-3 effects;
- direct, indirect, and mixed sympathomimetics;
- ocular actions of phenylephrine, brimonidine, apraclonidine, timolol, and
  betaxolol;
- major contraindications of topical beta blockers.

### Should know

- tyramine–MAO inhibitor interaction;
- reserpine and amphetamine mechanisms;
- major alpha- and beta-blocker uses;
- intrinsic sympathomimetic activity.

### Useful extension

- historic neuron-blocking agents;
- potency orders used to distinguish alpha and beta receptor families;
- non-ocular systemic uses.

## 1.1 Autonomic organization

The peripheral motor system includes:

- the **somatic nervous system**, which innervates skeletal muscle;
- the **autonomic nervous system**, which regulates cardiac muscle, smooth
  muscle, and glands.

Most autonomic pathways use two neurons:

1. a **preganglionic neuron** from the central nervous system to an autonomic
   ganglion;
2. a **postganglionic neuron** from the ganglion to the target organ.

### Sympathetic division

- thoracolumbar origin;
- generally short preganglionic fibres;
- generally long postganglionic fibres;
- ganglia near the spinal cord;
- mobilizes the body during activity and stress.

### Parasympathetic division

- craniosacral origin;
- generally long preganglionic fibres;
- short postganglionic fibres;
- ganglia near or within target organs;
- supports maintenance, digestion, secretion, and energy conservation.

## 1.2 Transmitters in autonomic pathways

**Acetylcholine (ACh)** is released by:

- all autonomic preganglionic fibres;
- most parasympathetic postganglionic fibres;
- sympathetic postganglionic fibres to sweat glands;
- somatic motor neurons at the neuromuscular junction.

**Noradrenaline (NA)** is released by most sympathetic postganglionic fibres.

The adrenal medulla behaves like a modified sympathetic ganglion. Preganglionic
ACh stimulates chromaffin cells, which release catecholamines into the blood.

### Major sympathetic exception

Sympathetic innervation of most sweat glands is anatomically sympathetic but
chemically cholinergic: the postganglionic fibre releases ACh onto muscarinic
receptors.

## 1.3 Selected autonomic effects

| Organ | Sympathetic tendency | Parasympathetic tendency |
|---|---|---|
| Iris | radial-muscle contraction and pupil dilation | sphincter contraction and miosis |
| Ciliary muscle | limited direct role in ordinary accommodation | contraction for near focus |
| Heart | increased rate, force, and conduction | reduced rate and conduction |
| Bronchi | relaxation/dilation | constriction and secretion |
| Gastrointestinal wall | reduced tone and motility | increased tone and motility |
| Gastrointestinal sphincters | contraction | relaxation |
| Bladder detrusor | relaxation | contraction |
| Lacrimal gland | modulatory vasomotor role | dominant secretomotor drive |

> **Clinical reasoning rule:** Do not memorize “sympathetic stimulates,
> parasympathetic inhibits.” Both divisions can contract or relax tissue. The
> response depends on the receptor and organ.

## 1.4 Adrenergic neuroeffector junctions

Sympathetic postganglionic axons often release transmitter from multiple
bead-like enlargements called **varicosities**, rather than from one tightly
defined terminal end plate.

This arrangement allows transmitter to influence a broader area of smooth
muscle or glandular tissue.

Adrenergic transmission can be divided into five stages:

1. synthesis;
2. storage;
3. release;
4. receptor binding;
5. removal.

Every stage is a possible drug target.

## 1.5 Catecholamine synthesis

The pathway is:

```text
Tyrosine
  --tyrosine hydroxylase-->
DOPA
  --DOPA decarboxylase-->
Dopamine
  --dopamine beta-hydroxylase-->
Noradrenaline
  --phenylethanolamine N-methyltransferase-->
Adrenaline
```

### Step 1: tyrosine to DOPA

- enzyme: **tyrosine hydroxylase**;
- rate-limiting step;
- inhibited by **metyrosine (alpha-methyltyrosine)**.

### Step 2: DOPA to dopamine

- enzyme: **aromatic L-amino-acid/DOPA decarboxylase**;
- inhibited peripherally by **carbidopa**;
- alpha-methyldopa can enter this pathway and form a false transmitter.

### Step 3: dopamine to noradrenaline

- enzyme: **dopamine beta-hydroxylase**;
- occurs mainly within storage vesicles.

### Step 4: noradrenaline to adrenaline

- enzyme: **phenylethanolamine N-methyltransferase (PNMT)**;
- especially important in the adrenal medulla.

### Memory hook

**T-D-D-N-A**

```text
Tyrosine
DOPA
Dopamine
Noradrenaline
Adrenaline
```

For the enzymes:

**Hydroxylate – Decarboxylate – Hydroxylate – Methylate.**

## 1.6 Storage

Newly synthesized catecholamine is transported into synaptic vesicles by the
**vesicular monoamine transporter (VMAT)**.

Vesicular storage:

- protects catecholamine from cytoplasmic metabolism;
- concentrates transmitter for release;
- allows regulated exocytosis.

### Reserpine

**Reserpine** inhibits VMAT. Catecholamines cannot be stored effectively, are
metabolized in the cytoplasm, and the nerve terminal becomes depleted.

Consequences include reduced sympathetic tone and lowered blood pressure.
Central monoamine depletion accounts for serious historical adverse effects,
including severe depression.

## 1.7 Release

An action potential opens voltage-gated calcium channels. Calcium entry causes
vesicles to fuse with the membrane and release noradrenaline.

Release can be modified by:

- drugs that promote displacement and reverse transport, such as amphetamine;
- neuron-blocking agents such as guanethidine;
- presynaptic receptors, especially alpha-2 autoreceptors.

## 1.8 Presynaptic feedback

Released noradrenaline can activate presynaptic **alpha-2 adrenoceptors**.
This inhibits further transmitter release, creating negative feedback.

The reasoning chain is:

```text
more noradrenaline in junction
→ alpha-2 autoreceptor activation
→ reduced additional release
→ limitation of sympathetic signal
```

Alpha-2 agonists can therefore reduce sympathetic transmission even though they
are receptor agonists.

## 1.9 Removal of noradrenaline

The major terminating process is neuronal reuptake through the
**norepinephrine transporter (NET)**, historically called **uptake 1**.

After reuptake, noradrenaline may:

- be returned to vesicles through VMAT;
- be metabolized by monoamine oxidase;
- diffuse or undergo extraneuronal uptake and metabolism.

**Catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT)** and **monoamine oxidase (MAO)**
contribute to catecholamine metabolism.

### Cocaine

Cocaine blocks catecholamine reuptake, increasing transmitter concentration at
adrenergic synapses. This is an indirect enhancement of transmission rather
than direct adrenoceptor stimulation.

## 1.10 Tyramine and the “cheese reaction”

Tyramine is present in some aged or fermented foods. Normally, monoamine oxidase
in the gut and liver limits systemic tyramine exposure.

With a non-selective MAO inhibitor:

1. dietary tyramine escapes normal breakdown;
2. tyramine enters adrenergic nerve terminals through NET;
3. it enters vesicles and displaces stored noradrenaline;
4. noradrenaline is released into the junction;
5. marked vasoconstriction and cardiac stimulation may occur.

Possible consequences include:

- throbbing headache;
- severe hypertension;
- tachycardia or arrhythmia;
- nausea;
- stroke.

### Key distinction

Tyramine does not need to behave mainly as a direct receptor agonist. It is an
**indirect sympathomimetic** that releases endogenous catecholamine.

## 1.11 Amphetamine

Amphetamine is also an indirect sympathomimetic.

It:

- enters the nerve terminal through catecholamine transporters;
- displaces noradrenaline from vesicles;
- increases cytoplasmic catecholamine;
- promotes reverse transport into the junction;
- may additionally reduce metabolism at higher exposures.

Its central effects include stimulation, wakefulness, euphoria, reduced
appetite, tolerance, dependence, paranoia, hallucinations, and psychosis.

### Compare tyramine and amphetamine

| Feature | Tyramine | Amphetamine |
|---|---|---|
| Main mechanism | displacement and release of stored catecholamine | displacement, reverse transport, central stimulation |
| Clinical context emphasized | food interaction with MAO inhibitors | stimulant drug and drug of abuse |
| Dependence on stores | high | high |
| Direct receptor action | not the main explanation | not the main explanation |

## 1.12 Adrenoceptor families

The lecture distinguishes alpha and beta families partly by catecholamine
potency.

- Alpha tendency: **NA > adrenaline >> isoprenaline**.
- Beta tendency: **isoprenaline > adrenaline > NA**.

Modern application depends more on receptor subtype and tissue distribution than
on memorizing the potency sequence alone.

## 1.13 Alpha-1 receptors

Alpha-1 receptors are mainly postsynaptic on effector cells.

Important effects include:

- contraction of iris radial muscle → mydriasis;
- arteriolar and venous constriction;
- contraction of some genitourinary and gastrointestinal sphincters;
- contraction of pilomotor muscle;
- hepatic glycogenolysis.

### Ocular reasoning

```text
alpha-1 activation
→ iris radial muscle contracts
→ pupil enlarges
→ mydriasis
```

The ciliary muscle is not paralyzed by this mechanism. Therefore,
alpha-1 mydriasis is not the same as antimuscarinic cycloplegia.

## 1.14 Alpha-2 receptors

Alpha-2 receptors occur presynaptically and at other central and peripheral
sites.

Important effects include:

- reduced noradrenaline release;
- reduced sympathetic outflow in some central pathways;
- reduced aqueous humour production;
- possible increase in uveoscleral outflow for some ocular drugs.

### Ocular drugs

- **Brimonidine:** relatively selective alpha-2 agonist; used in open-angle
  glaucoma and ocular hypertension.
- **Apraclonidine:** predominantly alpha-2 agonist with some alpha-1 activity;
  useful for short-term IOP control and prevention of pressure spikes.

> **Course correction:** Apraclonidine should not be remembered as a broad
> “non-selective alpha drug” without recognizing its predominant alpha-2 action.

## 1.15 Beta-1 receptors

Beta-1 receptors are especially important in the heart and juxtaglomerular
apparatus.

Effects include:

- increased heart rate (**positive chronotropy**);
- increased contractile force (**positive inotropy**);
- increased conduction (**positive dromotropy**);
- increased renin release.

### Drug example

**Dobutamine** is used when increased cardiac output is required.

## 1.16 Beta-2 receptors

Beta-2 receptors are prominent in:

- bronchial smooth muscle;
- vascular smooth muscle in selected beds;
- uterine smooth muscle;
- ciliary epithelium;
- some metabolic tissues.

Effects include:

- bronchodilation;
- vasodilation;
- uterine relaxation;
- skeletal-muscle tremor;
- glycogenolysis;
- stimulation of aqueous secretion by the ciliary epithelium.

### Clinical examples

- salbutamol/albuterol and terbutaline for bronchospasm;
- beta blockade to reduce aqueous production in glaucoma.

## 1.17 Beta-3 receptors

Beta-3 activation is associated mainly with:

- lipolysis in adipose tissue;
- relaxation of bladder detrusor in modern therapeutic application.

The lecture emphasizes lipolysis.

## Receptor comparison table

| Receptor | High-yield location | High-yield effect | Ocular relevance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alpha-1 | vascular smooth muscle, iris radial muscle | contraction | mydriasis, vasoconstriction |
| Alpha-2 | presynaptic terminals, CNS, ciliary processes | reduced transmitter release; reduced aqueous formation | brimonidine, apraclonidine |
| Beta-1 | heart, juxtaglomerular cells | cardiac stimulation, renin release | systemic adverse-effect reasoning |
| Beta-2 | bronchi, selected vessels, ciliary epithelium | smooth-muscle relaxation; aqueous secretion | beta blockade lowers aqueous production |
| Beta-3 | adipose tissue, bladder | lipolysis, detrusor relaxation | little direct routine ocular role |

## 1.18 Sympathomimetic classification

### Direct acting

Direct agonists bind the receptor.

Examples:

- adrenaline;
- noradrenaline;
- phenylephrine;
- clonidine;
- brimonidine;
- apraclonidine;
- isoprenaline;
- dobutamine;
- salbutamol;
- terbutaline.

### Indirect acting

Indirect drugs increase endogenous catecholamine without primarily activating
the receptor themselves.

Examples:

- amphetamine;
- tyramine;
- cocaine as an uptake inhibitor.

### Mixed acting

A mixed drug both activates receptors and promotes transmitter release.

Example:

- ephedrine.

## 1.19 Phenylephrine

**Phenylephrine** is mainly an alpha-1 agonist.

Ocular effects:

- contracts iris radial muscle;
- causes mydriasis;
- constricts conjunctival arterioles.

Uses in the lecture include:

- diagnostic dilation;
- selected ocular procedures;
- helping prevent or break posterior synechiae in inflammatory care;
- reducing conjunctival redness in some preparations.

### Important distinction

Phenylephrine dilates the pupil but does not directly paralyze the ciliary
muscle. Accommodation is therefore relatively preserved compared with
antimuscarinic cycloplegics.

### Predictable adverse effects

- hypertension;
- reflex bradycardia;
- palpitations;
- local irritation;
- risk from excessive concentration or systemic absorption.

## 1.20 Hydroxyamphetamine

The lecture labels hydroxyamphetamine as an alpha-1 agonist. The more useful
pharmacologic model is that it is **predominantly an indirect sympathomimetic**
that releases stored noradrenaline from intact postganglionic sympathetic nerve
terminals.

This distinction explains its historical diagnostic use in localizing
sympathetic denervation.

> **Course correction:** Learn hydroxyamphetamine primarily as a
> noradrenaline-releasing drug, not simply as direct alpha-1 stimulation.

## 1.21 Brimonidine and apraclonidine

### Brimonidine

Mechanism:

- alpha-2 receptor activation;
- reduced aqueous production;
- increased uveoscleral outflow may contribute.

Uses:

- open-angle glaucoma;
- ocular hypertension.

Adverse effects and cautions can include:

- allergy;
- dry mouth;
- fatigue or drowsiness;
- systemic hypotension;
- special caution in young children and with interacting drugs.

### Apraclonidine

Used mainly for:

- short-term pressure reduction;
- prevention of perioperative IOP spikes;
- selected diagnostic purposes.

Long-term use is limited by allergy and tachyphylaxis.

## 1.22 Ephedrine

Ephedrine has mixed action:

- direct alpha and beta stimulation;
- indirect release of catecholamine.

Possible effects include:

- vasoconstriction;
- cardiac stimulation;
- bronchodilation;
- mydriasis;
- central stimulation.

Repeated use may show tachyphylaxis as transmitter stores become depleted.

## 1.23 Adrenergic antagonists

Adrenoceptor antagonists block sympathetic receptor effects.

### Alpha blockers

#### Non-selective

- phenoxybenzamine;
- phentolamine.

#### Alpha-1 selective

- prazosin;
- doxazosin;
- terazosin;
- tamsulosin and related alpha-1A-selective agents.

Effects:

- reduced arteriolar and venous tone;
- lower peripheral resistance;
- relaxation of bladder neck and prostate smooth muscle.

Uses:

- hypertension;
- phaeochromocytoma preparation or control;
- benign prostatic enlargement.

Common adverse effect:

- postural hypotension, especially after initial dosing.

### Beta blockers

Examples in the lecture include:

- propranolol;
- metoprolol;
- atenolol;
- nadolol;
- pindolol;
- bisoprolol;
- esmolol;
- timolol;
- betaxolol;
- levobunolol.

Beta blockade can affect:

- heart;
- bronchi;
- vasculature;
- liver and glucose response;
- pancreas;
- ciliary epithelium.

## 1.24 Intrinsic sympathomimetic activity

Some beta blockers have partial agonist activity called **intrinsic
sympathomimetic activity (ISA)**.

Examples listed:

- pindolol;
- acebutolol;
- oxprenolol;
- celiprolol.

These drugs may produce less resting bradycardia than pure antagonists, but the
clinical suitability depends on the condition being treated.

## 1.25 Ocular beta blockers

Topical beta blockers reduce IOP mainly by reducing aqueous humour production.

### Timolol

- non-selective beta blocker;
- effective ocular hypotensive;
- may produce systemic beta blockade after topical administration.

### Betaxolol

- relatively beta-1 selective;
- may have less bronchial effect than a non-selective drug;
- selectivity is incomplete and dose-dependent.

### Contraindications and cautions

Especially important:

- asthma;
- chronic obstructive pulmonary disease;
- marked bradycardia;
- second- or third-degree atrioventricular block;
- selected heart-failure states.

### Why topical does not mean local only

Drug can drain through the nasolacrimal system and be absorbed from nasal
mucosa. Punctal occlusion and eyelid closure can reduce systemic absorption.

## 1.26 Systemic beta-blocker actions

### Heart

- reduced rate;
- reduced contractility;
- reduced conduction;
- reduced automaticity;
- reduced myocardial oxygen demand.

### Blood pressure

Chronic lowering may reflect:

- reduced cardiac output;
- reduced renin;
- central effects;
- reduced sympathetic facilitation.

### Bronchi

Non-selective beta blockade can increase airway resistance by blocking beta-2
bronchodilation.

### Glucose response

Beta blockade can impair adrenergic responses to hypoglycaemia and may mask some
warning symptoms.

## Common traps

### “Agonist means stimulation of the whole sympathetic system.”

A selective agonist produces the effects of the receptor it activates, not
every sympathetic effect.

### “Alpha-2 agonists increase noradrenaline because they are agonists.”

Presynaptic alpha-2 activation inhibits further release.

### “Mydriasis always means cycloplegia.”

Alpha-1 mydriasis contracts the radial muscle. Cycloplegia requires ciliary-
muscle paralysis, typically from muscarinic blockade.

### “Topical timolol cannot affect the lungs or heart.”

Systemic absorption can produce clinically important beta blockade.

### “Beta-1 selective means beta-1 only.”

Selectivity is relative and can diminish with dose.

## Key terms

- **Adrenergic:** involving adrenaline/noradrenaline transmission.
- **Sympathomimetic:** drug that mimics or increases sympathetic effects.
- **Varicosity:** bead-like transmitter-release enlargement along an autonomic
  axon.
- **VMAT:** vesicular transporter that stores monoamines.
- **NET:** neuronal noradrenaline transporter.
- **Autoreceptor:** receptor on the releasing neuron that regulates further
  transmitter release.
- **Chronotropy:** effect on heart rate.
- **Inotropy:** effect on contractile force.
- **Dromotropy:** effect on conduction.
- **Mydriasis:** pupil dilation.
- **Cycloplegia:** paralysis of accommodation.
- **Tachyphylaxis:** rapid reduction in response with repeated exposure.
- **ISA:** partial agonist action of some beta blockers.

## Memory aids

### Receptors

- **Alpha-1 = constrict one tube:** vessels and iris radial muscle.
- **Alpha-2 = reduce release:** presynaptic brake.
- **Beta-1 = one heart:** cardiac stimulation and renin.
- **Beta-2 = two lungs:** bronchodilation and smooth-muscle relaxation.
- **Beta-3 = fat/free bladder:** lipolysis and detrusor relaxation.

### Ocular drugs

```text
Phenylephrine → pupil
Brimonidine/apraclonidine → reduce aqueous
Timolol/betaxolol → block aqueous stimulation
```

## Worked case

A patient with open-angle glaucoma and severe asthma needs another pressure-
lowering medicine.

A non-selective topical beta blocker could reduce aqueous formation, but beta-2
blockade may increase airway resistance. The mechanism that makes the drug
effective in the eye also predicts the respiratory risk. An alternative class
or carefully selected agent should be considered within the full clinical
context.

## One-minute summary

Adrenergic transmission follows synthesis, storage, release, receptor action,
and removal. Tyrosine hydroxylase is rate-limiting; VMAT stores catecholamine;
calcium triggers release; NET performs major reuptake. Alpha-1 contracts vessels
and iris radial muscle, alpha-2 inhibits release and lowers aqueous formation,
beta-1 stimulates the heart, and beta-2 relaxes bronchi and supports aqueous
secretion. Direct agonists bind receptors, indirect drugs increase endogenous
transmitter, and antagonists block receptor responses.

# 2. Cholinergic Pharmacology

## Big picture

Cholinergic pharmacology concerns drugs that alter the synthesis, storage,
release, receptor action, or breakdown of **acetylcholine (ACh)**.

ACh acts at:

- **muscarinic receptors**, which are G-protein-coupled and generally slower;
- **nicotinic receptors**, which are ligand-gated ion channels and generally
  faster.

A drug may act directly at the receptor or indirectly by inhibiting
acetylcholinesterase.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- where ACh is released in autonomic and somatic pathways;
- ACh synthesis, vesicular storage, calcium-dependent release, and breakdown;
- muscarinic versus nicotinic receptors;
- M1/M3/M5 versus M2/M4 signalling pattern;
- Nm versus Nn location;
- direct versus indirect cholinomimetics;
- organ effects of muscarinic activation;
- atropine ocular effects;
- organophosphate toxicity and antidotal logic;
- non-depolarizing versus depolarizing neuromuscular block.

### Should know

- choline-ester differences;
- edrophonium, neostigmine, pyridostigmine, and physostigmine;
- ganglion blockers and predominant autonomic tone;
- antimuscarinic distribution differences between tertiary and quaternary
  compounds.

### Useful extension

- structure–activity relationships of choline esters;
- less commonly used ganglion stimulants and blockers.

## 2.1 Where ACh is released

ACh is released by:

- all autonomic preganglionic fibres;
- all parasympathetic postganglionic fibres;
- sympathetic postganglionic fibres to sweat glands;
- somatic motor neurons at skeletal neuromuscular junctions;
- many central nervous-system neurons.

The effect depends on receptor type. ACh can slow the heart through muscarinic
receptors and excite skeletal muscle through nicotinic receptors.

## 2.2 Cholinergic transmission sequence

```text
Choline uptake
→ ACh synthesis
→ vesicular storage
→ calcium-triggered release
→ muscarinic or nicotinic receptor activation
→ hydrolysis by cholinesterase
```

## 2.3 ACh synthesis

ACh is synthesized in the nerve terminal from:

- **choline**;
- **acetyl coenzyme A**.

The enzyme is **choline acetyltransferase (ChAT)**.

High-affinity choline uptake is important because it supplies substrate for
continued transmitter synthesis.

### Hemicholinium

Hemicholinium blocks choline uptake and therefore reduces ACh synthesis. It is
a pharmacologic tool rather than a routine therapeutic drug.

## 2.4 Vesicular storage

The vesicular ACh transporter loads ACh into synaptic vesicles.

### Vesamicol

Vesamicol blocks vesicular ACh transport, reducing the amount available for
quantal release. It is not a routine therapeutic agent.

## 2.5 Release

An action potential opens calcium channels. Calcium promotes vesicle docking,
fusion, and ACh release.

### Botulinum toxin

Botulinum toxin disrupts proteins required for vesicle fusion. ACh release is
reduced, producing local chemical denervation and weakness.

The mechanism explains both therapeutic uses and botulism paralysis.

### Latrotoxin

Black-widow-spider latrotoxin can trigger massive transmitter release,
including ACh release, causing painful muscle spasm and autonomic effects.

## 2.6 Cholinesterases

### Acetylcholinesterase

**Acetylcholinesterase (AChE)** is concentrated at cholinergic synapses and
rapidly terminates ACh action.

### Butyrylcholinesterase

**Butyrylcholinesterase**, also called plasma or pseudocholinesterase, is found
in plasma and tissues such as liver and intestine. It metabolizes several
choline esters and is particularly important in succinylcholine metabolism.

> **Course correction:** The slide states that ACh is not hydrolyzed by
> pseudocholinesterase. The clinically useful model is that butyrylcholinesterase
> has broader, lower-specificity activity and can hydrolyze ACh and several
> ester drugs; AChE is the principal synaptic enzyme.

## 2.7 Muscarinic receptors

Muscarinic receptors are G-protein-coupled receptors.

### M1, M3, and M5

The lecture groups these with the inositol-phosphate pathway. Their activation
commonly promotes glandular secretion or smooth-muscle contraction, depending
on tissue.

### M2 and M4

These inhibit adenylate cyclase and reduce cyclic AMP. M2 is especially
important in the heart.

### Locations emphasized

| Receptor | Major lecture location |
|---|---|
| M1 | nerves |
| M2 | heart, nerves, some smooth muscle |
| M3 | exocrine glands, smooth muscle, endothelium |
| M4 | CNS |
| M5 | CNS |

### Muscarinic response character

Compared with nicotinic responses, muscarinic responses are generally slower
and longer lasting. They may excite or inhibit depending on the signalling
pathway and tissue.

## 2.8 Nicotinic receptors

Nicotinic receptors are ligand-gated cation channels. Activation rapidly
increases sodium and calcium permeability and produces a short-lived excitatory
response.

### Subtypes

- **Nm:** skeletal neuromuscular junction.
- **Nn:** autonomic ganglia and adrenal medulla.

Excessive or prolonged agonist exposure can desensitize the receptor, so an
agonist can eventually produce functional blockade.

## Muscarinic versus nicotinic

| Feature | Muscarinic | Nicotinic |
|---|---|---|
| Receptor class | G-protein-coupled | ligand-gated ion channel |
| Speed | slower | rapid |
| Main locations | organs, glands, heart, CNS | ganglia, adrenal medulla, NMJ, CNS |
| Response | excitatory or inhibitory | initially excitatory |
| Main blockers | atropine-like drugs | ganglion or neuromuscular blockers |

## 2.9 Direct and indirect agonists

### Direct acting

Direct cholinomimetics bind and activate cholinoceptors.

Groups:

- choline esters;
- muscarinic alkaloids;
- nicotinic agonists.

### Indirect acting

Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors increase endogenous ACh at:

- autonomic ganglia;
- parasympathetic neuroeffector junctions;
- neuromuscular junctions;
- selected central synapses, depending on drug penetration.

## 2.10 Choline-esters structure and action

The lecture emphasizes three structural regions:

1. positively charged quaternary nitrogen;
2. ester or carbamate group;
3. connecting alkyl chain.

The quaternary nitrogen interacts with an anionic receptor region. The ester
group interacts with the esteratic region. Correct spacing allows effective
binding.

Replacing the acetate ester with a carbamate reduces susceptibility to
cholinesterase and prolongs action.

## 2.11 Major choline esters

| Drug | Cholinesterase susceptibility | Muscarinic activity | Nicotinic activity | High-yield use/model |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Acetylcholine | very high | strong | strong | very brief, nonselective |
| Methacholine | lower | strong | limited | bronchial challenge |
| Carbachol | negligible/low | moderate | strong | ocular miosis and selected uses |
| Bethanechol | negligible/low | moderate | minimal | bladder and GI smooth muscle |

### Reasoning

- Resistance to cholinesterase prolongs action.
- Beta-methyl substitution tends to increase muscarinic selectivity.
- A permanently charged quaternary structure limits central penetration.

## 2.12 Muscarine

Muscarine is a mushroom alkaloid that stimulates muscarinic receptors.
It can produce marked parasympathetic effects involving smooth muscle, heart,
and glands.

## 2.13 Pilocarpine

Pilocarpine is a naturally occurring muscarinic agonist.

Effects include:

- contraction of iris sphincter → miosis;
- contraction of ciliary muscle;
- increased glandular secretion;
- facilitation of aqueous outflow through trabecular mechanisms.

Uses include glaucoma and stimulation of salivary secretion in selected
conditions.

### Ocular caution

Strong miosis and ciliary contraction may cause brow ache, accommodative spasm,
reduced vision in dim light, and retinal risk in susceptible individuals.

## 2.14 Muscarinic effects

The lecture uses **SLUDGE**:

```text
Salivation
Lacrimation
Urination
Defaecation
Gastric distress
Emesis
```

Other high-yield effects include:

- miosis;
- accommodation for near;
- bronchoconstriction and secretion;
- bradycardia;
- increased GI motility;
- bladder detrusor contraction;
- sweating through sympathetic cholinergic fibres.

### Endothelial vasodilation

Muscarinic activation of intact vascular endothelium can release
endothelium-derived relaxing factor/nitric oxide, causing vasodilation.

## 2.15 Organ effects table

| Organ | Muscarinic activation |
|---|---|
| Iris sphincter | contraction → miosis |
| Ciliary muscle | contraction → near focus and increased trabecular outflow |
| Heart SA node | reduced rate |
| AV node | reduced conduction |
| Bronchi | contraction and increased secretion |
| GI wall | increased tone and motility |
| GI sphincters | relaxation |
| Bladder detrusor | contraction |
| Glands | increased salivary, lacrimal, sweat, and respiratory secretion |

## 2.16 Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors

AChE inhibitors are classified by duration and bonding to the enzyme.

### Simple alcohols

Example: **edrophonium**.

- short acting;
- reversible binding;
- historically used in diagnosis of myasthenia gravis.

> **Course qualification:** Edrophonium is historically important, but modern
> diagnosis commonly relies on antibody testing, electrodiagnostic testing, and
> clinical assessment rather than routine Tensilon testing.

### Carbamates

Examples:

- neostigmine;
- pyridostigmine;
- physostigmine.

They carbamylate AChE and have intermediate duration.

### Organophosphates

Examples include insecticides and older ocular agents.

They phosphorylate AChE. With time, the bond undergoes **ageing**, becoming
increasingly resistant to reactivation.

## 2.17 Comparing common inhibitors

| Drug | Central penetration | High-yield use |
|---|---|---|
| Edrophonium | limited | historic diagnostic testing |
| Neostigmine | poor | myasthenia, ileus, reversal of non-depolarizing block |
| Pyridostigmine | poor | longer-term myasthenia treatment |
| Physostigmine | good | severe central antimuscarinic toxicity |
| Ecothiophate | limited | historic/rare glaucoma use |

## 2.18 Organophosphate poisoning

Organophosphates cause excessive ACh at muscarinic, nicotinic, and central
receptors.

### Muscarinic manifestations

- salivation and lacrimation;
- bronchoconstriction;
- bronchial secretion;
- bradycardia;
- miosis and blurred vision;
- sweating;
- diarrhoea;
- urination;
- hypotension.

### Nicotinic manifestations

- fasciculation;
- weakness;
- paralysis;
- autonomic-ganglion effects, which may include tachycardia or hypertension.

### Central manifestations

- agitation;
- confusion;
- tremor;
- ataxia;
- seizures;
- respiratory depression;
- coma.

### Treatment logic

1. remove the person from exposure and protect rescuers;
2. decontaminate;
3. support airway, breathing, and circulation;
4. give **atropine** to block dangerous muscarinic effects;
5. give **pralidoxime** early when appropriate to reactivate AChE before ageing;
6. control seizures and complications.

### Why both atropine and pralidoxime?

- Atropine blocks muscarinic receptors but does not directly restore AChE.
- Pralidoxime can restore phosphorylated AChE before ageing and is especially
  relevant to nicotinic weakness.

## 2.19 Antimuscarinic drugs

Antimuscarinic drugs competitively block muscarinic receptors.

### Tertiary amines

Examples:

- atropine;
- scopolamine;
- tropicamide;
- cyclopentolate;
- benztropine;
- dicyclomine.

They are lipid soluble and may enter the CNS.

### Quaternary compounds

Examples:

- ipratropium;
- propantheline.

They cross lipid membranes and the blood–brain barrier less readily.

## 2.20 Atropine

Atropine is a non-selective competitive antimuscarinic tertiary amine.

### Eye

- blocks iris sphincter → unopposed radial tone → mydriasis;
- blocks ciliary muscle → cycloplegia;
- reduces lacrimal secretion → dryness.

### Heart

- blocks vagal slowing;
- increases heart rate;
- improves AV nodal conduction;
- has limited direct effect on ventricular contractility.

### Respiratory tract

- reduces secretion;
- produces some bronchodilation.

### Gastrointestinal and urinary systems

- dry mouth;
- reduced motility;
- delayed gastric emptying;
- constipation;
- bladder relaxation and urinary retention.

### Sweat glands

Muscarinic blockade reduces sweating and may impair heat loss.

### CNS

Low doses may cause mild central effects; toxic doses can cause agitation,
hallucination, and delirium.

## 2.21 Scopolamine and selected antimuscarinics

- **Scopolamine:** prominent central effect; motion-sickness prevention.
- **Tropicamide:** short-acting diagnostic mydriatic.
- **Cyclopentolate:** cycloplegic refraction, especially when stronger
  cycloplegia than tropicamide is needed.
- **Ipratropium:** inhaled quaternary antimuscarinic for airway disease.
- **Benztropine:** selected Parkinsonian symptoms.

### Eye-examination distinction

Phenylephrine produces mydriasis without cycloplegia. Tropicamide or
cyclopentolate produces muscarinic blockade and therefore cycloplegia to a
degree determined by the drug and patient.

## 2.22 Antimuscarinic cautions

Important cautions include:

- anatomically narrow or occludable anterior chamber angles;
- urinary retention or prostatic obstruction;
- tachyarrhythmia;
- severe constipation or reduced gut motility;
- heat exposure where sweating is important;
- central vulnerability in older patients.

> **Course qualification:** The risk is not identical in every patient labelled
> “glaucoma.” The major acute-angle concern relates to susceptible anterior-
> chamber anatomy and drug-induced dilation.

## 2.23 Nicotine

Nicotine is a nicotinic agonist acting at:

- autonomic ganglia;
- adrenal medulla;
- neuromuscular junction at high exposure;
- sensory endings;
- CNS.

Because ganglia of both autonomic divisions are stimulated, the net organ effect
depends on predominant autonomic tone.

At high doses, persistent stimulation causes receptor desensitization and
blockade, which can lead to respiratory paralysis.

## 2.24 Ganglion blockers

Ganglion blockers inhibit Nn receptors in both sympathetic and parasympathetic
ganglia.

They are rarely used clinically because blockade is broad and adverse effects
are extensive.

### Predominant-tone principle

The net effect depends on which autonomic division normally dominates the
organ.

| Organ | Predominant resting tone | Expected ganglion-block effect |
|---|---|---|
| Arterioles | sympathetic | vasodilation and hypotension |
| Heart | parasympathetic/vagal | tachycardia is often expected |
| GI tract | parasympathetic | reduced motility and constipation |
| Bladder | parasympathetic | urinary retention |
| Glands | parasympathetic/cholinergic | dry mouth and reduced secretion |
| Eye | mixed | partial mydriasis and impaired accommodation |

> **Course clarification:** One slide describes bradycardia as the usual cardiac
> effect. Mechanistically, removal of resting vagal tone often produces
> tachycardia. The exact response depends on baseline autonomic tone.

Examples include:

- hexamethonium;
- mecamylamine;
- trimethaphan;
- pentolinium;
- pempidine.

## 2.25 Neuromuscular blockers

Neuromuscular blockers act at Nm receptors and are used with anaesthesia. They
cause skeletal-muscle paralysis but do not provide unconsciousness or analgesia.

Two major classes:

1. non-depolarizing;
2. depolarizing.

## 2.26 Non-depolarizing blockers

Examples:

- tubocurarine;
- atracurium;
- mivacurium;
- pancuronium;
- vecuronium;
- rocuronium.

They competitively prevent ACh from activating Nm receptors. The end plate does
not depolarize effectively.

### Typical progression of weakness

Small rapidly moving muscles may be affected before the diaphragm. Recovery
often occurs in reverse order.

### Reversal

Increasing ACh with an AChE inhibitor can overcome competitive block.
An antimuscarinic is usually paired to reduce unwanted muscarinic effects.

**Sugammadex** directly binds selected steroidal blockers, especially rocuronium
and vecuronium.

> **Course correction:** Sugammadex is not a universal reversal agent for every
> non-depolarizing blocker.

## 2.27 Succinylcholine

Succinylcholine resembles two ACh molecules joined together and is a
depolarizing blocker.

### Phase I block

1. Nm receptor activation;
2. persistent end-plate depolarization;
3. brief fasciculation;
4. inability to generate repeated action potentials;
5. flaccid paralysis.

AChE inhibition does not reliably reverse Phase I and may intensify or prolong
it.

### Phase II block

With prolonged exposure, the membrane repolarizes but becomes desensitized and
transmission remains blocked.

### Metabolism

Succinylcholine is metabolized by plasma butyrylcholinesterase. Reduced enzyme
activity or atypical enzyme can prolong apnoea.

## Non-depolarizing versus depolarizing

| Feature | Non-depolarizing | Succinylcholine |
|---|---|---|
| Receptor action | competitive antagonist | agonist producing persistent depolarization |
| Fasciculation | usually absent | common at onset |
| AChE inhibitor | may reverse | not routine for Phase I |
| Example | rocuronium | succinylcholine |
| Special reversal | sugammadex for selected agents | none equivalent |
| Metabolism | drug dependent | plasma butyrylcholinesterase |

## 2.28 Ocular cholinergic integration

### Parasympathetic pathway to the eye

ACh released from postganglionic parasympathetic fibres acts mainly at M3-like
muscarinic receptors on:

- iris sphincter;
- ciliary muscle.

Effects:

```text
iris sphincter contraction → miosis
ciliary muscle contraction → accommodation for near
ciliary muscle contraction → increased trabecular outflow
```

### Antimuscarinic pathway

```text
muscarinic blockade
→ sphincter relaxation/unopposed radial tone
→ mydriasis

muscarinic blockade
→ ciliary muscle relaxation
→ cycloplegia
```

### Distinguish three common ocular drug patterns

| Drug | Receptor action | Pupil | Accommodation | Main teaching use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Phenylephrine | alpha-1 agonist | dilates | largely preserved | mydriasis without direct cycloplegia |
| Tropicamide | muscarinic antagonist | dilates | reduced temporarily | routine diagnostic dilation |
| Pilocarpine | muscarinic agonist | constricts | stimulates near focus | miosis and increased trabecular outflow |

## Common traps

### “All cholinergic drugs are parasympathetic drugs.”

ACh also acts at autonomic ganglia, the neuromuscular junction, sweat glands,
and CNS.

### “AChE inhibitors are selective muscarinic agonists.”

They raise endogenous ACh at both muscarinic and nicotinic synapses.

### “Atropine treats all organophosphate effects.”

Atropine blocks muscarinic effects but does not restore AChE or directly reverse
nicotinic paralysis.

### “An agonist always stimulates indefinitely.”

Persistent nicotinic agonism can cause depolarizing or desensitizing block.

### “Every antimuscarinic has the same CNS effect.”

Tertiary compounds enter the CNS more readily than quaternary compounds.

## Key terms

- **Cholinergic:** involving acetylcholine transmission.
- **Cholinomimetic:** drug that activates cholinergic function directly or
  indirectly.
- **ChAT:** choline acetyltransferase.
- **AChE:** acetylcholinesterase.
- **Butyrylcholinesterase:** plasma/tissue esterase important in metabolism of
  succinylcholine and other esters.
- **Muscarinic receptor:** G-protein-coupled cholinoceptor.
- **Nicotinic receptor:** ligand-gated cation channel.
- **Miosis:** pupil constriction.
- **Cycloplegia:** paralysis of accommodation.
- **Ageing:** strengthening of the organophosphate–AChE bond over time.
- **Depolarizing block:** paralysis caused by persistent receptor activation and
  membrane depolarization/desensitization.
- **Non-depolarizing block:** competitive Nm receptor antagonism.

## Memory aids

### Muscarinic groups

```text
M1, M3, M5 → activate IP pathway
M2, M4 → reduce cAMP
```

Odd numbers generally activate the IP pathway; even numbers inhibit adenylate
cyclase.

### Muscarinic excess

**SLUDGE** plus **miosis and bronchial secretions**.

### Organophosphate management

**Decontaminate – Airway – Atropine – Oxime – Seizure control.**

### Neuromuscular block

```text
Non-depolarizing = no activation
Succinylcholine = too much activation, then no useful response
```

## Worked case

A farm worker arrives with miosis, sweating, salivation, wheeze, copious
secretions, fasciculations, and confusion after pesticide exposure.

The findings cross three receptor domains:

- muscarinic: secretions, bronchoconstriction, miosis;
- nicotinic: fasciculation and weakness;
- CNS: confusion.

Atropine is needed to block dangerous muscarinic effects. Early pralidoxime may
reactivate AChE before ageing and help address neuromuscular toxicity. Airway,
oxygenation, decontamination, and seizure management are not optional extras;
they are part of the mechanism-based treatment.

## One-minute summary

ACh is synthesized from choline and acetyl-CoA by ChAT, stored in vesicles,
released by calcium-dependent exocytosis, and broken down mainly by AChE.
Muscarinic receptors are slower G-protein receptors; nicotinic receptors are
fast ion channels. Direct agonists stimulate receptors, while AChE inhibitors
increase endogenous ACh everywhere it is released. Atropine blocks muscarinic
effects; organophosphates inhibit AChE; non-depolarizing blockers competitively
block Nm; succinylcholine produces depolarizing block.

## Final must-know list

- trace transmitter synthesis, storage, release, receptor action, and removal;
- learn receptor effects by organ, not by vague “stimulation” language;
- alpha-1 dilates the pupil without direct cycloplegia;
- alpha-2 reduces transmitter release and aqueous production;
- beta blockade lowers aqueous production but may affect lungs and heart;
- muscarinic activation causes miosis and accommodation;
- antimuscarinic drugs cause mydriasis and cycloplegia;
- AChE inhibitors raise ACh at muscarinic and nicotinic sites;
- organophosphate poisoning requires supportive care, atropine, and early oxime
  logic;
- distinguish competitive non-depolarizing block from succinylcholine
  depolarizing block.
