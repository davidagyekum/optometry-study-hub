## How to use these notes

This module provides the tissue-level language needed for later ocular anatomy.
The aim is not to memorise isolated definitions. It is to recognise a structure,
connect that structure to function, and predict what happens when it is damaged.

Read each section in three stages:

1. **Build the model.** Follow the connected explanation.
2. **Learn the distinctions.** Use the comparison tables and key terms.
3. **Retrieve it.** Close the notes and answer the active-recall questions aloud.

The focus labels mean:

- **Must know:** central knowledge required for the module.
- **Should know:** supporting detail needed for confident application.
- **Useful extension:** helpful depth, but less central to the present module.

> cartilage, bone, blood and muscle. The current Tissue Foundations module and
> curated assessment cover nervous tissue, connective tissue proper and
> epithelium. Those later specialised tissues are therefore not expanded into
> full sections here.

# 1. Nervous Tissue

## Big picture

The nervous system is a communication and control network. It receives
information, processes it and produces a response. Nervous tissue performs this
work through two broad cell classes:

- **neurons**, which are specialised to receive and transmit signals;
- **neuroglia**, which maintain the environment in which neurons can function.

A useful way to organise the topic is:

```text
information arrives
→ dendrites and soma receive/integrate it
→ the axon conducts an impulse
→ an axon terminal releases a signal
→ a synapse transfers information to the next cell
```

The structure of every neuron and glial cell makes sense when related to its
place in this sequence.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- structural and functional divisions of the nervous system;
- soma, dendrites, axon, axon hillock and axon terminal;
- nucleus versus ganglion and tract versus nerve;
- the components and directionality of a chemical synapse;
- multipolar, bipolar and pseudounipolar neurons;
- sensory, motor and interneurons;
- the six neuroglial cell types and their locations;
- oligodendrocyte versus Schwann-cell myelin;
- gray matter versus white matter;
- endoneurium, perineurium and epineurium;
- the conditions that permit peripheral nerve regeneration.

### Should know

- Nissl bodies, neurofilaments, axon collaterals and telodendria;
- axodendritic, axosomatic and less common synapse types;
- Golgi type I and Golgi type II neurons;
- chromatolysis and Wallerian degeneration;
- the roles of satellite cells, astrocytes and microglia.

### Useful extension

- nervous-system plasticity;
- the importance of progressive myelination during development;
- why a peripheral lesion can recover while an apparently similar central lesion
  may not.

## 1.1 Organising the nervous system

The source defines the nervous system as a complex network of cells and nerves
that carries messages between the brain, spinal cord and the rest of the body.
Its two broad roles are:

1. responding to changes inside and outside the body;
2. controlling and integrating body activities such as movement, circulation
   and respiration.

### Structural division

| Division | Main components | Main idea |
|---|---|---|
| Central nervous system (CNS) | brain and spinal cord | integration and command centre |
| Peripheral nervous system (PNS) | cranial nerves, spinal nerves and ganglia | communication between CNS and the body |

The CNS is protected by bone, cerebrospinal fluid and meninges. The PNS lies
outside the brain and spinal cord.

### Functional division

| Division | Main role |
|---|---|
| Somatic nervous system | sensory information and motor control of skeletal muscle |
| Autonomic nervous system | control of cardiac muscle, smooth muscle and glands |

Both systems have information travelling toward and away from the CNS. The words
**afferent** and **efferent** describe this direction:

- **afferent:** toward the CNS;
- **efferent:** away from the CNS.

### Memory hook

**Afferent Arrives; Efferent Exits.**

## 1.2 Neurons and neuroglia

A **neuron** is the basic structural and functional unit of nervous tissue. It
is excitable, meaning it can generate and conduct electrical signals.

A **neuroglial cell** is a supporting cell. Glia regulate the neuronal
environment, provide metabolic support, form myelin, remove debris and assist
repair.

### Course qualification: neuron-to-glia ratio

The lecture repeats the traditional statement that glia outnumber neurons by
about 10:1. Do not treat this as a universal whole-brain ratio. The important
course point is that glia are abundant, regionally variable and indispensable
to neuronal function.

### Why neurons are vulnerable

Neurons have a high metabolic demand and depend continuously on oxygen and
glucose. The lecture uses “about five minutes without oxygen” to emphasise this
vulnerability. Treat that figure as a teaching approximation rather than an
identical death threshold for every neuron and every clinical situation.

Many mature neurons have very limited ability to divide. Loss can therefore be
lasting, although the nervous system retains plasticity and selected neuronal
populations can be renewed.

## 1.3 The neuronal cell body

The **cell body**, also called the **soma** or **perikaryon**, is the neuron’s
metabolic and synthetic centre.

It contains the nucleus and ordinary organelles, plus structures emphasised in
histology:

- **Nissl bodies/chromatophilic substance:** rough endoplasmic reticulum and
  free ribosomes used for protein synthesis;
- **neurofilaments/neurofibrils:** cytoskeletal elements that support the cell
  and its processes;
- pigments such as lipofuscin in some long-lived neurons.

Most neuronal cell bodies lie within the CNS. In the PNS, groups of cell bodies
form ganglia.

### Terminology pair

| Cell bodies | Axons/processes |
|---|---|
| CNS cluster = **nucleus** | CNS bundle = **tract** |
| PNS cluster = **ganglion** | PNS bundle = **nerve** |

This distinction is a common examination target.

## 1.4 Dendrites

**Dendrites** are usually numerous, tapering and extensively branched. Their
main role is to receive signals and conduct local electrical changes toward the
soma.

Their branching increases receptive surface area. Nissl substance may extend
into the proximal dendrite but not along the mature axon.

### Structure–function link

```text
many branches
→ large receptive surface
→ many synaptic inputs
→ integration at soma/axon hillock
```

## 1.5 The axon

A neuron ordinarily has one axon. It begins at the **axon hillock**, continues
as a long slender process and conducts impulses away from the soma.

Possible features include:

- **axon collaterals:** side branches along the axon;
- **telodendria:** fine terminal branches;
- **axon terminals/synaptic boutons:** terminal enlargements containing
  synaptic vesicles.

The axon lacks Nissl bodies and a Golgi apparatus. The high-yield structural
point is that the soma supplies most of the axon’s proteins and organelles by
axonal transport.

> **Course qualification:** The slide phrase “no protein synthesis in axon”
> should not be turned into an absolute claim about every axonal compartment.
> For this module, remember that mature axons lack rough ER/Nissl substance and
> depend heavily on the soma.

### Golgi type I and II neurons

- **Golgi type I:** long axon carrying information to distant sites; examples
  include cortical pyramidal cells and spinal motor neurons.
- **Golgi type II:** short axon acting locally; examples include many cortical
  and spinal interneurons.

## 1.6 Synapses

A **synapse** is a specialised functional contact between a neuron and another
neuron, an effector cell or a sensory receptor cell.

At a typical chemical synapse:

1. an impulse reaches the presynaptic terminal;
2. synaptic vesicles release neurotransmitter;
3. transmitter crosses the synaptic cleft;
4. it binds receptors on the postsynaptic cell;
5. the postsynaptic cell changes its electrical or biochemical activity.

### Core components

- **presynaptic neuron:** conducts the signal toward the synapse;
- **synaptic vesicles:** membrane-bound stores of neurotransmitter;
- **synaptic cleft:** narrow gap between cells;
- **postsynaptic membrane:** receptor-bearing receiving surface.

Chemical transmission is functionally one-way because the release machinery is
presynaptic and the main receptor apparatus is postsynaptic.

### Synapse types

| Type | Contact |
|---|---|
| Axodendritic | axon terminal to dendrite; most common in the lecture |
| Axosomatic | axon terminal to soma |
| Axoaxonic | axon to another axon |
| Dendrodendritic | dendrite to dendrite |
| Dendrosomatic | dendrite to soma |

A neuron-to-muscle synapse is a **neuromuscular junction**.

## 1.7 Structural classification of neurons

Structural classification depends on the number and arrangement of processes
leaving the soma.

| Type | Structure | Typical course location/function |
|---|---|---|
| Multipolar | many dendrites and one axon | most motor neurons and interneurons |
| Bipolar | one dendrite and one axon | retina, olfactory epithelium, inner ear |
| Pseudounipolar | one process that divides into peripheral and central branches | sensory neurons in spinal and cranial sensory ganglia |

Pseudounipolar neurons begin as bipolar cells during development. Their
peripheral branch receives sensory information; their central branch carries it
into the CNS.

### Common trap

**Wrong idea:** A pseudounipolar sensory neuron carries the signal through the
soma before it continues centrally.

**Correction:** The conducting process bypasses the soma along a continuous
branch; the soma remains metabolically essential but is displaced to the side.

## 1.8 Functional classification of neurons

Functional classification depends on the direction and role of information
flow.

### Sensory neurons

**Sensory/afferent neurons** carry information from receptors toward the CNS.
Most general sensory neurons are pseudounipolar.

### Motor neurons

**Motor/efferent neurons** carry commands from the CNS to effectors.

- somatic motor neurons innervate skeletal muscle;
- visceral/autonomic motor pathways regulate smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and
  glands.

Most somatic motor neurons are multipolar.

### Interneurons

**Interneurons/association neurons** lie within the CNS and connect sensory and
motor circuits. They are usually multipolar and perform integration rather than
directly connecting the CNS to a peripheral receptor or effector.

## 1.9 Neuroglia of the CNS

### Astrocytes

Astrocytes are large, branched, star-shaped glia located between neurons and
blood vessels.

Functions in the source include:

- structural support and anchoring;
- contribution to the blood–brain barrier environment;
- regulation of ions and extracellular fluid;
- uptake and recycling of neurotransmitters;
- metabolic exchange between vessels and neurons;
- influence on synapse formation and growth;
- response to injury and scar formation.

Astrocytes do not merely “hold neurons together.” They actively regulate the
environment in which signalling occurs.

### Microglia

Microglia are the resident phagocytic immune cells of the CNS. They survey
tissue, remove debris and respond to injury or infection.

> **Course qualification:** The source uses “derived from monocytes” as a
> shorthand. The durable learning point is that microglia belong to the
> macrophage lineage and serve as resident CNS phagocytes; do not picture them
> as ordinary mature circulating monocytes simply entering normal brain tissue.

### Ependymal cells

Ependymal cells line:

- brain ventricles;
- the central canal of the spinal cord.

Specialised ependymal cells participate in the choroid plexus and cerebrospinal
fluid production. Cilia help move CSF along ventricular surfaces.

### Oligodendrocytes

Oligodendrocytes form myelin in the CNS. One oligodendrocyte can extend several
processes and myelinate segments on more than one axon.

## 1.10 Neuroglia of the PNS

### Satellite cells

Satellite cells surround neuronal cell bodies in peripheral ganglia. Their
supportive and environmental role is comparable in broad terms to astrocytes
around CNS neurons.

### Schwann cells

Schwann cells, also called neurolemmocytes:

- surround peripheral axons;
- form PNS myelin;
- form the outer **neurolemma**;
- contribute to the regeneration pathway after peripheral nerve injury.

A single Schwann cell forms one myelin segment of one axon. This contrasts with
an oligodendrocyte, which can myelinate portions of several CNS axons.

## Glial comparison

| Cell | Location | High-yield function |
|---|---|---|
| Astrocyte | CNS | environmental regulation, metabolic support, injury response |
| Microglia | CNS | resident phagocyte/immune surveillance |
| Ependymal cell | CNS | lines ventricles and central canal; CSF-related function |
| Oligodendrocyte | CNS | myelinates segments of several axons |
| Satellite cell | PNS | supports cell bodies in ganglia |
| Schwann cell | PNS | surrounds axons; one myelin segment; regeneration support |

## 1.11 Myelin

**Myelin** is a multilayered lipid- and protein-rich sheath wrapped around
selected axons.

Functions:

- electrically insulates the axon;
- reduces current leakage;
- increases conduction speed;
- allows rapid saltatory propagation between nodes.

### Nodes of Ranvier

PNS myelin is interrupted at regular gaps called **nodes of Ranvier**. These
gaps contain a high density of ion channels and allow the action potential to be
regenerated from node to node.

### CNS versus PNS myelin

| Feature | CNS | PNS |
|---|---|---|
| Myelinating cell | oligodendrocyte | Schwann cell |
| One cell serves | several axons/segments | one segment of one axon |
| Neurolemma/regeneration tube | absent | present |
| Repair environment | relatively restrictive | more supportive when pathway remains intact |

### Clinical application

Demyelination slows or blocks conduction even when the axon initially remains
present. The source uses multiple sclerosis as the classic CNS demyelinating
example.

## 1.12 Gray and white matter

### Gray matter

Gray matter contains a high concentration of:

- neuronal cell bodies;
- dendrites;
- synapses and terminals;
- unmyelinated axons;
- neuroglia.

Examples in the source include cerebral cortex, basal nuclei, thalamus,
hypothalamus and cranial-nerve nuclei.

### White matter

White matter consists mainly of myelinated axons. Its pale appearance reflects
the lipid-rich myelin.

### Common trap

**Wrong idea:** Gray matter contains no myelin, and white matter contains only
axons.

**Correction:** These names describe the dominant components. Both regions also
contain glia, blood vessels and mixed neural elements.

## 1.13 Structure of a peripheral nerve

A peripheral nerve is an organ containing axons, Schwann cells, blood vessels
and connective-tissue sheaths.

| Sheath | What it surrounds |
|---|---|
| Endoneurium | an individual nerve fibre/axon and its Schwann-cell investment |
| Perineurium | a fascicle, or bundle of nerve fibres |
| Epineurium | the entire nerve and its fascicles |

The perineurium forms an important controlled barrier around each fascicle.
The epineurium provides toughness and allows the nerve to tolerate movement.

### Memory hook

**Endo = individual. Peri = packet. Epi = entire nerve.**

## 1.14 Plasticity, injury and regeneration

**Plasticity** is the ability of the nervous system to change with experience.
The source lists:

- new dendritic growth;
- altered protein synthesis;
- changes in synaptic contacts;
- continued myelination during development.

Plasticity is not identical to regeneration. A circuit can reorganise without
replacing a destroyed neuron.

### Peripheral nerve injury sequence

1. The neuronal soma reacts; Nissl substance disperses in **chromatolysis**.
2. The distal axon and myelin fragment in **Wallerian degeneration**.
3. Macrophages remove debris.
4. Schwann cells proliferate and align within the remaining neurolemma.
5. A regeneration tube forms across a suitable gap.
6. Axonal sprouts grow from the proximal stump.
7. A sprout that enters the correct tube may reach the original receptor or
   effector.

### Conditions for successful PNS regeneration

- the neuronal cell body survives;
- the Schwann-cell pathway remains functional;
- the gap is not too large;
- collagen scar does not block the route;
- the distal target remains viable;
- the regenerating axon enters the correct pathway.

The source gives an approximate growth rate of 1.5 mm/day. Treat this as a
useful course estimate, not a guaranteed rate in every injury.

### CNS repair

The lecture contrasts PNS regeneration with limited CNS repair. Astrocytic scar
formation, inhibitory molecules and the absence of a Schwann-cell regeneration
tube contribute to poor regrowth.

> **Course qualification:** “Little or no repair” should be understood as
> markedly limited axonal regeneration, not an absolute absence of plasticity or
> any biological repair response.

## Worked example: localising a peripheral nerve injury

A patient has a lacerated peripheral nerve. The neuronal cell bodies remain
intact, but the distal axons degenerate.

Reason through the recovery:

```text
intact soma
→ increased synthetic response and axonal sprouts
→ Schwann cells preserve a pathway
→ sprouts enter regeneration tubes
→ correct target may be reinnervated
```

If the gap is large or scar tissue disrupts fascicular alignment, recovery is
poor even though the cell body survives.

## Common traps

### “A nerve is a single axon.”

A nerve is an organ containing many axons grouped into fascicles, glia,
connective tissue and vessels.

### “All glia form myelin.”

Only oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells form myelin. Other glia have different
supportive roles.

### “White matter is where neuronal cell bodies are found.”

White matter is dominated by myelinated axons; gray matter contains most cell
bodies and synapses.

### “PNS regeneration always restores normal function.”

Regeneration depends on pathway continuity, gap size, scarring, target survival
and correct reconnection.

## Key terms

- **Neuron** — excitable cell specialised for receiving and transmitting
  information.
- **Neuroglia** — non-neuronal cells that support, regulate and protect nervous
  tissue.
- **Soma/perikaryon** — metabolic and synthetic centre of a neuron.
- **Nissl body** — rough ER and ribosomal material in the soma and proximal
  dendrites.
- **Dendrite** — neuronal process specialised mainly for receiving input.
- **Axon** — neuronal process conducting signals away from the soma.
- **Axon hillock** — region where the axon begins and impulses are initiated.
- **Synapse** — specialised communication junction between cells.
- **Ganglion** — cluster of neuronal cell bodies in the PNS.
- **Nucleus** — cluster of neuronal cell bodies in the CNS.
- **Tract** — bundle of axons in the CNS.
- **Nerve** — bundle of axons in the PNS.
- **Myelin** — multilayered insulating sheath around selected axons.
- **Node of Ranvier** — gap between adjacent myelin segments.
- **Neurolemma** — outer Schwann-cell cytoplasm around a PNS axon.
- **Chromatolysis** — dispersal of Nissl substance after axonal injury.
- **Wallerian degeneration** — degeneration of the distal axon and myelin after
  separation from the soma.
- **Fascicle** — bundle of nerve fibres enclosed by perineurium.

## Memory hook

Think of the neuron as a tree connected to a cable:

```text
dendritic branches receive
→ soma maintains and integrates
→ axon carries
→ terminal boutons release
→ synapse transfers
```

## One-minute summary

- CNS means brain and spinal cord; PNS means nerves and ganglia.
- Dendrites receive, the soma maintains and integrates, and the axon conducts.
- A chemical synapse contains a presynaptic terminal, vesicles, cleft and
  postsynaptic receptors.
- Multipolar, bipolar and pseudounipolar describe structure; sensory, motor and
  interneuron describe function.
- Oligodendrocytes myelinate CNS axons; Schwann cells myelinate PNS axons.
- Gray matter is rich in cell bodies and synapses; white matter is rich in
  myelinated axons.
- Endoneurium surrounds a fibre, perineurium a fascicle and epineurium the whole
  nerve.
- PNS regeneration is possible only when the soma, Schwann pathway and target
  conditions are favourable.

# 2. Connective Tissue

## Big picture

Connective tissue is the body’s supporting and integrating tissue. Unlike
epithelium, in which cells are tightly packed, connective-tissue cells are
separated by an **extracellular matrix (ECM)**.

The matrix determines much of the tissue’s behaviour:

```text
cell type
+ ground substance
+ fibre type
+ fibre arrangement
= tissue function
```

A tendon resists pull because its collagen is densely parallel. Areolar tissue
allows diffusion and immune movement because it contains abundant ground
substance and loosely arranged fibres.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- cells versus extracellular matrix;
- ground substance and its functions;
- collagen, reticular and elastic fibres;
- resident versus wandering cells;
- fibroblast, macrophage, mast cell, plasma cell and adipocyte;
- loose versus dense connective tissue;
- areolar, adipose and reticular tissue;
- dense regular, dense irregular and elastic connective tissue;
- structure–function reasoning from fibre amount and direction.

### Should know

- embryonic mesenchyme and mucous connective tissue;
- blast, cyte and clast suffixes;
- major collagen types listed in the lecture;
- why vitamin C deficiency weakens connective tissue;
- how connective tissue supports epithelium, vessels and nerves.

### Useful extension

- white versus brown adipose tissue;
- sites where connective-tissue patterns are especially recognisable;
- how extracellular matrix participates in inflammation and repair.

## 2.1 What defines connective tissue?

Connective tissue is widespread in skin, membranes, muscles, nerves and organs.
Its functions include:

- connecting tissues;
- holding organs together and giving shape;
- providing mechanical support;
- permitting exchange between blood and other tissues;
- transporting substances;
- immune defence;
- storing energy as fat.

The source describes connective tissue as highly vascular with exceptions such
as cartilage and poorly vascular tendons. The safer general model is that
vascularity varies widely among connective tissues.

## 2.2 The extracellular matrix

The **extracellular matrix** is the non-cellular material surrounding the cells.
It contains:

1. **ground substance**;
2. **fibres**.

The matrix is not inert packing. It provides mechanical support and influences
cell behaviour, diffusion and tissue repair.

### Ground substance

Ground substance is a colourless, hydrated gel composed of materials such as:

- glycosaminoglycans;
- proteoglycans;
- adhesive glycoproteins;
- water and dissolved substances.

Functions include:

- lubrication;
- resistance to compression;
- diffusion of nutrients and waste;
- retention of tissue fluid;
- biochemical and physical barrier function.

Its consistency can range from fluid to gelatinous or mineralised, depending on
the connective tissue.

### Cause–effect chain

```text
hydrophilic matrix molecules bind water
→ tissue retains a hydrated gel
→ dissolved substances diffuse through it
→ cells receive nutrients and remove waste
→ compression is resisted
```

## 2.3 Connective-tissue fibres

### Collagen fibres

Collagen is the major tensile structural protein. Collagen fibres are strong,
flexible and generally unbranched.

They are concentrated where tissue must resist pulling, stretching or tearing.

The lecture lists:

| Collagen type | High-yield course location |
|---|---|
| Type I | bone, tendon and many fibrous tissues |
| Type II | hyaline and elastic cartilage |
| Type III | reticular fibres |
| Type IV | basement membrane/basal lamina |
| Type V | associated with tissues including blood vessels |

The current section focuses on connective tissue proper; the specialised
cartilage and bone applications are mentioned only to place the collagen types.

### Reticular fibres

Reticular fibres are delicate, branching fibres made largely of type III
collagen. They form a supporting meshwork in:

- liver;
- spleen;
- lymph nodes;
- bone marrow and other haemopoietic organs;
- around capillaries, nerve fibres and muscle cells.

They can be demonstrated with silver stains.

### Elastic fibres

Elastic fibres contain elastin supported by fibrillin-rich microfibrils. They
branch and recoil after stretching.

Important sites in the source include:

- large elastic arteries;
- lungs;
- skin;
- bladder;
- tracheobronchial tissues;
- selected ligaments.

### Compare the fibres

| Fibre | Main property | Pattern | Representative role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Collagen | tensile strength | thick, usually non-branching bundles | resists pulling |
| Reticular | delicate support | fine branching network | stroma around cells |
| Elastic | stretch and recoil | thin branching fibres/sheets | restores original shape |

### Common trap

**Wrong idea:** “Elastic fibres are strongest because they stretch.”

**Correction:** Elastic fibres specialise in recoil. Collagen provides much of
the tensile strength that resists tearing.

## 2.4 Connective-tissue cells

Cells are described as **resident/fixed** or **wandering/transient**.

### Resident cells

Resident cells remain relatively stable within the tissue.

#### Fibroblasts

Fibroblasts are the principal matrix-producing cells of connective tissue
proper. They synthesise:

- collagen fibres;
- elastic-fibre components;
- reticular fibres;
- ground-substance molecules.

Active fibroblasts tend to have more abundant cytoplasm and a prominent
euchromatic nucleus. Quiescent fibrocytes appear smaller and less active.

#### Macrophages

Macrophages are phagocytic cells derived from monocyte-lineage precursors. They:

- ingest microbes and debris;
- participate in inflammation;
- present antigens;
- help coordinate repair.

Some remain fixed in particular tissues; others are recruited during injury.

#### Mast cells

Mast cells contain granules rich in mediators such as histamine and heparin.
They participate in inflammatory and allergic responses.

Histamine increases vascular dilation and permeability, helping explain rapid
swelling in some reactions.

#### Adipocytes

Adipocytes store triglyceride and also function as metabolically active cells.

#### Adult stem cells

Resident progenitor cells can contribute to repair and differentiation in
selected tissues.

### Wandering cells

Wandering cells enter connective tissue from blood, often in response to a
specific stimulus:

- lymphocytes;
- plasma cells;
- neutrophils;
- eosinophils;
- basophils;
- monocytes and recruited macrophages.

#### Plasma cells

Plasma cells develop from B lymphocytes and secrete antibodies.

### Cell-name suffixes

- **-blast:** actively produces matrix;
- **-cyte:** mature cell that maintains matrix;
- **-clast:** resorbs or breaks down matrix.

This pattern is useful across cartilage and bone, even though those specialised
tissues are outside the present module’s detailed scope.

## 2.5 Classification of connective tissue

The lecture divides connective tissue into:

1. embryonic connective tissue;
2. connective tissue proper;
3. specialised connective tissue such as cartilage, bone and blood.

The present module concentrates on connective tissue proper.

### A useful quantitative idea

```text
cells + ground substance > fibres
→ loose connective tissue

fibres > cells + ground substance
→ dense connective tissue
```

This is a learning model, not a formal equation.

## 2.6 Embryonic connective tissue

### Mesenchyme

Mesenchyme contains stellate or spindle-shaped cells in semifluid ground
substance with delicate fibres. It gives rise to supporting/connective tissues
during development.

### Mucous connective tissue

Mucous connective tissue contains widely scattered fibroblast-like cells in a
viscous, gelatinous matrix with fine collagen. The classic example is
**Wharton’s jelly** of the umbilical cord.

## 2.7 Loose connective tissue

Loose connective tissue contains abundant ground substance and cells with fewer,
more loosely arranged fibres.

### Areolar connective tissue

Areolar tissue is widely distributed.

Locations in the source include:

- beneath epithelium as lamina propria;
- around small nerves and blood vessels;
- around individual muscle fibres;
- packaging and supporting organs;
- parts of the hypodermis.

Functions:

- permits diffusion;
- supports epithelium;
- provides a route for vessels and nerves;
- accommodates immune and inflammatory cells;
- allows movement between adjacent structures.

Because epithelium is avascular, its underlying areolar connective tissue is
essential for nutrient exchange.

### Adipose tissue

Adipose tissue consists mainly of adipocytes.

#### White adipose tissue

White adipocytes are typically **unilocular**: one large lipid droplet displaces
the nucleus and cytoplasm to the periphery.

Functions include:

- energy storage;
- insulation;
- cushioning;
- endocrine signalling.

#### Brown adipose tissue

Brown adipocytes are **multilocular**, contain many mitochondria and have a rich
blood supply. They produce heat and are especially prominent in infants, with
smaller deposits in adults.

### Reticular connective tissue

Reticular tissue contains a network of reticular fibres and reticular cells.

It forms the stroma of organs such as:

- lymph nodes;
- spleen;
- liver;
- bone marrow.

The network supports large numbers of free cells while allowing them to move
through the tissue.

## 2.8 Dense connective tissue

Dense connective tissue contains abundant fibres, fewer cells and less ground
substance.

### Dense regular connective tissue

Dense regular tissue has collagen bundles arranged mainly in the same
direction. Fibroblasts lie between the bundles.

Locations:

- tendons;
- ligaments;
- aponeuroses;
- corneal stroma is listed in the supplied material as an organised dense
  collagenous tissue.

Function:

- high tensile resistance along the main direction of pull.

### Dense irregular connective tissue

Dense irregular tissue contains collagen bundles arranged in many directions.

Locations in the source include:

- dermis;
- organ and joint capsules;
- submucosa;
- periosteum;
- perichondrium;
- fibrous pericardium.

Function:

- resists stress from several directions.

### Elastic connective tissue

Elastic connective tissue is dominated by elastic fibres.

Locations include:

- large elastic arteries;
- selected ligaments;
- bronchial and tracheal tissues;
- lung;
- true vocal cords.

Function:

- stretches and returns toward its original shape.

## Dense-tissue comparison

| Tissue | Fibre arrangement | Main mechanical demand | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dense regular | parallel/ordered | pull mainly in one direction | tendon |
| Dense irregular | interwoven/random | stress from many directions | dermis, capsule |
| Elastic | abundant elastic fibres | repeated stretch and recoil | elastic artery |

## 2.9 How to identify connective tissue on a slide

Use a sequence rather than guessing by colour.

1. **Assess cell spacing.** Are cells separated by abundant matrix?
2. **Identify the dominant fibre.** Thick collagen, fine reticular network or
   branching elastic fibres?
3. **Assess fibre direction.** Parallel or irregular?
4. **Estimate ground substance and cell number.** Loose or dense?
5. **Look for characteristic cells.** Fibroblasts, adipocytes, inflammatory
   cells?
6. **Connect morphology to mechanical function.**

### Worked examples

#### Tendon

```text
thick parallel collagen
+ flattened fibroblast nuclei in rows
+ little ground substance
→ dense regular connective tissue
→ resists force along one axis
```

#### Dermis

```text
thick collagen bundles in several directions
+ fewer cells
→ dense irregular connective tissue
→ withstands multidirectional stress
```

#### Lamina propria

```text
loose fibres
+ abundant cells and ground substance
+ vessels beneath epithelium
→ areolar connective tissue
→ diffusion, support and immune surveillance
```

## 2.10 Clinical links

### Scurvy

Vitamin C is required for normal collagen formation. Deficiency weakens
collagen-containing tissues and can produce:

- bleeding;
- poor wound healing;
- loose teeth;
- fragile supporting tissues.

### Inflammation

Loose connective tissue is a common site of inflammation because it contains
vessels, ground substance, mast cells and routes for wandering leukocytes.

### Wound repair

Fibroblasts produce collagen and ground substance during repair. Excessive or
disorganised deposition can create scar tissue that changes normal tissue
function.

## Common traps

### “Connective tissue is defined mainly by its cells.”

The extracellular matrix is the defining feature. Different matrix composition
can make tissues with similar cells behave very differently.

### “All connective tissue is highly vascular.”

Vascularity varies. Tendons have limited blood supply and cartilage is
avascular.

### “Reticular fibres and elastic fibres perform the same function because both
branch.”

Reticular fibres form delicate support networks; elastic fibres stretch and
recoil.

### “Dense regular means the fibres are perfectly straight.”

It means they are predominantly aligned to resist force along a principal axis.

## Key terms

- **Connective tissue** — tissue in which cells are dispersed within an
  extracellular matrix.
- **Extracellular matrix** — ground substance plus fibres surrounding cells.
- **Ground substance** — hydrated non-fibrous matrix supporting diffusion and
  compression resistance.
- **Collagen fibre** — high-tensile-strength structural fibre.
- **Reticular fibre** — fine type III collagen network supporting cells and
  organs.
- **Elastic fibre** — elastin-containing fibre that stretches and recoils.
- **Fibroblast** — principal matrix-producing cell of connective tissue proper.
- **Macrophage** — phagocytic immune cell involved in defence and cleanup.
- **Mast cell** — granulated cell releasing inflammatory mediators.
- **Plasma cell** — antibody-secreting descendant of a B lymphocyte.
- **Adipocyte** — lipid-storing and metabolically active connective-tissue cell.
- **Mesenchyme** — embryonic connective tissue giving rise to supporting
  tissues.
- **Lamina propria** — connective tissue beneath a mucosal epithelium.
- **Stroma** — supporting connective-tissue framework of an organ.
- **Areolar tissue** — widespread loose connective tissue.
- **Dense regular connective tissue** — dense fibres aligned mainly in one
  direction.
- **Dense irregular connective tissue** — dense fibres arranged in multiple
  directions.

## Memory hook

For the matrix, use **G-F-C**:

```text
Ground substance
Fibres
Cells
```

For the fibres:

```text
Collagen carries tension.
Reticular creates a net.
Elastic extends and returns.
```

## One-minute summary

- Connective tissue is defined by cells dispersed in extracellular matrix.
- Ground substance supports diffusion, lubrication and compression resistance.
- Collagen resists tension, reticular fibres form support networks and elastic
  fibres recoil.
- Fibroblasts make matrix; macrophages clean and defend; mast cells release
  mediators; plasma cells secrete antibodies; adipocytes store fat.
- Loose tissue has more cells and ground substance; dense tissue has more
  fibres.
- Areolar tissue supports epithelium, adipose stores energy and reticular tissue
  forms stroma.
- Dense regular tissue resists one main direction; dense irregular tissue
  resists several directions; elastic tissue stretches and recoils.

# 3. Epithelium and Tissue Integration

## Big picture

Epithelium covers surfaces, lines cavities and tubes, and forms glands. Its cells
are closely packed and organised into sheets.

An epithelium is understood through four questions:

1. How many cell layers are present?
2. What shape are the cells at the free surface?
3. What apical specialisations are present?
4. What function does that structure support?

Epithelium does not work alone. It is attached to a basement membrane and
supported by underlying connective tissue, which provides nutrients, immune
cells and mechanical support.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- covering/lining versus glandular epithelium;
- cellularity, polarity, avascularity, junctions and regeneration;
- basement membrane;
- simple, stratified and pseudostratified organisation;
- squamous, cuboidal and columnar cell shape;
- microvilli, cilia, stereocilia and goblet cells;
- major epithelial types, locations and functions;
- transitional epithelium;
- exocrine versus endocrine glands;
- simple versus compound ducts and tubular versus acinar secretory units;
- merocrine, apocrine and holocrine secretion.

### Should know

- endothelium, mesothelium and endocardium;
- keratinised versus non-keratinised stratified squamous epithelium;
- serous, mucous and mixed glands;
- intercalated, striated and larger excretory ducts;
- epithelial–connective-tissue integration during healing.

### Useful extension

- parakeratinisation;
- endocrine-cell arrangements in cords, clumps or follicles;
- how epithelial metaplasia can change tissue function.

## 3.1 What epithelium does

The source lists four broad functions:

- protection;
- absorption;
- secretion;
- filtration.

Additional functional outcomes follow from these:

- sensation at specialised surfaces;
- transport of material by cilia;
- controlled exchange between compartments;
- gland formation.

### Two broad categories

- **covering/lining epithelium:** forms surface sheets;
- **glandular epithelium:** forms secretory structures.

## 3.2 Cardinal features

### Cellularity

Epithelial cells are closely packed with little extracellular matrix between
them. This allows a continuous barrier.

### Specialised contacts

Junctions link neighbouring cells and control the passage of substances between
them. The exact junction type varies, but the high-yield idea is that the sheet
behaves as an integrated barrier rather than as isolated cells.

### Polarity

An epithelial cell has distinct domains:

- **apical:** faces a lumen or external surface;
- **lateral:** contacts neighbouring cells;
- **basal:** attaches to basement membrane.

Different domains contain different proteins and specialisations.

### Avascularity with innervation

Epithelium contains no blood vessels. Oxygen and nutrients diffuse from
capillaries in the underlying connective tissue across the basement membrane.
Many epithelia are nevertheless well innervated.

### Regeneration

Surface cells are continually lost and replaced. Basal or stem/progenitor cells
divide to maintain the sheet. The source gives intestinal epithelium as an
example of rapid renewal.

## 3.3 Basement membrane

The basal surface rests on a **basement membrane**, classically described as
basal lamina plus associated reticular connective-tissue components.

Functions:

- anchors epithelium;
- supports cell polarity;
- separates epithelium from connective tissue;
- acts as a selective filtration interface;
- provides a surface for migration during growth and repair.

### Tissue integration

```text
epithelial cells form the barrier
→ basement membrane anchors and filters
→ connective tissue supplies vessels, immune cells and mechanical support
```

Damage to any part of this unit can disrupt the whole tissue.

## 3.4 Classifying epithelium

Use a two-step algorithm.

### Step 1: count layers

- **simple:** one layer; every cell contacts the basement membrane;
- **stratified:** two or more layers;
- **pseudostratified:** one layer that appears multilayered because nuclei lie at
  different heights.

### Step 2: name the apical cell shape

- **squamous:** wider than tall;
- **cuboidal:** approximately as tall as wide;
- **columnar:** taller than wide.

Stratified epithelium is named according to the shape of the most superficial
cells, not the basal cells.

### Common trap

**Wrong idea:** If nuclei are at several levels, the epithelium is stratified.

**Correction:** In pseudostratified epithelium, all cells contact the basement
membrane even though not all reach the surface.

## 3.5 Apical specialisations

### Microvilli

Microvilli are short, non-motile, finger-like projections that increase surface
area.

They form a **brush border** or **striated border** when densely packed.
Examples include absorptive intestinal cells and kidney tubules.

### Cilia

Cilia are motile projections that move material along the epithelial surface.

Examples:

- respiratory tract: moves mucus;
- uterine tube: helps move the oocyte and fluid.

### Stereocilia

Stereocilia are long, non-motile microvilli.

Examples in the source:

- epididymis and vas deferens;
- sensory hair cells of the inner ear.

### Goblet cells

Goblet cells are unicellular exocrine glands. They produce mucin; mucin combines
with water to form mucus.

Mucus lubricates and protects epithelial surfaces.

## 3.6 Simple squamous epithelium

Simple squamous epithelium is thin, allowing rapid diffusion and filtration.

Locations:

- alveoli;
- renal corpuscles;
- blood and lymphatic vessels;
- heart chambers;
- serous body cavities.

### Special names

- **endothelium:** lining of blood and lymphatic vessels;
- **endocardium:** lining of heart chambers;
- **mesothelium:** lining of closed serous cavities and covering of their
  organs.

### Structure–function link

```text
one layer of flat cells
→ short diffusion distance
→ efficient exchange or filtration
```

## 3.7 Simple cuboidal epithelium

Simple cuboidal cells combine a manageable barrier with active secretion and
absorption.

Locations in the source include:

- kidney tubules;
- thyroid follicles;
- ovary surface;
- small ducts and secretory portions of glands.

Kidney tubular cells may have microvilli where absorption is prominent.

## 3.8 Simple columnar epithelium

Simple columnar epithelium is specialised for absorption and secretion.

### Non-ciliated form

Locations:

- stomach and intestine;
- gallbladder;
- endocervix;
- selected gland ducts.

It may contain microvilli and goblet cells.

### Ciliated form

Locations:

- uterine tubes;
- selected small airways.

Cilia move material along the surface.

## 3.9 Pseudostratified columnar epithelium

Pseudostratified epithelium is a simple epithelium in which:

- all cells contact the basement membrane;
- only some reach the free surface;
- nuclei lie at different heights.

### Ciliated respiratory type

Lines much of the upper respiratory tract and trachea. Goblet cells produce
mucus and cilia move it.

### Stereociliated reproductive type

Found in structures such as epididymis and vas deferens, where long stereocilia
support absorption and secretion.

## 3.10 Stratified squamous epithelium

Stratified squamous epithelium is adapted to resist abrasion.

### Keratinised

Surface cells are flattened, dead and filled with keratin. This produces a
tough, water-resistant surface.

Locations in the source include:

- epidermis;
- gingiva and hard palate;
- selected oral surfaces.

### Non-keratinised

Surface cells remain living and moist.

Locations:

- oral cavity;
- oesophagus;
- vagina;
- anal canal;
- selected urethral and cervical surfaces.

### Parakeratinised

Parakeratinised surface cells retain nuclei even though the cytoplasm is
keratinised to a degree. This pattern occurs at selected oral surfaces.

## 3.11 Stratified cuboidal and columnar epithelium

These are relatively uncommon.

### Stratified cuboidal

Usually two layers of cuboidal cells.

Locations include larger ducts of:

- sweat glands;
- mammary glands;
- salivary glands.

Main function: protection with limited secretion/conduction.

### Stratified columnar

Contains several layers with columnar superficial cells.

Locations in the source include:

- parts of the male urethra;
- large gland ducts;
- part of the conjunctiva.

## 3.12 Transitional epithelium

**Transitional epithelium/urothelium** lines the urinary tract:

- calyces;
- ureters;
- bladder;
- proximal urethra.

It changes appearance with distension. Superficial cells are dome-shaped when
relaxed and become flatter when stretched.

The tissue must satisfy two requirements simultaneously:

1. stretch without tearing;
2. maintain a urine-resistant barrier.

### Common trap

**Wrong idea:** Transitional epithelium is simply stratified squamous epithelium
that has been stretched.

**Correction:** Urothelium is a specialised epithelial type with distinctive
surface cells and barrier properties.

## Epithelial comparison table

| Type | Main structural feature | Representative location | Main function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simple squamous | one flat layer | alveoli, vessels | diffusion/filtration |
| Simple cuboidal | one cuboidal layer | kidney tubules | absorption/secretion |
| Simple columnar | one tall layer | intestine | absorption/secretion |
| Pseudostratified ciliated columnar | all cells on basement membrane; nuclei at different levels | trachea | mucus secretion/transport |
| Stratified squamous keratinised | many layers; dead keratinised surface | epidermis | dry protection |
| Stratified squamous non-keratinised | many layers; moist living surface | oesophagus | abrasion resistance |
| Transitional | variable surface-cell shape | bladder | distension plus barrier |

## 3.13 Gland formation

Glands develop when epithelial cells grow into underlying connective tissue.

### Exocrine glands

Exocrine glands retain a duct to an epithelial surface.

They may be:

- **unicellular:** goblet cell;
- **multicellular:** secretory unit plus duct.

### Endocrine glands

Endocrine glands are ductless. They release hormones into interstitial fluid
and then blood.

Examples in the source include pituitary, thyroid, parathyroid, adrenal and
pineal glands.

Some organs, such as pancreas, ovary and testis, contain both endocrine and
exocrine components.

## 3.14 Classifying multicellular exocrine glands

### By duct branching

- **simple:** unbranched duct;
- **compound:** branched duct system.

### By secretory-unit shape

- tubular;
- acinar/alveolar;
- tubuloacinar/tubuloalveolar.

### By product

- **serous:** watery, protein-rich secretion;
- **mucous:** mucin-rich secretion;
- **mixed/seromucous:** both.

### By mode of release

| Mode | How product leaves |
|---|---|
| Merocrine | exocytosis; cell remains intact |
| Apocrine | apical portion of cell is released |
| Holocrine | whole cell disintegrates into secretion |

Examples in the source:

- merocrine: salivary glands, pancreas, many sweat glands;
- apocrine: mammary gland is used as the teaching example;
- holocrine: sebaceous gland.

## 3.15 Duct system

Multicellular exocrine glands often have:

- **intercalated ducts:** small, low-cuboidal intralobular ducts;
- **striated ducts:** columnar intralobular ducts that modify secretion;
- **interlobular/extralobular ducts:** larger ducts lined by simple or
  stratified epithelium.

The duct epithelium can modify the primary secretion rather than merely acting
as a passive pipe.

## Worked example: classifying an unknown lining

A slide shows one layer of tall cells, nuclei near the basal region, goblet cells
and a brush border.

Reasoning:

```text
one layer
→ simple

cells taller than wide
→ columnar

brush border + goblet cells
→ absorption and mucus secretion

likely intestinal-type simple columnar epithelium
```

A second slide shows nuclei at different heights, cilia and goblet cells, but
all cells contact basement membrane:

```text
one true layer with false stratification
→ pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium
```

## Common traps

### “Simple means the tissue is functionally unimportant.”

Simple describes layer number. Simple epithelia perform highly specialised
filtration, absorption and secretion.

### “Stratified epithelium is named from the deepest cells.”

It is named from the apical/superficial cells.

### “Microvilli and cilia are interchangeable.”

Microvilli increase surface area; cilia move material.

### “All glands have ducts.”

Exocrine glands have ducts. Endocrine glands are ductless.

### “Epithelium is nourished by its own capillaries.”

Epithelium is avascular and receives nutrients from underlying connective
tissue.

## Key terms

- **Epithelium** — closely packed tissue covering surfaces, lining cavities and
  forming glands.
- **Polarity** — structural and functional difference among apical, lateral and
  basal cell domains.
- **Basement membrane** — supportive interface between epithelium and
  connective tissue.
- **Simple epithelium** — one cell layer.
- **Stratified epithelium** — two or more cell layers.
- **Pseudostratified epithelium** — one layer appearing multilayered because
  nuclei lie at different heights.
- **Squamous** — wider than tall.
- **Cuboidal** — approximately as tall as wide.
- **Columnar** — taller than wide.
- **Microvillus** — non-motile projection increasing surface area.
- **Cilium** — motile projection moving material across a surface.
- **Stereocilium** — long non-motile microvillus.
- **Goblet cell** — unicellular mucus-secreting exocrine gland.
- **Endothelium** — simple squamous lining of vessels.
- **Mesothelium** — simple squamous lining of serous cavities.
- **Urothelium** — transitional epithelium of the urinary tract.
- **Exocrine gland** — gland secreting through a duct.
- **Endocrine gland** — ductless hormone-secreting gland.
- **Merocrine secretion** — release by exocytosis.
- **Apocrine secretion** — release with part of the apical cell.
- **Holocrine secretion** — release through disintegration of the whole cell.

## Memory hook

Classify an epithelium with **L-S-S**:

```text

Surface-cell Shape
Specialisations
```

For gland secretion:

```text

Apocrine = apex leaves.
Holocrine = whole cell leaves.
```

## One-minute summary

- Epithelium covers, lines and forms glands.
- It is highly cellular, polar, avascular, junction-rich and regenerative.
- The basement membrane anchors epithelium to underlying connective tissue.
- First count layers, then name apical cell shape.
- Pseudostratified epithelium is one layer despite staggered nuclei.
- Microvilli absorb, cilia move and goblet cells secrete mucus.
- Simple squamous supports exchange; stratified squamous supports protection;
  transitional epithelium supports urinary distension.
- Exocrine glands use ducts; endocrine glands are ductless.
- Exocrine glands are classified by duct branching, secretory-unit shape,
  product and mode of secretion.
