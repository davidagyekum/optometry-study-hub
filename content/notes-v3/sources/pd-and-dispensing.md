## How to use this module

The source begins with a practical warning: a correct lens prescription can still produce an unhappy patient when frame fit, optical-centre position, PD, lens choice, or final inspection is wrong.

A reliable dispensing process is therefore:

```text
understand the patient
-> select and adjust the frame
-> measure accurately
-> manufacture and verify
-> fit on the face
-> educate and document
```

> **Source approach:** The near-PD section contains a conflict at a distance PD of exactly 64 mm. Both source statements are shown and flagged. Do not hide the conflict in notes or assessment items.

<a id="quality-mistakes"></a>
# 1. Common Dispensing Mistakes That Go Unnoticed

## 1.1 Treating the prescription as the whole job

The prescription defines optical power, but successful spectacles also depend on:

- accurate PD;
- frame fit;
- optical-centre position;
- lens type and design;
- vertex distance, tilt, and wrap where relevant;
- quality control and patient instruction.

## 1.2 Choosing the wrong frame

A visually attractive frame may be unsuitable for the prescription or lens design.

Examples from the course logic:

- a large eye size can increase edge thickness in a high-minus lens;
- a shallow frame may not accommodate a progressive corridor;
- a frame that slips changes the effective fitting height;
- a poorly fitting bridge can cause asymmetry and unwanted decentration.

## 1.3 Rushing PD measurement

A small error may cause:

- eyestrain;
- headache;
- discomfort;
- adaptation difficulty;
- unwanted prism.

Sensitivity rises with lens power because Prentice's rule contains power as a multiplier.

## 1.4 Failing to adjust the frame before or after glazing

A frame handed over without final adjustment may:

- sit crooked;
- slip down the nose;
- press behind the ears;
- tilt one optical centre above the other;
- place a multifocal zone too high or low.

Adjustment is part of dispensing, not an optional cosmetic step.

## 1.5 Not asking lifestyle questions

The source recommends asking about:

- computer use;
- night driving;
- duration and distance of reading;
- occupational and hobby tasks.

A prescription alone does not reveal the patient's dominant working distances or visual priorities.

## 1.6 Poor expectation management

A first-time PAL wearer may need time and instruction. If this is not explained, normal early awareness may be interpreted as failure. Conversely, a real measurement or power error must not be dismissed as “just adaptation.”

## 1.7 Skipping final quality control

The deck lists defects that can be missed:

- wrong power;
- scratches;
- incorrect cylinder axis;
- loose screws;
- poor frame alignment.

The largest error is assuming that everything is correct without checking.

<a id="pd-concepts"></a>
# 2. PD Concepts

## 2.1 Definition

**Interpupillary distance (PD)** is the horizontal separation between the visual axes/pupil centres for a specified fixation distance.

The source gives a common total range of approximately 50-75 mm, while recognising individual variation.

## 2.2 Distance PD

**Distance PD** is measured while the patient fixates a distant target. The visual axes are relatively parallel, so the pupils are farther apart than during near fixation.

## 2.3 Near PD

**Near PD** is measured for a specified near working distance. Convergence turns both eyes inward, so:

\[
near\ PD<distance\ PD
\]

A near PD is incomplete without a working distance. A 40 cm near PD is not automatically the same as a 25 cm near PD.

## 2.4 Binocular and monocular PD

### Binocular PD

One number describing total separation between the two visual axes.

### Monocular PD

The distance from the facial midline/bridge reference to each visual axis:

```text
right monocular PD + left monocular PD = binocular PD
```

The two monocular values may differ. They are particularly important for PALs, high prescriptions, asymmetric faces, and prism control.

## 2.5 Measurement precision

The lecture states that PD should be accurate to within 0.5 mm. The practical meaning is that the examiner must:

- work at the same eye level as the patient;
- control fixation;
- avoid parallax;
- repeat doubtful measurements;
- distinguish distance from near PD;
- avoid assuming symmetry.

## 2.6 Why PD matters

### During refraction

The optical centres of trial lenses must align with the patient's visual axes. Misalignment induces prism and may alter the subjective response.

### During spectacle manufacture

The distance between finished lens optical centres must match the prescribed centration requirement. A mismatch induces unwanted prism.

<a id="pd-rule-methods"></a>
# 3. PD Rule Methods

## 3.1 Why the pupil centre can be difficult to judge

A small ruler is simple, but the pupil edge can be indistinct, especially in dark irides. The source therefore teaches alternative visible landmarks.

## 3.2 Limbus method

The **limbus** is the corneal boundary. The source measures from:

- temporal limbus of the patient's right eye;
- to nasal limbus of the patient's left eye.

This approximates centre-to-centre PD because the distance from pupil centre to limbus is assumed similar in the two eyes.

### Procedure

1. Seat the patient and examiner at the same eye level.
2. Ask the patient to fixate the designated distant target or the examiner's eye.
3. Hold the PD rule level and close to the brow/nasal bridge without touching the eyes.
4. Align zero with the selected right-eye limbal landmark.
5. Read at the corresponding left-eye landmark.
6. Avoid moving your head between alignments.
7. Repeat and compare readings.

## 3.3 Corneal reflex method

A pen torch creates a corneal light reflex. The separation between right and left reflexes is used as a visual-axis measure.

### Key controls

- central, steady fixation;
- torch held in the correct position;
- examiner and patient at the same level;
- no head movement during the reading;
- separate monocular readings when asymmetry or strabismus is relevant.

## 3.4 Canthus method

For a very young child or a person with strabismus or nystagmus, the source suggests measuring from the outer canthus of the right eye to the inner canthus of the left eye. It explicitly states that this is less accurate than the limbus method.

## 3.5 Parallax control

When the examiner views one eye from the wrong position, the ruler mark appears displaced relative to the pupil. A common manual method is to use alternate examiner eyes so each observed pupil is viewed more nearly straight on.

## 3.6 Repetition and reasonableness checks

- repeat at least once;
- compare with previous spectacles where available;
- check that right plus left monocular PD equals the binocular PD;
- confirm that near PD is smaller than distance PD;
- investigate an unusually large asymmetry instead of “correcting” it by assumption.

<a id="pupillometer"></a>
# 4. Pupillometer Measurement

## 4.1 What a pupillometer measures

The source describes a **pupillometer** as measuring the distance between the visual axes using corneal reflexes rather than simply estimating anatomical pupil centres.

It can measure:

- binocular distance PD;
- right and left monocular PD;
- near PD at selected working distances;
- patients with strabismus by alternate occlusion.

## 4.2 Advantages listed

- greater accuracy than a manual PD rule;
- better repeatability;
- monocular values are obtained quickly;
- each eye can be occluded;
- several near working distances can be selected;
- portable and usable with limited training.

## 4.3 Disadvantages listed

- more expensive than a rule and pen torch;
- less portable than the simplest manual tools;
- can be more difficult with young children.

## 4.4 Distance-PD method from the source

1. Set working-distance control to infinity.
2. Explain that you will measure the distance between the eyes.
3. Ask the patient to hold the instrument like binoculars.
4. Ask the patient to look at the centre of the internal target.
5. View through the instrument with both eyes open.
6. Occlude the patient's left eye to measure the right eye.
7. Move the right monocular slide until the hairline aligns with the right corneal reflex.
8. Uncover the left eye and occlude the right.
9. Align the left hairline with the left corneal reflex.
10. Read the binocular and/or monocular displays.

## 4.5 Near-PD method from the source

The same sequence is used, except the working-distance dial is set to the intended near distance, commonly 40 cm in the deck. The internal target then stimulates the corresponding convergence.

## 4.6 Avoiding instrument errors

- seat the nose bridge correctly;
- keep the instrument level;
- ensure the patient is actually looking at the target;
- align to the corneal reflex, not a random bright spot;
- record right and left values in the correct order;
- verify implausible values manually.

<a id="near-pd"></a>
# 5. Near PD: Measurement and Calculation

## 5.1 Direct measurement is preferred

The source states that near PD can be found with:

- corneal-reflex measurement;
- pupillometer at the selected distance;
- calculation when direct measurement is not possible.

It also states that calculation is less accurate than direct corneal-reflex measurement.

## 5.2 Geometric idea

At near, each eye converges. The lecture uses an insertion approximation related to dioptric demand:

\[
dioptric\ demand=\frac{100}{working\ distance\ in\ cm}
\]

At 40 cm:

\[
Demand=\frac{100}{40}=2.50\text{ D}
\]

The source then applies approximately 0.75 mm inset per eye for each dioptre of demand:

\[
inset\ per\ lens=2.50\times0.75=1.875\text{ mm}\approx2\text{ mm}
\]

Total reduction in binocular PD is about 4 mm.

Example:

\[
near\ PD=64-4=60\text{ mm}
\]

## 5.3 Source shortcut

The deck gives:

- if distance PD is greater than 64 mm, subtract 4 mm;
- if distance PD is less than or equal to 64 mm, subtract 3 mm.

Examples:

- 67 mm -> 63 mm;
- 57 mm -> 54 mm.

## 5.4 Source conflict at exactly 64 mm

The worked calculation uses 64 mm and subtracts 4 mm, producing 60 mm. The later shortcut says a value **less than or equal to 64 mm** should have 3 mm subtracted, which would produce 61 mm.

> **Course-source discrepancy:** Exactly 64 mm is internally inconsistent. The package preserves both statements. Codex should not create a normal-answer question at exactly 64 mm unless the question explicitly asks the student to identify the discrepancy. Direct measurement at the required working distance remains the preferred method.

## 5.5 Working-distance effect

As working distance becomes shorter:

- dioptric demand increases;
- convergence increases;
- near PD becomes smaller.

Therefore, a fixed “subtract 3 or 4 mm” rule is only a rough shortcut.

<a id="pd-prism"></a>
# 6. PD Error and Unwanted Prism

## 6.1 Optical-centre alignment

When the spectacle optical centre does not lie on the visual axis, the wearer looks through a decentered point and experiences prism.

\[
P=cF
\]

where:

- \(P\) = prism dioptres;
- \(c\) = decentration in centimetres;
- \(F\) = lens power in the meridian of decentration.

## 6.2 Why high powers are less tolerant

A 2 mm error is 0.2 cm.

For a 2.00 D lens:

\[
P=0.2\times2.00=0.4\Delta
\]

For an 8.00 D lens:

\[
P=0.2\times8.00=1.6\Delta
\]

The same physical error creates four times as much prism in the higher-powered lens.

## 6.3 Symptoms listed in the source

- asthenopia/eye strain;
- headache;
- dizziness;
- nausea;
- diplopia;
- blurred vision;
- inability to tolerate the spectacles in severe cases.

## 6.4 Where the error can arise

### Examiner error

- PD measured incorrectly;
- wrong fixation distance;
- parallax;
- right/left values transposed;
- frame not adjusted before marking.

### Laboratory/technician error

- finished optical centres do not match ordered centration;
- lenses swapped;
- wrong frame datum or decentration;
- glazing error changes lens position.

### Delivery error

- frame sits differently from the measured position;
- one side is higher;
- pads or temples are not adjusted;
- frame slips after a few minutes.

## 6.5 Dominant eye test in the source

The deck includes a simple pointing test:

1. Point at a distant object with both eyes open.
2. Close the right eye. If alignment remains, left-eye dominance is suggested.
3. Close the left eye. If alignment remains, right-eye dominance is suggested.

This is a basic screening demonstration, not a full binocular-vision evaluation.

<a id="final-dispensing"></a>
# 7. Final Dispensing and Quality Control

## 7.1 Before the patient arrives

- verify lens powers and axes;
- verify addition and prism;
- check optical-centre/PD and fitting-height data;
- inspect coatings and surfaces;
- tighten screws;
- confirm frame symmetry and alignment;
- confirm right and left lenses are correct.

## 7.2 On-face checks

- bridge and pads sit evenly;
- pupils align with intended reference points;
- frame is level;
- temples do not overpress;
- vertex distance is appropriate and reasonably equal;
- pantoscopic tilt and wrap are correct for the design;
- frame does not slip with normal movement.

## 7.3 Functional checks

- confirm distance clarity;
- confirm near task at the patient's working distance;
- confirm intermediate task when relevant;
- ask about comfort, not only acuity;
- observe posture and head movement.

## 7.4 Education

Explain:

- lens purpose and zones;
- cleaning and handling;
- expected adaptation;
- warning signs or persistent symptoms that need review;
- when to return for adjustment.

## 7.5 Final mastery checklist

You should be able to:

- distinguish distance, near, binocular, and monocular PD;
- explain why near PD is smaller;
- measure PD by limbus and corneal-reflex methods;
- perform the source's pupillometer sequence;
- calculate an approximate near PD for ordinary distances;
- identify the exact-64-mm source conflict;
- calculate induced prism from a PD error;
- explain why high-power wearers are more sensitive;
- complete a systematic final inspection and patient delivery.
