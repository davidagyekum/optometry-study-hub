## How to use these notes

The **ocular adnexa** are the structures next to the globe that protect it,
move the lids, create tear components and drain tears.

Do not study the eyelid as a disconnected list of tissues. Learn it as a
functional sequence:

```text
landmarks define the surface
→ skin and muscles move the lid
→ tarsus gives shape
→ glands and conjunctiva support the tear film
→ lacrimal gland supplies aqueous tears
→ blinking spreads tears
→ the drainage system removes them medially into the nose
```

The focus labels mean:

- **Must know:** central anatomy and pathway.
- **Should know:** supporting structural detail.
- **Useful extension:** valuable clinical depth.

> material is the primary organisational source. These notes paraphrase its
> teaching content and do not reproduce its copyrighted figures.

<a id="landmarks"></a>
# 1. Landmarks and Topography

## Big picture

The eyelids form a mobile protective opening in front of the globe. Their
surface landmarks are not only descriptive: they identify the transition
between lid regions, the route of tear drainage and the sites examined during a
clinical lid assessment.

The source gives four main lid functions:

1. protect the globe from light and mechanical damage;
2. produce components of the tear film;
3. distribute the tear film across the anterior surface;
4. direct tears toward the medial drainage system.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- ocular adnexa;
- palpebral aperture;
- medial and lateral canthi;
- tarsal and orbital parts of the upper lid;
- superior palpebral sulcus;
- eyebrow, epicanthus, caruncle and plica semilunaris;
- lacrimal papilla and punctum;
- why lid movement is essential to tear distribution and drainage.

### Should know

- how the upper-lid topography relates to the underlying tarsal plate;
- the sensory and protective role of lashes and eyebrow;
- why the medial canthus contains several specialised structures in a small
  area.

### Useful extension

- how abnormalities of lid position alter the apparent palpebral aperture;
- how landmark asymmetry can help localise muscle or nerve dysfunction.

## 1.1 Ocular adnexa

**Ocular adnexa** means structures located in close proximity to the eyeball.
The supplied module concentrates on:

- eyelids;
- conjunctiva;
- lacrimal gland;
- tear film;
- lacrimal drainage apparatus.

These structures create the immediate environment of the anterior eye.

## 1.2 Palpebral aperture and canthi

The **palpebral aperture** is the opening between the upper and lower lids.

At each end, the lids meet at a **canthus**:

- **medial canthus:** nasal angle;
- **lateral canthus:** temporal angle.

The aperture changes with blinking, lid elevation, fatigue, age and disease.
Therefore, an apparently “small eye” may actually reflect reduced lid opening
rather than a smaller globe.

### Structure–function link

```text
lid elevators and closers alter aperture
→ aperture controls globe exposure
→ exposure affects protection, tear evaporation and appearance
```

## 1.3 Tarsal and orbital parts

The upper lid has two topographic regions.

### Tarsal part

- lies over the globe;
- contains the tarsal plate;
- is relatively firm.

### Orbital part

- extends from the tarsal region toward the eyebrow;
- overlies the superior orbital tissues;
- is separated from the tarsal part by a surface furrow.

The **superior palpebral sulcus** marks the transition between these parts.

### Memory hook

**Tarsal touches the globe; orbital travels to the brow.**

## 1.4 Eyebrow

The eyebrow marks the upper boundary of the orbital lid.

Functions in the source:

- diverts moisture away from the palpebral aperture;
- contributes strongly to facial expression.

Its protective role is indirect: it helps reduce the flow of sweat or moisture
toward the eye.

## 1.5 Epicanthus

An **epicanthus** is a medial skin fold that covers part of the nasal scleral
area.

It alters the visible contour of the medial canthus. It should not be confused
with the plica semilunaris, which is conjunctival tissue.

## 1.6 Caruncle and plica semilunaris

### Lacrimal caruncle

The **caruncle** is a small fleshy pad at the medial canthus. The source notes
that it contains sebaceous and sweat glands and can trap foreign material.

### Plica semilunaris

The **plica semilunaris** is a crescent-shaped fold of bulbar conjunctiva near
the medial canthus.

### Distinguish them

| Structure | Tissue character | Location/appearance |
|---|---|---|
| Caruncle | small gland-containing tissue pad | most medial fleshy prominence |
| Plica semilunaris | fold of conjunctiva | just lateral to the caruncle |
| Epicanthus | skin fold | overlies part of the medial ocular surface |

## 1.7 Lacrimal papilla and punctum

Near the medial end of each lid is a small elevation called the **lacrimal
papilla**. At its summit is the **lacrimal punctum**.

The punctum is the entry to the drainage system.

```text
tear lake near medial canthus
→ upper and lower puncta
→ canaliculi
→ lacrimal sac
→ nasolacrimal duct
```

The puncta must remain positioned against the tear meniscus. Lid laxity or
malposition can therefore cause watering even when tear production is normal.

## 1.8 Functional map of the lid

| Function | Supporting structure |
|---|---|
| mechanical protection | rapid lid closure, thin mobile lid and lashes |
| light protection | narrowing or closure of palpebral aperture |
| tear production | meibomian, goblet, accessory and lacrimal glands |
| tear spreading | blinking and lid–globe contact |
| drainage | medial lid movement, puncta, canaliculi and lacrimal pump |

## Worked example: apparent unequal eye size

A patient appears to have one “smaller eye.”

A landmark-based assessment asks:

1. Is the globe actually smaller, or is the palpebral aperture reduced?
2. Is the upper lid lower?
3. Is the lower lid higher or malpositioned?
4. Are the canthi symmetric?
5. Is the eyebrow compensatorily elevated?
6. Is there an epicanthal fold changing the visible nasal sclera?

This prevents a surface observation from being mistaken for a globe-size
diagnosis.

## Common traps

### “The caruncle and plica semilunaris are the same structure.”

The caruncle is a small gland-containing tissue pad; the plica is a conjunctival
fold.

### “The punctum produces tears.”

The punctum is the entrance to the drainage system.

### “The tarsal part is named because it contains bone.”

The tarsal plate is a dense supporting structure, not a bone.

## Key terms

- **Ocular adnexa** — structures surrounding and supporting the globe.
- **Palpebral aperture** — opening between the eyelids.
- **Canthus** — angle where upper and lower lids meet.
- **Superior palpebral sulcus** — furrow separating tarsal and orbital parts of
  the upper lid.
- **Tarsal part** — firm lid region over the globe containing the tarsal plate.
- **Orbital part** — upper-lid region extending toward the eyebrow.
- **Epicanthus** — medial skin fold partly covering the nasal sclera.
- **Caruncle** — small medial tissue pad containing skin-associated glands.
- **Plica semilunaris** — crescentic bulbar-conjunctival fold.
- **Lacrimal papilla** — small elevation bearing a punctum.
- **Punctum** — opening into the lacrimal drainage system.

## Memory hook

At the medial canthus, move from medial to lateral:

```text
caruncle
→ plica semilunaris
→ bulbar conjunctiva
```

For drainage, remember:

**Punctum is a portal, not a producer.**

## One-minute summary

- Ocular adnexa protect the globe and maintain the tear environment.
- The palpebral aperture lies between the lids, ending at medial and lateral
  canthi.
- The tarsal part covers the globe; the orbital part extends toward the eyebrow.
- The superior palpebral sulcus separates the two parts.
- The caruncle is a small gland-containing pad; the plica is a conjunctival
  fold.
- The punctum lies on the lacrimal papilla and begins tear drainage.

3. What does the superior palpebral sulcus mark?
4. Distinguish epicanthus, caruncle and plica semilunaris.
5. Why must the punctum remain apposed to the tear meniscus?
6. Why can ptosis make an eye look smaller even when globe size is normal?

# 2. Skin, Lashes and Muscles

## Big picture

The upper lid must be light enough to move rapidly, strong enough to protect the
globe and precisely controlled enough to blink without damaging the ocular
surface.

Its movement depends mainly on three muscular systems:

- **orbicularis oculi:** closes the lid;
- **levator palpebrae superioris:** principal upper-lid elevator;
- **superior tarsal/Müller muscle:** provides a smaller sympathetically driven
  elevation.

The amount of ptosis helps distinguish failure of the major elevator from loss
of the smaller sympathetic contribution.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- why eyelid skin is unusually thin and mobile;
- lash arrangement and protective sensory role;
- orbital, palpebral and lacrimal portions of orbicularis;
- levator palpebrae superioris action and CN III supply;
- Müller muscle as sympathetic smooth muscle;
- marked versus mild ptosis;
- the orbital septum and levator aponeurosis.

### Should know

- approximate lash counts in the teaching deck;
- voluntary closure versus involuntary blinking;
- the possible contribution of the lacrimal orbicularis to the tear pump;
- why lid skin and muscle arrangement permit rapid movement.

### Useful extension

- how brow elevation may compensate for upper-lid droop;
- how blink failure can destabilise the ocular surface.

## 2.1 Eyelid skin

The source describes eyelid skin as very thin and loosely attached to the
underlying muscle.

This organisation supports:

- rapid folding;
- frequent opening and closing;
- minimal mass;
- fine facial expression.

The epidermis overlies a dermis rich in elastic fibres.

### Structure–function link

```text
thin skin + loose attachment + elastic dermis
→ low resistance to movement
→ rapid blink and expressive lid motion
```

## 2.2 Eyelashes

Eyelashes are arranged in approximately two to three rows.

The source gives approximate counts:

- upper lid: about 150;
- lower lid: about 75.

Their roles are not only mechanical. Contact with a lash can trigger protective
blinking before a foreign object reaches the cornea.

Glands associated with the lash region include:

- glands of Zeiss;
- glands of Moll;
- nearby meibomian openings.

These are studied in detail in the next section.

## 2.3 Orbicularis oculi

Orbicularis oculi is striated muscle located deep to the skin/subcutaneous
tissue. It closes the eyelids.

The source divides it into three non-sharply separated parts.

### Orbital portion

- overlies the extraorbital bones;
- contributes to forceful closure.

### Palpebral portion

- lies within the eyelid;
- produces voluntary gentle closure and involuntary blinking.

### Lacrimal portion

- extends behind the lacrimal sac;
- may help compress or move the lacrimal sac during blinking;
- contributes to the lacrimal pump.

### Functional progression

```text
palpebral part
→ gentle blink and routine closure

orbital part
→ stronger protective closure

lacrimal part
→ assists medial tear drainage
```

## 2.4 Levator palpebrae superioris

The **levator palpebrae superioris** is the principal upper-lid elevator.

The source states that it is supplied by the **superior division of cranial
nerve III**, the oculomotor nerve.

Its broad tendon, the **levator aponeurosis**, fans into the upper lid and
attaches to the distal tarsal region and related tissues.

### Clinical reasoning

```text
CN III/levator dysfunction
→ loss of the principal elevator
→ marked upper-lid ptosis
```

The exact appearance depends on the extent of the lesion and accompanying
ocular motor signs.

## 2.5 Müller muscle

The **superior tarsal muscle**, commonly called **Müller muscle**, is smooth
muscle associated with the upper lid.

It:

- enhances lid elevation;
- receives sympathetic fibres from the superior cervical ganglion;
- may widen the palpebral aperture during sympathetic activation.

### Horner pattern

Sympathetic interruption weakens Müller muscle.

```text
loss of small sympathetic lift
→ incomplete/mild ptosis
```

This is usually less severe than ptosis from loss of the main levator.

### Compare the elevators

| Feature | Levator palpebrae superioris | Müller muscle |
|---|---|---|
| Tissue | striated skeletal muscle | smooth muscle |
| Main role | principal lid elevation | supplementary elevation |
| Innervation in source | superior division of CN III | sympathetic fibres |
| Failure pattern | marked ptosis | mild/incomplete ptosis |

## 2.6 Orbital septum

The **orbital septum** is a membranous sheet across the upper and lower lid
regions.

The source describes it as:

- continuous with the periorbita at the orbital margin;
- perforated by the levator aponeurosis;
- connected to the tarsal region.

Functionally, it separates more superficial lid tissues from deeper orbital
contents and forms part of the supporting framework of the lids.

## Worked example: two forms of ptosis

### Patient A

- marked upper-lid droop;
- principal elevation greatly reduced.

This pattern suggests dysfunction involving levator palpebrae superioris or its
motor supply.

### Patient B

- slight upper-lid droop;
- main elevation preserved;
- sympathetic signs elsewhere.

This pattern fits loss of Müller-muscle tone.

The distinction follows muscle contribution, not merely the presence or absence
of ptosis.

## Common traps

### “Orbicularis oculi opens the eye.”

Orbicularis closes the lid. Levator and Müller muscle elevate the upper lid.

### “All three orbicularis portions are separate muscles.”

They are functional regions of one muscle with no sharp boundary in the source.

### “Horner syndrome causes the same degree of ptosis as complete levator
failure.”

Müller muscle provides a smaller supplementary lift, so sympathetic loss
typically causes milder ptosis.

### “The orbital septum is the same as the tarsal plate.”

They are related supporting structures but are not identical. The septum spans
the lid/orbital opening; the tarsal plate provides firm lid shape.

## Key terms

- **Orbicularis oculi** — striated muscle that closes the lids.
- **Orbital portion** — outer orbicularis portion used in forceful closure.
- **Palpebral portion** — lid portion used in blinking and gentle closure.
- **Lacrimal portion** — medial orbicularis portion associated with the lacrimal
  sac and pump.
- **Levator palpebrae superioris** — principal upper-lid elevator.
- **Levator aponeurosis** — fan-like terminal tendon of the levator.
- **Müller muscle** — sympathetically supplied smooth muscle supplementing lid
  elevation.
- **Ptosis** — abnormally low position of the upper eyelid.
- **Orbital septum** — fibrous sheet separating superficial lid from deeper
  orbital contents.

## Memory hook

**O-L-M**

```text
Orbicularis closes.
Levator lifts.
Müller makes the final small lift.
```

## One-minute summary

- Thin, loosely attached lid skin permits rapid movement.
- Lashes are sensory protective structures arranged in several rows.
- Orbicularis oculi closes the lid; its palpebral part blinks, orbital part
  closes forcefully and lacrimal part assists drainage.
- Levator palpebrae superioris is the principal elevator and is supplied by
  superior-division CN III.
- Müller muscle is smooth, sympathetic and provides supplementary elevation.
- Levator failure produces greater ptosis than loss of Müller-muscle tone.

## Active recall checkpoint

1. Why is eyelid skin structurally suited to blinking?
2. Name the three portions of orbicularis and their main roles.
3. Which muscle is the principal upper-lid elevator?
4. Compare levator and Müller muscle by tissue, innervation and effect of
   failure.
5. What is the levator aponeurosis?
6. How can lid-muscle anatomy help distinguish CN III dysfunction from
   sympathetic interruption?

<details>
<summary>Reveal answers</summary>

1. It is very thin, elastic and loosely attached, so it folds with little
   resistance.
2. Orbital—forceful closure; palpebral—blink/gentle closure; lacrimal—assists
   the tear pump.
3. Levator palpebrae superioris.
4. Levator is striated, supplied by CN III and its loss causes marked ptosis;
   Müller is smooth, sympathetic and its loss causes milder ptosis.
5. The fan-like terminal tendon of levator palpebrae superioris.
6. Severe loss of main elevation points toward levator/CN III dysfunction;
   mild ptosis with sympathetic signs points toward Müller-muscle denervation.

</details>

# 3. Tarsus, Conjunctiva and Eyelid Glands

## Big picture

The posterior part of the eyelid combines a firm supporting plate, a smooth
mucosal lining and multiple glands.

- the **tarsal plate** preserves lid shape;
- the **conjunctiva** creates a continuous mobile mucosal surface;
- glands produce lipid, aqueous and mucin components of tears.

The lid is therefore both a mechanical structure and a secretory organ.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- tarsal plate function and attachments;
- meibomian-gland location and secretion;
- palpebral and bulbar conjunctiva;
- superior and inferior fornices;
- conjunctival continuity at the limbus;
- goblet cells and mucin;
- Zeiss, Moll, Krause and Wolfring glands;
- how gland dysfunction changes the tear film.

### Should know

- approximate upper- and lower-lid meibomian-gland numbers;
- Palisades of Vogt;
- gland relationships to lashes, tarsus, fornix and orbital tarsal border.

### Useful extension

- why gland obstruction can cause a mass or evaporative symptoms;
- how conjunctival folds allow globe movement without tearing the lining.

## 3.1 Tarsal plate

The source describes the tarsal plate as a firm thickened supporting structure
related to the orbital septum.

Functions:

- provides rigidity and contour;
- supports the lid margin;
- houses meibomian glands;
- anchors to medial and lateral palpebral ligaments.

The tarsal plate is not bone or cartilage. It is dense supporting tissue.

## 3.2 Meibomian glands

**Meibomian/tarsal glands** are modified sebaceous glands embedded vertically in
the tarsal plate. Their ducts open in a row along the posterior lid margin.

The source gives approximate numbers:

- upper lid: about 30;
- lower lid: about 20.

They produce the main lipid component of the tear film.

### Why lipid matters

```text
meibomian lipid spreads over aqueous tears
→ reduces evaporation
→ improves tear stability
→ reduces overflow at lid margin
```

Obstruction or poor-quality secretion destabilises the tear film and can
increase evaporative dry-eye symptoms.

### Common trap

**Wrong idea:** Meibomian glands produce the thick aqueous layer.

**Correction:** They primarily produce lipid. The lacrimal and accessory
lacrimal glands produce aqueous tears.

## 3.3 Conjunctiva

The **conjunctiva** is a mucous membrane covering:

- the posterior surface of the lids;
- the anterior part of the globe, excluding the cornea.

### Palpebral conjunctiva

Lines the inner surface of the eyelids and is closely related to the tarsal
plate.

### Bulbar conjunctiva

Covers the anterior scleral surface.

### Fornices

The palpebral and bulbar parts reflect into one another at the:

- superior fornix;
- inferior fornix.

The fornices create slack that allows the globe and lid to move without tearing
the membrane.

### Limbus

At the corneoscleral junction, conjunctival epithelium becomes continuous with
corneal epithelium.

The source identifies the **Palisades of Vogt** as radial ridges at the limbus,
approximately 1.5–2 mm long.

## 3.4 Conjunctival goblet cells

Goblet cells are unicellular glands in conjunctival epithelium.

They secrete mucin, which:

- helps aqueous tears spread over the epithelial surface;
- improves wetting;
- contributes to tear-film stability;
- traps particles and supports surface defence.

Loss or dysfunction of goblet cells can produce a poorly wetting, unstable
surface even when aqueous production is adequate.

## 3.5 Glands of Zeiss

The **glands of Zeiss** are sebaceous glands associated with eyelash follicles.
They release sebum into the follicular region.

Their position distinguishes them from meibomian glands, which lie within the
tarsal plate and open directly along the lid margin.

## 3.6 Glands of Moll

The **glands of Moll** are modified sweat glands near the lid margin and lashes.

They are not the principal source of tear-film aqueous or lipid, but they form
part of the specialised glandular anatomy of the lid margin.

## 3.7 Accessory lacrimal glands

### Glands of Krause

Located in the conjunctival fornix and contribute aqueous secretion.

### Glands of Wolfring

Located near the orbital border of the tarsal plate and also contribute to the
aqueous tear component.

These accessory glands support basal tear production alongside the main
lacrimal gland.

## Gland comparison

| Gland/cell | Location | Main product/role |
|---|---|---|
| Meibomian | within tarsal plate | lipid |
| Zeiss | associated with lash follicle | sebum |
| Moll | near lid margin | modified sweat secretion |
| Krause | conjunctival fornix | accessory aqueous |
| Wolfring | near orbital tarsal border | accessory aqueous |
| Goblet cell | conjunctival epithelium | mucin |

## 3.8 Integrating the layers

From anterior to posterior, the upper lid can be studied as:

```text
thin skin and lashes
→ orbicularis muscle
→ orbital septum/levator structures
→ tarsal plate with meibomian glands
→ palpebral conjunctiva with goblet cells
```

This sequence explains how the lid combines movement, rigidity and a smooth
wet posterior surface.

## Worked example: unstable tear film with normal Schirmer result

A patient has burning and fluctuating vision. A Schirmer test suggests adequate
aqueous production, but the meibomian gland openings are obstructed.

Reasoning:

```text
aqueous quantity may be adequate
+ lipid layer is deficient
→ evaporation rises
→ tear film breaks up quickly
→ symptoms and fluctuating vision
```

This illustrates why “dry eye” does not always mean insufficient lacrimal-gland
aqueous secretion.

## Common traps

### “All eyelid sebaceous glands are meibomian glands.”

Zeiss glands are also sebaceous but are associated with lash follicles.
Meibomian glands are embedded in the tarsal plate.

### “The conjunctiva ends at the fornix.”

The fornix is where palpebral conjunctiva reflects onto the globe as bulbar
conjunctiva.

### “Goblet cells produce aqueous tears.”

Goblet cells produce mucin.

### “The tarsal plate is a gland.”

It is a supporting plate that contains many meibomian glands.

## Key terms

- **Tarsal plate** — firm supporting tissue that shapes the lid.
- **Meibomian gland** — modified sebaceous gland in the tarsal plate producing
  tear-film lipid.
- **Palpebral conjunctiva** — conjunctiva lining the posterior eyelid.
- **Bulbar conjunctiva** — conjunctiva covering the anterior sclera.
- **Fornix** — reflection between palpebral and bulbar conjunctiva.
- **Limbus** — corneoscleral junction.
- **Palisades of Vogt** — radial limbal ridges.
- **Goblet cell** — unicellular mucin-secreting conjunctival gland.
- **Gland of Zeiss** — sebaceous gland associated with an eyelash follicle.
- **Gland of Moll** — modified sweat gland near the lid margin.
- **Gland of Krause** — accessory lacrimal gland in the fornix.
- **Gland of Wolfring** — accessory lacrimal gland near the upper tarsal border.

## Memory hook

**M-Z-M-K-W-G**

```text
Meibomian = main lipid
Zeiss = sebum beside lash
Moll = modified sweat
Krause = aqueous in the crease/fornix
Wolfring = aqueous near tarsus
Goblet = mucin
```

## One-minute summary

- The tarsal plate gives the lid rigidity and contains meibomian glands.
- Meibomian glands produce lipid and reduce tear evaporation.
- Palpebral conjunctiva lines the lid, bulbar conjunctiva covers sclera and the
  fornices connect them.
- Goblet cells produce mucin.
- Zeiss glands are sebaceous glands of lash follicles; Moll glands are modified
  sweat glands.
- Krause and Wolfring are accessory aqueous glands.
- The lid’s layered organisation combines movement, structure and a wet
  posterior surface.

5. Compare Zeiss, Moll, Krause and Wolfring glands.

2. They lie in the tarsal plate and produce lipid.

   conjunctiva, then becomes continuous with corneal epithelium at the limbus.

# 4. Lower Lid and Blood Supply

## Big picture

The lower lid follows the general layered plan of the upper lid but lacks an
equivalent of the levator palpebrae superioris. It is therefore less mobile and
more dependent on tissue tone, orbicularis action and lid–globe apposition.

Its vascular supply is rich and interconnected, supporting frequent movement
and repair but also allowing inflammation to become visibly red and swollen.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- lower lid as a smaller inverted version of the upper lid;
- absence of a levator equivalent;
- reduced mobility;
- age-related laxity and sagging;
- ophthalmic-artery contribution to eyelid supply;
- palpebral arterial arcades and posterior palpebral arteries;
- ophthalmic venous drainage.

### Should know

- anterior ciliary anastomoses near the limbus;
- how lid laxity interferes with punctal position and drainage;
- why vascular anatomy explains rapid swelling.

### Useful extension

- the relationship among lower-lid position, exposure and epiphora;
- how venous connections contribute to spread of infection.

## 4.1 Lower-lid structure

The source calls the lower lid a smaller, inverted version of the upper lid.

It contains:

- thin skin;
- orbicularis;
- a supporting tarsal structure;
- meibomian glands;
- palpebral conjunctiva.

It lacks a direct lower-lid equivalent of levator palpebrae superioris.

Consequences:

- less vertical mobility;
- greater dependence on passive tissue support;
- increasing tendency to sag with age.

## 4.2 Age-related change

With age, supporting tissues may become lax.

Possible functional effects include:

- loss of close apposition to the globe;
- punctal eversion or displacement;
- poor tear collection;
- increased exposure;
- watering despite an otherwise open drainage system.

### Cause–effect chain

```text
lower-lid laxity
→ lid margin/punctum moves away from globe
→ tears are not collected efficiently
→ overflow onto cheek
```

## 4.3 Arterial supply

The source states that eyelid arteries are principally branches of the
**ophthalmic artery**.

Arterial branches form **palpebral arcades**, interconnected networks within the
lids.

**Posterior palpebral arteries** pass backward across the fornix and over bulbar
conjunctiva toward the limbus, where they anastomose with anterior ciliary
arteries.

The anastomotic pattern provides collateral supply to active, mobile tissues.

## 4.4 Venous drainage

Venous blood from tarsal and conjunctival regions drains through divisions of
the **ophthalmic veins**.

The source also describes drainage connections toward the angular and
superficial temporal venous systems.

Because facial and orbital venous pathways communicate, infection around the
lids should not be dismissed as purely superficial when deeper signs are
present.

## Worked example: tearing in an older adult

An older patient reports tears rolling down the cheek. Tear production is not
markedly increased, but the lower lid sags outward.

Reasoning:

```text
lower-lid laxity
→ punctum no longer sits in tear lake
→ canalicular entry falls
→ tears overflow
```

The symptom is a drainage-position problem rather than necessarily excessive
secretion.

## Common traps

### “The lower lid has a small levator.”

The source explicitly states that there is no equivalent of the upper-lid
levator.

### “Watering always means the lacrimal gland is overactive.”

Watering can result from poor lid apposition, punctal displacement or drainage
failure.

### “Conjunctival vessels are isolated from the rest of the eyelid circulation.”

Posterior palpebral and anterior ciliary vessels form anastomotic connections.

## Key terms

- **Lower lid** — less mobile inferior lid lacking a levator equivalent.
- **Lid laxity** — reduced structural tone allowing malposition.
- **Palpebral arcade** — interconnected arterial network in an eyelid.
- **Posterior palpebral artery** — vessel supplying conjunctival regions.
- **Anterior ciliary artery** — anterior ocular vessel anastomosing near the
  limbus.
- **Ophthalmic vein** — major venous drainage pathway of orbital and eyelid
  regions.
- **Epiphora** — overflow of tears onto the cheek.

## Memory hook

**Lower lid: less lift, more laxity with age.**

## One-minute summary

- The lower lid resembles a smaller inverted upper lid.
- It lacks levator palpebrae superioris and is less mobile.
- Age-related laxity can disturb apposition and punctal drainage.
- Eyelid arteries arise mainly from ophthalmic-artery branches and form
  palpebral arcades.
- Posterior palpebral vessels supply conjunctiva and connect near the limbus.
- Venous drainage reaches ophthalmic and related facial venous pathways.

# 5. Lacrimal Gland

## Big picture

The main lacrimal gland is the major source of reflex aqueous tears. It lies in
the superolateral orbit, drains into the superior fornix and receives a
parasympathetic secretomotor pathway that travels through branches of both the
facial and trigeminal nerves.

The gland is easiest to understand by linking:

```text
location
→ two anatomical portions
→ acini and ducts
→ superior-fornix openings
→ blink spreads secretion across the eye
```

## What to focus on

### Must know

- superolateral orbital location in the frontal bone;
- orbital and palpebral portions;
- acini and duct sequence;
- approximately 10–12 excretory ducts opening into the superior fornix;
- lacrimal artery and vein;
- trigeminal sensory afferent limb of reflex tearing;
- facial parasympathetic secretomotor efferent pathway;
- sympathetic vasomotor route;
- why interruption at different points changes tearing.

### Should know

- relationship between levator aponeurosis and the two gland portions;
- reflex versus emotional/central tearing;
- why the palpebral portion is clinically accessible through the fornix.

### Useful extension

- how superolateral gland enlargement can influence globe position;
- how duct injury can impair secretion even when glandular acini remain.

## 5.1 Location and shape

The lacrimal gland is:

- almond-shaped;
- situated in the superolateral orbit;
- lodged in a depression of the frontal bone.

Its position explains why enlargement in the superolateral orbit may displace
the globe inferomedially.

## 5.2 Orbital and palpebral portions

The gland has:

- a larger **orbital portion** superiorly;
- a smaller **palpebral portion** inferiorly.

The levator aponeurosis separates the portions anatomically, but their duct
systems remain connected.

The palpebral portion is closer to the superior conjunctival fornix.

## 5.3 Secretory architecture

The gland consists of many small secretory **acini/lobules**.

The drainage sequence is:

```text
acini
→ small connecting ducts
→ larger collecting ducts
→ about 10–12 excretory ducts
→ superior conjunctival fornix
```

Tears then spread across the ocular surface during blinking.

### Common trap

**Wrong idea:** The lacrimal gland empties directly into the lacrimal sac.

**Correction:** It secretes into the superior conjunctival fornix. The lacrimal
sac belongs to the medial drainage pathway.

## 5.4 Blood supply

The source states:

- arterial supply: **lacrimal artery**;
- venous drainage: **lacrimal vein**, joining the superior ophthalmic vein.

## 5.5 Reflex tearing

A reflex requires an afferent sensory limb, a central connection and an
efferent secretomotor limb.

### Afferent limb

Ocular-surface stimulation is carried by trigeminal sensory fibres, especially
through the ophthalmic division and lacrimal-related sensory pathways.

Examples of triggers:

- corneal or conjunctival irritation;
- foreign material;
- strong sensory stimulation.

### Central processing

Sensory input reaches brainstem centres, which activate the facial
parasympathetic secretomotor pathway.

### Efferent parasympathetic limb

The standard pathway used in the curated module is:

```text
facial nerve secretomotor fibres
→ greater petrosal nerve
→ nerve of the pterygoid canal
→ pterygopalatine ganglion (synapse)
→ zygomatic nerve, V2
→ communicating branch
→ lacrimal nerve, V1
→ lacrimal gland
```

### Course correction

The source deck’s innervation slides compress and partly mislabel the route. In
particular, the **greater petrosal nerve carries parasympathetic fibres**, not
the postganglionic sympathetic supply.

The durable principle is:

- trigeminal branches provide the final anatomical carrier to the gland;
- the secretomotor parasympathetic fibres originate with CN VII;
- the pterygopalatine ganglion is the parasympathetic relay.

## 5.6 Sympathetic pathway

The sympathetic route begins with postganglionic fibres from the superior
cervical ganglion.

A useful pathway is:

```text
superior cervical ganglion
→ internal carotid plexus
→ deep petrosal nerve
→ nerve of the pterygoid canal
→ through pterygopalatine ganglion without synapsing
→ zygomatic/lacrimal branches
→ gland
```

Its role is mainly vasomotor and modulatory rather than the dominant ordinary
secretomotor drive.

### Course correction

Do not memorise the source statement that sympathetic fibres reach the gland by
the greater petrosal nerve. **Deep petrosal** is the sympathetic component;
**greater petrosal** is parasympathetic.

## 5.7 Reflex, basal and emotional tearing

### Basal tears

Maintain the ocular surface continuously and are supported by the main and
accessory lacrimal glands plus other tear-film sources.

### Reflex tears

Large rapid secretion after ocular-surface or sensory stimulation.

### Emotional tears

Central emotional pathways can activate the same secretory apparatus.

The output differs in trigger, but the glandular secretion still reaches the
superior fornix and is distributed by blinking.

## Worked example: localising reduced reflex tearing

A patient has intact ocular-surface sensation but markedly reduced reflex
tearing.

Possible reasoning:

```text
afferent sensation intact
→ sensory limb probably functioning

reduced secretion
→ consider facial secretomotor pathway,
   pterygopalatine relay,
   communicating trigeminal route,
   gland or ducts
```

If corneal sensation is also absent, the afferent trigeminal limb becomes a more
likely contributor.

## Common traps

### “The lacrimal nerve is the origin of secretomotor fibres.”

It is a trigeminal carrier near the gland. The parasympathetic secretomotor
fibres originate with the facial nerve.

### “Parasympathetic fibres synapse in the superior cervical ganglion.”

They synapse in the pterygopalatine ganglion. The superior cervical ganglion is
sympathetic.

### “The greater and deep petrosal nerves carry the same autonomic division.”

Greater petrosal carries parasympathetic fibres; deep petrosal carries
sympathetic fibres.

### “The orbital and palpebral portions drain separately into the nose.”

Both contribute to ducts opening into the superior conjunctival fornix.

## Key terms

- **Lacrimal gland** — major aqueous tear gland in the superolateral orbit.
- **Orbital portion** — larger superior part of the gland.
- **Palpebral portion** — smaller inferior part near the fornix.
- **Acinus** — secretory end unit/lobule.
- **Excretory duct** — duct opening gland secretion into the superior fornix.
- **Lacrimal artery** — arterial supply to the gland.
- **Reflex tearing** — increased secretion caused by sensory stimulation.
- **Secretomotor** — autonomic drive that stimulates glandular secretion.
- **Greater petrosal nerve** — facial parasympathetic pathway component.
- **Deep petrosal nerve** — sympathetic pathway component.
- **Pterygopalatine ganglion** — parasympathetic relay for lacrimal secretion.
- **Lacrimal nerve** — V1 sensory branch and final carrier of autonomic fibres
  toward the gland.

## Memory hook

Parasympathetic route:

**VII → Greater → Ganglion → Zygomatic → Lacrimal.**

Sympathetic contrast:

**Superior cervical → Deep petrosal → passes through ganglion.**

## One-minute summary

- The lacrimal gland lies superolaterally in the frontal bone.
- It has orbital and palpebral portions.
- Acini drain through small and large ducts into about 10–12 ducts opening in
  the superior fornix.
- The lacrimal artery supplies it; the lacrimal vein drains to the superior
  ophthalmic vein.
- Trigeminal fibres carry sensory input.
- Facial parasympathetic fibres travel by greater petrosal nerve, relay in the
  pterygopalatine ganglion and reach the gland through zygomatic–lacrimal
  communications.
- Sympathetic fibres arise from the superior cervical ganglion and use the deep
  petrosal route.

7. Why can gland enlargement displace the globe inferomedially?

   conjunctival fornix.

# 6. Tear Film, Production and Drainage

## Big picture

The tear film is the functional interface between the atmosphere and the ocular
surface. It smooths the optical surface, lubricates blinking, supplies and
protects epithelium, and carries debris toward drainage.

The classical course model has three layers:

```text
outer lipid
middle aqueous
inner mucin
```

Each layer has a different source and role. Tear symptoms can therefore arise
from inadequate aqueous production, poor lipid, abnormal mucin, excessive
evaporation, poor spreading or drainage failure.

## What to focus on

### Must know

- classical lipid, aqueous and mucin layers;
- source and function of each component;
- tear meniscus;
- basal, reflex and emotional tearing;
- parasympathetic dominance of normal lacrimal secretion;
- Schirmer test and tear-film break-up time;
- evaporation versus drainage;
- punctum → canaliculus → lacrimal sac → nasolacrimal duct → inferior meatus;
- gravity, capillarity, lid movement and lacrimal pump;
- why tearing may reflect drainage failure rather than excess production.

### Should know

- approximate 25% evaporative loss given in the source;
- blink rate, climate and atmosphere as evaporation variables;
- role of lacrimal orbicularis around the sac;
- tear meniscus as the reservoir sampled by the puncta.

### Useful extension

- the modern concept of an aqueous–mucin gradient rather than perfectly
  separated inner layers;
- how different tear tests evaluate different parts of the system.

## 6.1 Functions of tears

The tear film:

- creates a smooth refracting surface;
- lubricates eyelid movement;
- wets corneal and conjunctival epithelium;
- carries oxygen, nutrients and defence molecules;
- washes away particles;
- supports antimicrobial defence;
- transports tears toward the medial drainage apparatus.

## 6.2 Classical three-layer model

### Lipid layer

- outermost layer;
- produced mainly by meibomian glands;
- reduces evaporation;
- stabilises the air–tear interface;
- reduces overflow at the lid margin.

### Aqueous layer

- thickest classical layer;
- produced mainly by the lacrimal gland and accessory glands of Krause and
  Wolfring;
- carries water, electrolytes, proteins and soluble defence factors;
- nourishes and cleans the surface.

### Mucin layer

- lies closest to the epithelium in the classical model;
- produced mainly by conjunctival goblet cells;
- improves wetting of the corneal surface;
- helps spread aqueous tears evenly.

### Course qualification

The three-layer model is the structure used in the supplied deck and is useful
for learning sources and functions. In living tears, the aqueous and mucin
components form a more integrated gradient than three sharply separated sheets.
Do not let the diagram imply rigid boundaries.

## Tear-component comparison

| Component | Main source | Main role | Dysfunction pattern |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lipid | meibomian glands | reduces evaporation and stabilises surface | rapid evaporation, unstable tear film |
| Aqueous | main/accessory lacrimal glands | volume, soluble nutrients and defence | low tear volume, surface dryness |
| Mucin | conjunctival goblet cells | wetting and spreading | poor surface coating and breakup |

## 6.3 Tear meniscus

A **tear meniscus/prism** is visible along the lid margin, especially the lower
lid.

It acts as a small reservoir. The puncta contact the meniscus medially and draw
tears into the canaliculi.

A reduced meniscus may suggest low volume; a high meniscus may reflect increased
production or poor drainage.

## 6.4 Tear production control

The source distinguishes rapid reflex tearing after:

- peripheral sensory stimulation;
- central stimulation such as bright light or emotional crying.

### Course correction: autonomic dominance

The source states that normal tear production is primarily sympathetic. The
current curated module uses the standard physiology:

> **Parasympathetic secretomotor activity is the dominant driver of lacrimal
> secretion.**

Sympathetic fibres influence vascular tone and may modulate secretion, but they
are not the main ordinary secretomotor pathway.

## 6.5 Basal, reflex and emotional tears

### Basal secretion

Maintains the surface continuously through contributions from main and
accessory glands plus the lipid and mucin systems.

### Reflex secretion

Triggered by irritation, foreign material or strong sensory input. It can
produce a large volume quickly.

### Emotional secretion

Generated through central emotional pathways.

All three must be spread by blinking and removed by evaporation or drainage.

## 6.6 Tests

### Schirmer test

The source describes the Schirmer test as a crude measure of aqueous tear
production.

Interpretation depends on technique and whether anaesthetic is used. The
high-yield point is that it chiefly assesses aqueous output, not lipid quality.

### Tear-film break-up time

**Tear-film break-up time (TBUT)** assesses tear-film stability after a blink.

A short break-up time can reflect:

- poor mucin-associated wetting;
- lipid deficiency and evaporation;
- broader surface instability.

### Common trap

**Wrong idea:** A normal Schirmer result proves the tear film is normal.

**Correction:** Lipid, mucin, spreading and evaporation can be abnormal despite
adequate aqueous production.

## 6.7 Evaporation

The source estimates that about 25% of tears may be lost through evaporation,
but the fraction varies with:

- blink rate;
- humidity;
- wind and atmosphere;
- climate;
- exposed ocular surface;
- individual tear-film properties.

Reduced blinking during concentrated display use can increase evaporation.

## 6.8 Drainage pathway

The anatomical sequence is:

```text
tear meniscus
→ upper and lower puncta
→ canaliculi
→ lacrimal sac
→ nasolacrimal duct
→ inferior nasal meatus
```

After entering the inferior nasal meatus, fluid spreads within the nose and
evaporates or moves posteriorly.

### Why crying causes a runny nose

A large tear volume enters the nasolacrimal duct and reaches the inferior nasal
meatus.

## 6.9 Forces supporting drainage

The source lists:

- gravity;
- lid movement;
- capillary attraction within canaliculi;
- compression/movement of the lacrimal sac by the lacrimal portion of
  orbicularis during lid closure.

These are often grouped as the **lacrimal pump**.

### Blink cycle model

```text
blink closes lids
→ tears are swept medially
→ puncta and canaliculi engage tear lake
→ orbicularis-related movement changes sac/canalicular pressure
→ tears advance into sac and nasolacrimal duct
```

The exact pressure changes are more complex than a simple squeeze, but the
course principle is that blinking is an active part of drainage.

## 6.10 Why the eye waters

**Epiphora** can arise from several mechanisms.

### Excess production

- irritation;
- surface inflammation;
- emotional crying;
- bright-light or sensory reflexes.

### Poor drainage

- punctal stenosis;
- punctal malposition;
- canalicular obstruction;
- lacrimal-sac or nasolacrimal-duct obstruction;
- weak or ineffective blink pump;
- lower-lid laxity.

### Surface instability

A dry or unstable surface can stimulate reflex tearing, so a patient may report
both “dryness” and watering.

## Worked examples

### Case 1: evaporative pattern

- normal Schirmer result;
- obstructed meibomian openings;
- rapid TBUT;
- fluctuating vision.

Mechanism:

```text
lipid deficiency
→ evaporation increases
→ tear film breaks early
→ surface irritation and reflex tearing
```

### Case 2: drainage pattern

- tears spill over the cheek;
- high tear meniscus;
- lower punctum is everted.

Mechanism:

```text
punctum not apposed to tear lake
→ reduced drainage entry
→ tear retention
→ epiphora
```

### Case 3: aqueous-deficient pattern

- low tear meniscus;
- reduced Schirmer wetting;
- surface staining and discomfort.

Mechanism:

```text
low aqueous secretion
→ inadequate tear volume
→ poor lubrication and epithelial stress
```

## Common traps

### “Watery eye means too many tears.”

It may reflect drainage failure or reflex tearing caused by an unstable dry
surface.

### “The lacrimal gland supplies every tear-film component.”

It mainly supplies aqueous. Meibomian glands supply lipid and goblet cells
supply mucin.

### “All tears enter the drainage system.”

A variable fraction evaporates.

### “The nasolacrimal duct opens into the middle meatus.”

It opens into the **inferior nasal meatus**.

### “Sympathetic activity is the main driver of normal secretion.”

Parasympathetic secretomotor activity is dominant.

## Key terms

- **Tear film** — thin fluid layer covering cornea and conjunctiva.
- **Lipid layer** — outer meibomian-derived component reducing evaporation.
- **Aqueous layer** — water-rich lacrimal component.
- **Mucin** — glycoprotein-rich component supporting wetting.
- **Tear meniscus** — tear reservoir along the lid margin.
- **Basal tearing** — continuous maintenance secretion.
- **Reflex tearing** — increased secretion after sensory stimulation.
- **Schirmer test** — paper-strip test estimating aqueous production.
- **TBUT** — time from blink to first tear-film breakup.
- **Punctum** — opening into a canaliculus.
- **Canaliculus** — small channel carrying tears to the lacrimal sac.
- **Lacrimal sac** — medial reservoir leading to the nasolacrimal duct.
- **Nasolacrimal duct** — duct carrying tears to the inferior nasal meatus.
- **Lacrimal pump** — blink-related mechanism aiding tear drainage.
- **Epiphora** — overflow of tears onto the face.

## Memory hook

For the tear film:

**M-A-M**

```text
Meibomian makes lipid.
Accessory/main lacrimal makes aqueous.
Mucin comes from goblet cells.
```

For drainage:

**P-C-S-D-M**

```text
Punctum
Canaliculus
Sac
Duct
Meatus
```

## One-minute summary

- The tear film creates a smooth, lubricated and protected optical surface.
- The classical model is lipid outside, aqueous in the middle and mucin at the
  epithelial surface.
- Meibomian glands provide lipid, lacrimal glands provide aqueous and goblet
  cells provide mucin.
- Parasympathetic secretomotor activity drives lacrimal secretion.
- Schirmer chiefly assesses aqueous output; TBUT assesses stability.
- Tears are lost by evaporation and by drainage.
- Drainage runs from puncta through canaliculi, sac and nasolacrimal duct to the
  inferior meatus.
- Blinking spreads tears and assists the lacrimal pump.
- Watering can result from overproduction, surface irritation or drainage
  failure.
