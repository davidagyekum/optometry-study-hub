# HVP Notes V3 — Depth Perception

Source: `4. Depth Perception.pptx`, 77-slide lecture deck.

<a id="depth-foundations"></a>

## Big picture

Depth is the perception of **distance in space**. It answers questions such as: how close or far is an object from the observer, how close is one object from another, or how close one part of an object is from another part of the same object.

**Absolute depth** is the distance from the observer to an object. It asks: how far is the object from me? **Relative depth** is the distance between two objects, or between different parts of one object. It asks: which object or part is nearer? The lecture stresses an exam-important contrast: although absolute depth is vital for daily living, relative-depth judgement is often more precise.

Depth perception matters because it guides everyday behaviour. While driving, it helps a person judge the distance of an approaching vehicle. While walking, climbing or descending stairs, it helps estimate how high to move the legs. In animals, accurate depth judgement supports hunting and survival.

### Key distinction
- **Absolute depth:** observer-to-object distance.
- **Relative depth:** object-to-object or part-to-part distance.
- **Main trap:** do not assume the more important daily skill is always the more precise one. The lecture states that absolute depth is very important, but relative-depth judgement is more precise.

<a id="depth-overview"></a>

## Big picture

Depth can be judged with one eye or with both eyes. **Monocular depth perception** is depth judgement that can occur using only one eye. Either absolute or relative depth can be judged monocularly. **Binocular depth perception** requires the two eyes working together.

**Stereopsis** is the ability to judge depth because the brain combines inputs from the two eyes. The lecture also calls it 3-D vision and presents it as the highest benefit of binocular vision. However, depth perception is not solely binocular. A one-eyed observer can still use many monocular cues, especially at longer distances.

### Distance rule from the lecture
Binocular cues are most useful at close and intermediate viewing distances, especially in **peripersonal space**. They support fine, quantitative judgements and can detect very small depth differences. Monocular cues work at all distances and become the main cues in **extrapersonal space**, especially when objects are far away.

<a id="monocular-cues"></a>

## Big picture

The lecture lists several monocular cues: relative size, linear perspective, texture gradient, interposition or occlusion, aerial perspective or clarity, light and shadow, motion parallax, and height in the horizon.

**Relative size** means that larger retinal images are often perceived as closer, especially when two objects are known or assumed to be the same size. If beads are truly the same size, then a bead that forms an image twice as small is interpreted as being twice as far away.

**Texture gradient** means that detailed, coarse texture tends to appear closer, while smoother texture tends to appear farther away. Distant surfaces lose visible detail.

**Interposition, overlap or occlusion** occurs when one object blocks part of another. The object doing the blocking is perceived as closer.

**Linear perspective** occurs because parallel lines appear to converge as they extend into depth. The converging part of a railway track, for example, is perceived as farther away.

**Light and shadow** provide depth because the visual system assumes that light comes from above. Brightly lit objects may appear closer, shaded objects may appear farther, and cast shadows can make one surface appear raised above another.

**Aerial perspective or clarity** means that clearer and sharper objects are perceived as closer, while distant objects become blurred or bluish because of dust, smog, clouds or water vapour.

**Motion parallax** is a kinetic cue. During movement, nearby objects appear to move faster across the visual field and often appear to move against the direction of the observer’s motion. Far objects move more slowly.

**Height in the horizon** means that objects nearer the bottom of the visual field can be judged as closer, while objects nearer the horizon are interpreted as farther away.

<a id="depth-illusions"></a>

## Big picture

Depth interpretation can produce illusions. The lecture highlights the Ebbinghaus illusion, Ponzo illusion and Moon illusion.

The **Ebbinghaus illusion** or **Titchener circles** is context based. A central circle surrounded by small circles appears larger, while an equal central circle surrounded by large circles appears smaller. The relative-size context changes perceived size.

The **Ponzo illusion** uses linear perspective. An upper line appears larger because the converging context makes it seem farther away, and the brain interprets a far object with a large retinal image as physically large.

The **Moon illusion** occurs when the moon appears larger near the horizon than high in the sky. The lecture notes that there is no universally accepted explanation, but it names relative-size and apparent-distance explanations as popular accounts.

<a id="binocular-cues"></a>

## Big picture

The lecture recognizes two major binocular cues: **convergence** and **retinal disparity**.

**Convergence** means that the closer an object is, the more the eyes must turn inward to fixate it singly. The magnitude of convergence is sensed and used as a depth cue. Convergence is also described as an oculomotor cue because it is related to extraocular muscle activity.

<a id="retinal-disparity-panum"></a>

## Big picture

**Retinal disparity** occurs because the two laterally separated eyes view the world from slightly different angles. The difference between relative image positions on the two retinas provides stereoscopic depth information.

When disparity becomes too large, it falls outside **Panum’s fusional area/space** and diplopia occurs. When disparity is small enough to remain within Panum’s space, the object is fused and perceived in depth. The lecture also describes physiologic diplopia as a binocular cue because seeing double can signal that two objects occupy different depth planes.

<a id="stereopsis"></a>

## Big picture

**Stereopsis** means solid vision. It is the sense of depth mediated by binocular vision. It is strongest for fine depth perception when other cues are weak or absent and is especially useful for objects within reach.

<a id="clinical-stereopsis-testing"></a>

## Big picture

The lecture identifies many clinical tests and stereotest families: Randot Stereofly, Randot, Random Dot E, Lang stereotest, Frisby stereotest, TNO stereotest, PASS/Stereosmile, telebinocular tests, Howard-Dolman peg test, Randot distance stereotest and vectographic projector slides.

Most clinical stereotests are near tests because stereopsis is most useful within arm’s length. Distance stereotests exist, but the lecture emphasizes that monocular cues become more useful at distance.

A stereogram is made from two copies of the same target separated by controlled distances. The patient fuses the pair using fusional vergence. If fusional convergence is used, part of the image may appear closer or raised above the page; if fusional divergence is used, part may appear farther away. The smallest separation that can be seen in depth is the stereoacuity threshold. Stereoacuity is reported in seconds of arc. The deck states that about 40 seconds of arc is considered average clinically normal stereoacuity and that many people may see lower, up to about 20 seconds.

<a id="practical-lab"></a>

## Big picture

The lab asks students to compare local and global stereopsis, identify suppression checks, and gain hands-on experience with Randot, PASS and telebinocular tests. It also asks students to attempt Randot tasks with one eye and through a +4.00 D lens in front of one eye. This is a teaching strategy: it reveals how some tests can be “cheated” using monocular cues and how degrading one eye can reduce binocular stereopsis.

The deck also introduces chromostereopsis, asking which colour appears closer and how colour may create a depth effect. It ends with reading tasks on Stereo Sue, 3-D movies, anaglyphic glasses, 3-D glasses and the Ames Room illusion.
