## How to use this module

Multifocal optics becomes easier when every lens is analysed as two components:

```text
distance prescription + supplementary near addition
```

At any point in the near portion:

```text
total near power = distance power + add power
total prism = prism from main lens + prism from segment
```

The optical performance then depends on:

- where the distance optical centre is located;
- where the segment optical centre is located;
- where the eye looks through the lens at near;
- the segment shape, size, height, and inset;
- the add power.

> **Source approach:** The three supplied bifocal/multifocal decks are treated as course evidence. Where they use slightly different terminology or numerical conventions, the note keeps the course language and makes the difference visible rather than silently choosing one version.

<a id="presbyopia-add"></a>
# 1. Presbyopia and Reading Addition

## 1.1 Accommodation and presbyopia

**Accommodation** is the eye's change in focus for nearer objects. The main OPT 370 deck describes accommodative ability as a “wasting asset”: it progressively decreases with age.

**Presbyopia** is the age-related state in which the available accommodation becomes insufficient for comfortable near work at the desired distance. It usually becomes noticeable in the middle forties, although onset and symptoms vary.

### Source-listed lenticular changes

- sclerosis of the lens nucleus;
- reduced flexibility of the lens and capsule;
- thickening and brittleness of the capsule;
- changes in the distance between the ciliary muscle and lens equator.

### Source-listed extra-lenticular changes

- age-related changes in the ciliary processes and ciliary muscle;
- reduced zonular elasticity;
- altered resistance around the accommodating lens.

### Premature presbyopia associations listed in the lecture

- diabetes and hypertension;
- medications and trauma;
- uncorrected hypermetropia;
- premature lens sclerosis;
- presenile ciliary-muscle weakness;
- chronic simple glaucoma.

These are lecture associations. They should not be converted into a diagnosis without clinical assessment.

## 1.2 Symptoms and signs

The deck lists:

- near difficulty first in dim light or in the evening, then later under better illumination;
- headache and ciliary fatigue;
- intermittent diplopia associated with convergence disturbance;
- worsening with fatigue, illness, fever, or chronic conditions;
- reduced amplitude of accommodation as the principal sign.

## 1.3 Reading addition

The **reading addition**, usually written **Add**, is the extra plus power placed over the distance correction for near vision.

\[
F_{near}=F_{distance}+Add
\]

Example:

```text
Distance Rx: +1.00 DS
Add: +2.00 D
Near portion power: +3.00 D
```

The main deck states that the add is nearly always the same in both eyes and that unequal additions should be queried unless explicitly prescribed. It also states that additions below +0.75 D or above +3.50 D are uncommon in routine prescribing.

## 1.4 Age-based lecture estimate

The deck gives a tentative formula for a 40 cm working distance:

\[
Add\,(D)=\frac{age}{8}-5.00\pm0.25
\]

At age 52:

\[
Add=\frac{52}{8}-5.00=+1.50\text{ D}
\]

This is a lecture estimate, not a substitute for measuring near requirements, working distance, residual accommodation, binocular status, and task demand.

<a id="construction-types"></a>
# 2. Bifocal Construction Types

## 2.1 Definition of a bifocal

A **bifocal lens** has two distinct areas of different focal power, usually:

- a larger **distance portion (DP)**;
- a smaller **near portion (NP)** or **reading portion (RP)**, normally in the lower part of the lens.

The source decks classify bifocals by construction as split, cemented, fused, and solid/one-piece designs.

## 2.2 Split or Franklin bifocal

A **split bifocal** is made from two separate lenses:

- the upper portion is cut from the distance lens;
- the lower portion is cut from the near lens;
- the pieces are fitted edge-to-edge in one frame.

It is historically associated with Benjamin Franklin. One source deck notes that Franklin described his satisfaction with “double spectacles” but did not necessarily claim sole invention.

### Optical strengths

Because the two parts are independent, the practitioner can control:

- distance and near prescriptions separately;
- optical centres independently;
- differential vertical prism at the near visual points;
- tint, photochromic state, and other characteristics independently.

### Limitations

- conspicuous dividing line;
- dirt-collecting ridge;
- poorer cosmetic acceptance;
- uncommon in modern routine dispensing.

The “perfection bifocal” is presented as an improved split form in which one component is bevelled into a groove in the other.

## 2.3 Cemented or wafer bifocal

A **cemented bifocal** has a separate near segment attached to the surface of a main lens.

The source describes:

- Canada balsam as a traditional adhesive;
- ultraviolet-cured epoxy as a later alternative for glass components;
- freedom to choose segment size, shape, position, and centration;
- a thin “wafer” form ground nearly to a knife edge.

### Strengths

- excellent optical freedom;
- the segment may be moved or replaced;
- useful for specialised prescriptions and custom prism control.

### Limitations

- visible, dirt-collecting segment edge;
- possible discoloration or deterioration of adhesive;
- largely obsolete for routine permanent wear.

The lecture describes a water- or alcohol-adhered Fresnel near segment as a modern temporary analogue, while also noting its cosmetic and optical limitations.

## 2.4 Fused bifocal

A **fused bifocal** is a glass lens in which a high-index segment button is fused into a depression in a lower-index crown-glass main lens.

### Construction sequence

1. A depression of predetermined curvature is ground and polished in the main blank.
2. A higher-index button is placed in the depression.
3. The components are fused at high temperature.
4. The segment side is finished so the surface is continuous.

The segment boundary can be seen but cannot normally be felt.

### Source features

- common segment diameters roughly 20-22 mm, with an upper practical limit around 25 mm in the source;
- suitable for round, straight-top, curved-top, D, and ribbon forms;
- the add depends on front-surface power, depression curve, and the refractive-index relationship of the glasses;
- transverse chromatic aberration can occur because the segment glass has greater dispersion.

## 2.5 Solid, one-piece, Executive or E-style bifocal

A **solid bifocal** is made from one piece of lens material. The add results from a difference in surface curvature between the distance and near portions.

For the **Executive/E-style**:

- the segment extends across the full width of the lens;
- the segment optical centre lies on the dividing line;
- there is no segment-induced image jump at the dividing line;
- the design provides broad distance and near fields;
- the lens can be thick and cosmetically conspicuous.

### Prism thinning

The main deck gives:

\[
P=\frac{yA}{40}
\]

where:

- \(P\) is prism used in thinning;
- \(A\) is the add;
- \(y\) is the distance from dividing line to the top of the finished lens in millimetres.

The source recommends anti-reflection coating for a prism-thinned E-style lens to reduce ghost imaging from internal reflection.

<a id="segment-designs"></a>
# 3. Segment Terminology and Designs

## 3.1 Segment terminology

The sources use the following measurements.

### Segment width or diameter

The greatest horizontal dimension of the segment. Common straight-top widths include 22, 25, 28, and 35 mm. The main deck particularly identifies ST25, ST28, and ST35.

### Segment depth

The vertical distance from the segment top to a tangent at the lowest point of the segment.

### Segment height

The vertical distance from the lowest point of the eyewire or lens shape to the segment top.

### Segment drop

The vertical relationship between the distance reference point/optical centre and the segment top. The main deck also gives:

\[
segment\ drop\ or\ raise=segment\ height-\frac{B}{2}
\]

where \(B\) is the vertical frame dimension.

### Segment inset

The horizontal displacement of the near segment toward the nose to account for convergence.

\[
inset\ per\ lens=\frac{distance\ PD-near\ PD}{2}
\]

Example:

\[
\frac{69-65}{2}=2\text{ mm per lens}
\]

## 3.2 Round/Kryptok segment

The optical centre of a round segment lies at the geometrical centre of the circle. The decks describe common sizes around 22 mm, although larger variants exist.

### Advantages listed

- relatively inconspicuous;
- no external ridge in a fused design;
- simple manufacture;
- low cost;
- useful for some distance-dominant users.

### Limitations listed

- relatively small near field in the common size;
- greater image jump than a flat top of similar practical use;
- chromatic aberration in fused glass;
- “drooping of eyes” is listed in one external-source slide; treat this wording as source terminology rather than a formal diagnosis.

## 3.3 Flat-top or D segment

A **flat-top/D segment** is essentially a round segment with the upper portion cut away. Its segment optical centre is commonly a few millimetres below the segment top.

### Advantages

- smaller image jump than a comparable round segment;
- wide and useful near area;
- built-in geometric inset in common blanks;
- popular for both plus and minus distance prescriptions;
- segment centre can lie close to the assumed NVP.

### Source caution

One slide states that fused flat-top designs have chromatic aberration while one-piece forms do not. The main principle is that chromatic effects depend on the materials and prismatic effect, not simply the outline of the segment.

## 3.4 Curve-top and panoptik segments

These resemble flat tops but have a curved upper line. Panoptik segments have a curved top with rounded corners.

## 3.5 Ribbon/B and R segments

Ribbon segments are round segments with top and bottom portions removed. The deck describes:

- B segment: approximately 9 mm depth;
- R segment: approximately 14 mm depth;
- optical centre around the middle of the segment.

A ribbon segment may preserve distance vision below the near zone for a specialised task.

## 3.6 Crescent/Ultex segment

The **Ultex** or down-curve segment is a large round/crescent form. Its segment optical centre is outside the visible near portion.

This has major prism consequences:

- in a plus distance lens, segment base-down prism may partly neutralise the main lens's base-up prism at near;
- in a minus distance lens, both may act base down, increasing near prism substantially.

The main deck therefore favours flat- or curve-top D segments over large down-curve segments for many myopes.

## 3.7 Executive segment

The segment optical centre is on the dividing line. Therefore segment jump is zero. The source lists:

- wide distance and near areas;
- no image jump;
- distortion-free principal zones;
- poor cosmetic acceptance, top-portion thickness, and weight as disadvantages.

## 3.8 Occupational multifocals

An **occupational multifocal** is selected and positioned for a specialised viewing task. The source illustrates designs such as:

- double-D;
- quadrifocal arrangements;
- near or intermediate segments positioned superiorly for overhead work.

Examples of users include plumbers, pharmacists, librarians, electricians, and mechanics who need near or intermediate vision above eye level.

<a id="nvp-prism"></a>
# 4. Near Visual Point and Prismatic Effect

## 4.1 Near visual point

The **near visual point (NVP)** is the assumed point where the line of sight intersects the lens during near viewing.

The main deck uses a typical position:

- 8 mm below the distance optical centre;
- 2 mm inward from it.

This is a teaching position, not an invariant anatomical measurement.

## 4.2 Prentice's rule

\[
P=cF
\]

where:

- \(P\) = prism in prism dioptres;
- \(c\) = decentration from the relevant optical centre in centimetres;
- \(F\) = power in the meridian of decentration.

Base direction follows lens sign and point of view. For vertical decentration below the optical centre:

- plus lens -> base up;
- minus lens -> base down.

## 4.3 Component method

At the NVP, calculate separately:

1. prism from the main distance lens;
2. prism from the add segment;
3. combine by base direction.

This is safer than using the total near power without considering the two optical centres.

## 4.4 Lecture example: +3.00 D with +2.00 D Ultex, 38 mm segment

The NVP is 8 mm below the distance optical centre.

### Main lens

\[
P=0.8\times3.00=2.4\Delta\ BU
\]

The segment radius is 19 mm. The NVP is 5 mm below segment top, so it lies 14 mm above the segment centre.

### Segment

\[
P=1.4\times2.00=2.8\Delta\ BD
\]

### Combined prism

\[
2.8\Delta\ BD-2.4\Delta\ BU=0.4\Delta\ BD
\]

The segment partly neutralises the main-lens prism.

## 4.5 Same segment with -3.00 D distance power

Main lens at 8 mm below centre:

\[
P=0.8\times3.00=2.4\Delta\ BD
\]

Segment:

\[
2.8\Delta\ BD
\]

Total:

\[
2.4+2.8=5.2\Delta\ BD
\]

Both components act in the same base direction, creating a much larger near prismatic effect.

## 4.6 D segment example

The lecture uses a D segment whose segment optical centre coincides with the NVP. The segment therefore contributes zero prism at that point.

For +3.00 D distance power at 8 mm below the distance centre:

\[
P=0.8\times3.00=2.4\Delta\ BU
\]

The NVP prism is the same as it would be through the main lens alone.

## 4.7 E-style example

The E-style segment optical centre is on the dividing line. If the NVP is 5 mm below it and the add is +2.00 D:

\[
P_{segment}=0.5\times2.00=1.0\Delta\ BU
\]

For a -3.00 D main lens at 8 mm below centre:

\[
P_{main}=2.4\Delta\ BD
\]

Combined:

\[
2.4\Delta\ BD-1.0\Delta\ BU=1.4\Delta\ BD
\]

## 4.8 Vertical differential prism

The patient is most disturbed by the **difference** between the right- and left-eye vertical prism at the NVP. Equal prism in the same direction may be tolerated better than a large imbalance. Segment selection, split bifocals, prism-controlled designs, or slab-off may be used to manage imbalance.

<a id="jump-tca"></a>
# 5. Image Jump and Chromatic Aberration

## 5.1 Image jump

As the eye crosses the dividing line, the segment's prism is introduced abruptly. The apparent displacement is called **image jump**.

\[
Jump=cA
\]

where:

- \(c\) = distance from segment top to segment optical centre in centimetres;
- \(A\) = add power.

Jump depends on the segment, not on the distance prescription.

## 5.2 Source comparisons for +2.00 D add

### 38 mm Ultex

Segment centre is 19 mm from the top:

\[
Jump=1.9\times2.00=3.8\Delta
\]

This is the largest of the lecture examples.

### D segment

Segment centre approximately 5 mm below the top:

\[
Jump=0.5\times2.00=1.0\Delta
\]

### E-style

Segment centre lies on the dividing line:

\[
Jump=0\times2.00=0\Delta
\]

## 5.3 Transverse chromatic aberration

The deck highlights **transverse chromatic aberration (TCA)** in fused bifocal near portions. It is associated with dispersion and prism.

\[
TCA=\frac{P}{V}
\]

where:

- \(P\) = prismatic effect at the point;
- \(V\) = Abbe number.

The lecture gives an average threshold around 0.1 prism dioptre for likely awareness, while noting variation between wearers.

### Worked lecture example

A +5.00 D lens with +2.00 D add has a point 8 mm below the distance centre and 6 mm below the segment reference used in the example.

Main lens:

\[
P=0.8\times5.00=4.0\Delta\ BU
\]

Segment:

\[
P=0.6\times2.00=1.2\Delta\ BU
\]

Total:

\[
5.2\Delta\ BU
\]

For CR-39 with the source Abbe value near 58.9:

\[
TCA=\frac{5.2}{58.9}\approx0.09\Delta
\]

The deck concludes this is unlikely to cause a complaint.

# 6. Bifocal Fitting and Segment Placement

## 6.1 Fitting goal

The segment must provide adequate distance and near fields while the frame is worn in its final adjusted position.

The main deck's general-purpose rule places the segment top approximately tangent to the lower limbus/lower iris edge, often close to the lower lid line in primary gaze. Occupational use can require a different height.

## 6.2 Measurements to control

- segment height;
- segment inset or near centration distance;
- segment width;
- frame B dimension and geometric centre;
- distance reference point;
- right and left asymmetry.

## 6.3 Measurement routine synthesised from the decks

1. Select the final frame.
2. Adjust bridge, pads, temple position, vertex distance, tilt, and level.
3. Seat the patient naturally with the fitter at the same eye level.
4. Ask the patient to look at the fitter's corresponding eye.
5. Mark the proposed segment top at the lower limbus/lid reference for each eye.
6. Remove the frame without disturbing the fit.
7. Draw or verify a horizontal reference line.
8. Measure from the lowest point of the eyewire/lens shape to the marked segment top.
9. Repeat for the other eye; do not assume symmetry.
10. Replace the frame and verify the markings on the patient.

One deck specifically describes two methods:

- lower-limbus method;
- lower-lid-margin method.

## 6.4 Segment inset

For a near PD of 65 mm and distance PD of 69 mm:

\[
inset=\frac{69-65}{2}=2\text{ mm per lens}
\]

Inset aims to:

- place the near line of sight through the intended near area;
- make the right and left near fields overlap appropriately.

## 6.5 Occupational placement

The main deck illustrates three broad patterns:

- constant/general use;
- mainly near use;
- occasional near use.

A higher segment improves access to near but can obstruct distance. A lower segment preserves distance but may force excessive downgaze or make near access difficult. Placement must follow task history, posture, and frame geometry.

<a id="trifocals"></a>
# 7. Trifocals

## 7.1 Definition

A **trifocal** has three viewing zones:

- distance portion (DP);
- intermediate portion (IP);
- near/reading portion (NP/RP).

The intermediate add is often expressed as a percentage of the near add:

\[
IP/NP\ ratio=\frac{IP\ Add}{NP\ Add}\times100\%
\]

## 7.2 Examples

For near add +2.50 D:

- IP +1.00 D -> 40%;
- IP +1.25 D -> 50%;
- IP +1.50 D -> 60% by direct arithmetic.

> **Course-source discrepancy:** One slide labels +1.50/+2.50 as 80%. The displayed formula gives 60%. This package does not silently rewrite the slide; questions should test the formula and identify the slide value as a source discrepancy requiring review.

## 7.3 Choosing the ratio

The main deck states:

- about 35-45% when the intermediate zone is intended to extend clear distance vision closer toward arm's length;
- about 60-70% when it is intended to extend near vision outward toward arm's length;
- 50% is a common default when no ratio is specified.

## 7.4 Final mastery checklist

You should be able to:

- define presbyopia and reading addition;
- calculate near power from distance power and add;
- compare split, cemented, fused, and solid bifocals;
- locate the segment optical centre for round, D, Ultex, and Executive designs;
- define segment width, depth, height, drop, and inset;
- calculate NVP prism by separating main-lens and segment components;
- calculate image jump;
- explain why Ultex behaves differently for plus and minus wearers;
- estimate TCA from prism and Abbe number;
- take and verify bifocal fitting measurements;
- calculate an intermediate-to-near add ratio and recognise the source arithmetic discrepancy.
